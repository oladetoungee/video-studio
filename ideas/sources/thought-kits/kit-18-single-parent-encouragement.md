# KIT 18 — They're Cold to You Because They Trust You Not to Leave

**Premise:** The idea that the present parent "teaches" the child to hate the absent one is backwards. Kids idealize the absent parent (a highlight reel) and offload their anger onto the present one (the safe target). It's not poisoning — it's that they haven't grown eyes yet. A letter of comfort to the solo parent: give them time.

**Register:** This is a LETTER, not an essay. Warm, directly addressed, repetitive, reassuring. Keep her "I promise you," "don't be weary," the second-person address, the circular reassuring rhythm. Research is one steadying line, not a section.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

When people say things like, oh, the parent that was present (usually the mother) taught the children to hate the parent that was absent (usually the father) — I always laugh, because if you've experienced this life you'll know it doesn't work like that. First of all, it operates on the assumption that children raised in single-parent households are just stupid people programmed by the parent. They're normal children like everyone else. They don't listen to their parents, they're stubborn and disobedient like you are or were.

And to blow your mind: children raised in single-parent households tend to glamorize the absent parent. The parent that ships in twice a year, comes with a few gifts once a year or once in two years — that's the co-parent they build a fantasy with in their head. The parent staying day and night with them is not the cool parent. They don't like that person. That's literally how it works. I'm not joking.

But it takes them some years — they start to see things more clearly, they grow, they stop seeing things like children. And this is the point where people claim they were "taught" to hate. It doesn't work like that.

This is an encouragement for people raising children alone. They won't like you. They'll always like the other person, and it'll blow your mind — how could they be so ungrateful? It's normal. Just give them a few years. They don't see your struggle now. They don't see your effort. They don't see that you're carrying two people's work alone. But they will. First they'll notice their own struggle, then the extra thing they don't have because of the absence, then the extra effort they have to apply. And little by little they'll connect the dots by themselves. They'll come to appreciate you. Don't be weary, don't be sad, don't be frustrated. What they need is time. They'll start to see as adults see. They'll see your struggle and come to appreciate you. I promise you.

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**This is comfort, not argument.** Addressed to a specific tired person — the solo parent. Everything serves them, not a debate. Protect the encouragement; bring research only where it steadies her hand, never where it turns warmth into a lecture.

**Her central claim is real and has a name.** The "present parent poisons the child" accusation is *parental alienation* — the thing absent parents reach for most. What she describes is close to the opposite, and it's documented: kids often idealize the absent parent. Giving the parent the *reason* strengthens the comfort — it stops being "trust me" and becomes "here's the machinery, so stop taking it personally."

**The machinery, plainly.** The present parent is the one who says no (bedtime, homework, can't afford it). Parenting is a long line of small no's for the child's good. The absent parent never says no — shows up twice a year with gifts and yeses and lightness. So the child compares a whole real person to a *highlight reel* (same trap as several other kits), and the real one loses every time — precisely because they do the work. Gifts are cheap; your no's are expensive, and they cost you affection in the short term.

**The deeper layer (the part that lands).** A child can't easily hold "the parent who left didn't choose me" — too heavy. So the mind builds a fantasy absent parent (good reasons, loves from afar) as self-protection. That fantasy gets projected against the present parent. The child can be angry at you *safely* because you won't leave; they can't risk anger at the one who already proved they might. So you absorb anger that belongs to the other person — because you're the safe one. **Your reliability is what makes you the safe target. They're cold to you because they trust you not to leave.**

**Her timeline instinct is exactly right — state it as a mechanism.** The later "waking up" isn't the poisoning finally working — it's that *they grew eyes*. The eyes come when the child carries weight themselves: when they have to be the "no," run out of money, do one person's job alone and feel their own back ache. Appreciation doesn't come from a lecture; it comes from finally standing where the parent stood. You can't explain struggle to someone who's never carried weight — you wait for life to hand them their own.

**Honesty guard (the one place comfort could tip into something unfair).** Not every absent parent is a villain, not every present one a saint — some are kept away, some leave for reasons a child later understands. And "they'll appreciate you" is a beautiful promise but not a guaranteed contract; some take longer, a few never quite come around. So aim the ending slightly differently: don't pour yourself out to collect a debt later — do it because it's who you are; let the seeing, when it comes, be a gift, not a wage. This keeps the comfort *true* and lands softer, releasing the parent from waiting on a receipt.

**Faith (fits here better than almost anywhere).** Galatians 6:9 — "do not grow weary in doing good, for in due season you will reap if you do not give up." *Due season* = her whole timeline in six words. Deeper turn (optional, used lightly): the steady, present, unglamorous provision we ignore while chasing the exciting thing, and only recognize looking back — close to how God's love is described. The solo parent does the least-thanked, most God-like work there is.

**Decisions:** faith IN; honesty guard IN; retain her tone above all — add material but keep her voice, repetition, and direct address.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

When people say things like, oh, the parent who was present, usually the mother, taught the children to hate the parent who was absent, usually the father, I always laugh. I laugh, because if you have actually lived this life, you know it does not work like that. It doesn't work like that at all.

First of all, that whole idea assumes that children raised by one parent are just stupid little robots who get programmed by whoever is around. That is not a real child. Real children don't even listen to their parents. They are stubborn and disobedient, exactly the way you were, exactly the way I was. You think this same child who won't do their homework is somewhere absorbing a careful campaign of hatred? Be serious.

And let me actually blow your mind, because it's the opposite of what people think. Children raised by one parent tend to glamorize the parent who is not there. The one who shows up twice a year. The one who comes with a few gifts once a year, once in two years, and then disappears again. That is the parent they build a fantasy around in their little heads. That one is the cool parent.

And the parent who is there, day and night, morning and night, the one actually holding the whole thing together? That's not the cool parent. They don't like that one as much. I'm not joking. That is literally how it works.

And there's a reason it works like that, once you think about it. The parent who is present is the one who has to say no. No, you can't have that. No, it's bedtime. No, we can't afford it right now. Go and do your homework. Every single day, you are the no. Because raising a person is basically just a long line of small no's, for their own good. But the parent who is absent never has to say no. They don't do bedtime. They don't do discipline. They don't do Tuesday. They just show up with yes and gifts and lightness and then they leave.

So the child is not comparing a good parent to a bad parent. They're comparing a whole real human being to a highlight reel. And the real one loses that comparison every time, precisely because they're the one doing the actual work. The gifts are cheap. Your no's are expensive. And they cost you the child's affection, for now.

And it goes even deeper than gifts, and this is the part I really want you to hear. A child cannot easily carry the thought that the parent who left didn't choose them. It's too heavy for a small person. So their mind protects them, and it builds a beautiful fantasy version of the absent parent instead. One who must have had good reasons. One who loves them from far away. And guess who that fantasy gets projected against? You. The real one. The one standing right there.

The child can be angry at you safely, because you are not going anywhere. They can't risk being angry at the one who already showed they might leave. So you get the anger that actually belongs to the other person, simply because you're the safe one to give it to. Your reliability, the very thing that makes you the good parent, is the exact thing that makes you the safe place to dump it. Think about that. They are cold to you because they trust you not to leave.

Now here's where people get confused. Because it's true that one day the child wakes up. They start to see things more clearly. They stop seeing like children and they start seeing like adults. And this is the exact point where people jump in and say, ah, you see, they finally got taught, the poisoning finally worked. No. It's not that they were taught. It's that they grew eyes.

And I'll tell you exactly when the eyes come. It's not from a lecture. You cannot sit a child down and explain your struggle to them, it won't enter, because they've never carried any weight. The eyes come when they carry weight themselves. When they become the one who has to say no to somebody. When they run out of money and finally understand what stretching it actually looks like. When they do one person's job alone and feel their own back ache and think, wait. Wait. She did this with two people's work on her shoulders. For years. And I never once saw it.

That's when it lands. Not because somebody told them. Because they finally stood where you're standing right now.

So this is my encouragement, and it's for every person raising a child alone. They won't like you. They will like the other person, the one who's barely there, and it will blow your mind. You'll think, how can they be this ungrateful? After everything? It's normal. I promise you it's normal. Just give them a few years.

They don't see your struggle right now. They don't. They don't see your effort. They don't see that you are carrying two people's work and doing it by yourself. They can't see it yet, because they've never had to. But they will. Just give them time. First they'll start to notice their own struggle. Then they'll start to notice the extra thing they didn't have because that person wasn't there. Then the extra effort they had to put in because of that absence. And little by little, all by themselves, with nobody teaching them, they will connect the dots. And they will come to appreciate you.

So don't be weary. Don't be sad. Don't be frustrated. What they need is not for you to defend yourself. What they need is time. They will start to see the way adults see, and they will see your struggle, and they will come and appreciate you. I promise you they will.

But let me tell you one last thing, and I say this so the promise doesn't sit wrong on you. Don't do it for that day. Don't pour yourself out just so that one day they'll finally see it and pay you back with gratitude. Because if you're doing it to collect a debt later, the waiting will break you, and honestly, a few of them take longer than others, and life is more tangled than a simple story of one good parent and one bad one. So do it because it's who you are. Do it because it's what they needed and you were there to give it. And when the seeing comes, and it usually does, let it be a gift, not a payment you were owed. That way you're free either way.

Because the Bible says do not grow weary in doing good, for in due season you will reap, if you do not give up. Due season. Not today. Not on the child's schedule. In due season. So keep going. You are doing the most thankless, most invisible, most God-like work there is, loving somebody who does not yet understand what it's costing you.

They will get there. Just give them a little more time. I promise you.

## REFERENCES
- **Idealization of the absent parent** — a well-recognised pattern in developmental and clinical psychology; children frequently idealize an absent/non-custodial parent and direct frustration at the custodial one. Used as a general, accurate observation, not tied to one contestable study.
- **Parental alienation** — the term for one parent deliberately turning a child against the other; named here only to distinguish it from what she's describing (which is closer to the reverse). No strong empirical claim attached.
- **Galatians 6:9** — "Let us not grow weary in doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up." Quoted accurately, paraphrased in delivery.
