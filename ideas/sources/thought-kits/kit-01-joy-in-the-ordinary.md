# KIT 01 — Joy in the Ordinary Days

**Premise:** We've reserved happiness for the highlights and written off the ordinary days as filler. But the filler is the life.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

People are not happy. We majority happiness is declining. People generally are not happy. One of the things — one of the podcasts that I love the most is Arthur Brooks. I really love his podcast. I love to watch it. He talks about all of those things, but in relation to like life quality, work, love, friendship, faith, to the quality of our life in terms of happiness.

I feel like happiness has become so limited to the highlights of our life. Like of course, you graduate from your PhD or your master's program, you are happy. That's a big highlight thing that happened. You got married, you have a baby, you bought a house, you bought a car, you bought something that you wanted to buy — those are highlights. And I think that happiness has become something that we sustain for those highlight moments. But there is joy in the ordinary days as well, in the normal days where nothing out of the ordinary happens. The normal go to work, come back from work, take your bath, then normal days. There is joy in the normal days.

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The name for the instinct.** This is the *arrival fallacy* — coined by Tal Ben-Shahar in his book *Happier* — the belief that happiness lives at the destination and that once you arrive, the feeling will finally settle and stay. Brooks talks about this a lot. The mechanism underneath it is *hedonic adaptation*: even the big highlights fade far faster than we expect, and people return to baseline within months of the wedding, the promotion, the car.

**Do the math (this is the sharpest half).** In a good year, how many highlight days do you actually get? Five? Ten? If happiness is only permitted on those days, you've quietly written off ~355 days as filler. That's not a happiness strategy, it's a rounding error. The filler is the life.

**The highlight-reel problem.** Social media didn't invent milestone-happiness but it industrialised it. We only post arrivals, so we calibrate our sense of a "happy life" against everyone else's arrivals. The ordinary Tuesday has no content value, so it starts to feel like it has no life value either.

**Testimony culture (the faith angle, close to home).** Even church can accidentally do this. The mic goes to the visa, the job, the baby, the healing — all real, all worth celebrating. But if we only recognise God moving at the breakthrough, we train our ears to sleep through the daily-bread part: waking up, food, breath, the ordinary mercy itself.

**Ordinary Time.** The theologians literally named most of the church calendar "Ordinary Time" on purpose. Most of life is not Christmas or Easter — it's the long green stretch in between, and the invitation was always to find God there too.

**Enjoyment vs pleasure (Brooks).** Pleasure is the spike; enjoyment is pleasure plus people plus memory. An ordinary evening cooking while on a call with someone you love holds enjoyment. It'll never make a highlight reel, but it's load-bearing for an actual happy life.

**The diagnostic question the essay lives on:** When we say there's joy in the normal days — is the problem that we don't *notice* it (fixed by attention/savouring), or that we've *defined* happiness so it's not allowed to count (needs the definition torn up)? Different diagnoses. The essay lands on the second.

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

People are not happy. Like, generally. Majority happiness is declining, and you can feel it even without looking at any data — you can feel it in conversations, in how tired everybody sounds.

One of the podcasts I love the most is Arthur Brooks'. I really love watching him. He talks about happiness in relation to everything — work, love, friendship, faith — the actual quality of our lives. And listening to him got me thinking about something that's been sitting in my chest for a while.

Somewhere along the line, happiness became something we reserve for the highlights.

You graduate from your master's program, happy. You get married, you have a baby, you buy a house, you buy the car, you finally buy that thing you've been saving for, happy. And those are real. I'm not dismissing them. But if I asked you to count them, how many of those days do you actually get in a year? Three? Five? In a really good year, maybe ten. So if happiness only lives on those days, what have you done to the other 355? You've filed them away as filler. Waiting rooms. Days you just get through on your way to the next event.

That's most of your life, by the way. The filler is the life.

And the painful part is, the highlights don't even keep their promise. There's a name for this — psychologists call it the arrival fallacy. Tal Ben-Shahar came up with it, and Brooks talks about it all the time. It's the illusion that once you arrive — once you get the degree, the visa, the title, the ring — the happiness will land and stay. It lands, yes. Then it leaves. There's a famous study from 1978 that looked at lottery winners, and these people, who got the biggest highlight most of us could imagine, were not happier than their neighbours months later. Worse, they enjoyed ordinary things less. The mundane stuff — breakfast, a chat, a walk — it all got dimmer next to the memory of the jackpot.

Sit with that for a second. The highlight didn't just fade. It taxed the ordinary days on its way out.

I think that's what we've done to ourselves culturally. We've built a whole way of living, and honestly a whole internet, around arrivals. Nobody posts Tuesday. Nobody posts "I went to work, work was fine, I came back, I took my bath, I made dinner, the dinner was actually really good, I slept well." There's no content in that. So slowly we started believing there's no life in that either.

But the ordinary Tuesday is where you actually live. The wedding is one day; the marriage is ten thousand ordinary evenings. The graduation is one afternoon; the degree was two years of normal mornings. Even faith, and I say this as somebody in church, we can do this with testimonies. The mic goes to the visa, the job, the healing, the breakthrough. And those deserve the mic! But if the only time we recognise God moving is at the breakthrough, we've trained ourselves to sleep through the daily bread part. The waking up itself. The ordinary mercy of a normal day where nothing went wrong.

So I've been asking myself a different question. Instead of "when is my next highlight coming," I've started asking, "what was good about today, specifically, this normal day where nothing happened?" And the answer is never nothing. The food was good. The evening light in my kitchen was doing something. Somebody made me laugh. I understood something I didn't understand yesterday. Small things, but they were real, and they were today, and today is the only place I actually ever am.

I'm not saying stop chasing the big things. Chase them. But maybe stop outsourcing your entire happiness to them, because they're rare, they fade fast, and if you're not careful they'll make your regular days taste like nothing. Joy in the ordinary isn't a consolation prize for people whose lives are boring. It might actually be the whole skill. The highlights will come when they come. The Tuesdays are already here.

## REFERENCES
- **Arrival fallacy** — Tal Ben-Shahar, *Happier* (2007). The belief that attaining a goal brings lasting happiness.
- **Lottery study** — Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978), "Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 36(8), 917–927. Winners were not happier than controls and took less pleasure in mundane events. *Caveat:* small sample (22 winners); later larger studies complicate the money-happiness link, so keep the claim to what the study found.
- **"Happiness is declining"** — true for some groups (esp. young people in Western countries per recent World Happiness Report data), not uniformly true globally. Keep the "it feels like" framing.
