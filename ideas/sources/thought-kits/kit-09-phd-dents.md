# KIT 09 — A Dent in the Edge of Everything We Know

**Premise:** I rate people who do PhDs — not for intelligence, for the commitment of time and mental resources to one specific fragment of knowledge. And underneath it, my own quiet realisation: I wanted the title more than I wanted the question. Those are two different desires.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

Before my master's, before I started my master's program, I always wanted to do a PhD, even without my master's, but that didn't work out — and I'm so glad it didn't, because I don't think I would have loved what I wanted to do a PhD in. But anyways, that aside: I absolutely rate people that do PhDs. I think it's very admirable to commit that amount of time and mental resources to just pursuing a specific fragment of knowledge in a domain.

*(Follow-up: keep the almost-committed-to fragment vague — don't name the field.)*

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The image that draws exactly what she's saying.** Matt Might's *The Illustrated Guide to a Ph.D.* (2012) — twelve pictures. A circle containing all human knowledge. Elementary school, you know a little. High school, a bit more. Bachelor's, a specialty. Master's deepens it. Reading research papers takes you to the edge of human knowledge. At the boundary you focus, push for a few years, until the boundary gives way — and **that dent you've made is called a Ph.D.** Her "specific fragment of knowledge in a domain," drawn.

**The detail that proves why dents matter (and it's real).** Might's own son had an undiagnosed disorder; via exome sequencing he turned out to be the first documented case of a new disease (N-glycanase deficiency). Might wrote: *one small dent in the circle of knowledge; one giant leap for my son.* Somebody's obscure, who-cares fragment is the thing that names a child's illness.

**What she's actually admiring — and she said it precisely.** Not intelligence. *Time and mental resources.* That's the better instinct: a PhD is mostly not an intelligence test, it's a tolerance test. Tolerance for not knowing, for years where nothing works, for being the only person on earth who cares about your question. Brilliant people fail it for want of stomach, not brain. Nobody claps in year three.

**Praise without cost is cheap — the honest numbers.** Levecque et al. (2017, Belgium, N=3,659): one in two PhD students experiences psychological distress; ~32% at risk of a common psychiatric disorder, significantly higher than highly educated employees, the highly educated general population, and other higher-ed students. Nature's 2019 global survey (~6,300 candidates): 36% had sought help for anxiety or depression caused by their PhD. Financial payoff often never arrives either.

**The commons argument.** Modern life sits on thousands of dents made by people whose names we'll never learn, working on questions that sounded pointless at the time.

**The second essay hiding in her first paragraph — possibly the better one.** She wanted a PhD, it didn't work out, she's *glad*. That's the difference between **wanting the title and wanting the question.** Wanting to be a person with a doctorate is legitimate and common; only later did she realise she didn't want *that specific fragment* enough. A PhD is one of the rare commitments where 80% interested isn't enough — 80% carries you through a master's, not year four of something nobody applauds. Her relief is evidence *for* the admiration: she measured the fire required, checked her own honestly, and admitted it.

**Caveat that protects the piece.** Admiration must not slide into credential worship. Dents get made outside universities too — practitioners, builders, unfunded obsessives. The thing worth admiring is the years-long push against a boundary, not the letters.

**Structure decided:** personal turn → the dent picture → what it costs → the commons → title vs question → land on "most of us never even find the question."

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

Before my master's, even before I started my master's program, I always wanted to do a PhD. That was the plan in my head. It didn't work out, and honestly? I'm so glad it didn't. Because looking back now, I don't think I would have loved the thing I wanted to do it in. I wanted it, but I don't think I wanted it enough, in that specific area, for that specific number of years.

But that aside, let me say what I actually came to say. I rate people who do PhDs. I rate them so much. I think it is genuinely admirable to commit that amount of time and that amount of mental resources to pursuing one specific fragment of knowledge in a domain.

And if you've never thought about what that actually means, there's this thing called The Illustrated Guide to a PhD, by a professor named Matt Might, and it's just twelve simple pictures. Imagine a circle that contains all of human knowledge. By the time you finish primary school, you know a little bit of it. Secondary school, small more. Bachelor's degree, now you have a specialty. Master's degree, that specialty gets deeper. Then you start reading research papers and they carry you all the way to the edge of the circle, the actual boundary of everything humans currently know. And once you're standing at that boundary, you pick a spot, and you push. For years. Until one day the boundary gives way. And that little dent you made in the edge of human knowledge, that dent is your PhD.

A dent. Years of your life for a dent.

And I think when you understand it as a dent, you understand why it's not really an intelligence competition. It's not about being the smartest person in the room. It's about tolerance. Tolerance for not knowing. Tolerance for years where the thing simply does not work. Tolerance for being the only human being on this earth who cares about your particular question. Plenty of brilliant people cannot do it, and it's not because their brain is small, it's because that specific type of long, quiet, unwitnessed waiting is not for everybody. Nobody is clapping in year three. There's no applause in the middle of it. You're just there, pushing on a boundary in a room by yourself.

And it costs people something real, so let me not romanticise it. There was a big study out of Belgium, thousands of PhD students, and it found that one in two of them experiences psychological distress, and about one in three is at risk of a common psychiatric disorder. That's significantly higher than other highly educated people and other students. A Nature survey of over six thousand doctoral candidates across the world found more than a third had sought help for anxiety or depression connected to their PhD. And for a lot of them the money never even makes sense at the end. So when I say I admire the commitment of mental resources, I mean it more literally than I first realised.

But here's the thing about those dents. Everything you're currently enjoying is standing on top of thousands of them. The medicine, the phone, the vaccine, the thing that diagnosed somebody's condition, all of it is sitting on top of small dents made by people whose names you will never know, who spent years on questions that sounded pointless at the time.

And the man who made that illustrated guide, Matt Might, actually lived this. His son had a disorder nobody could diagnose. And eventually, because of advances in genetic sequencing, they discovered his son was the very first documented case of a new disease. He wrote about it and said, one small dent in the circle of knowledge, one giant leap for my son. Somebody's obscure, unglamorous, who-cares-about-this fragment turned out to be the thing that finally named a child's illness. That's it. That's why the dents matter, even when they look pointless from outside.

Now, I want to be fair, because I'm not here to worship credentials. Dents get made outside the university too. There are practitioners and builders and obsessive people with no funding and no letters after their name who are pushing on boundaries just as hard. The thing I'm admiring is not the title. It's the years of pushing.

Which brings me back to me. Because I've realised the reason I didn't do it is not really that the door closed. It's that I wanted the title more than I wanted the question. And those are two completely different desires, and it took me a while to be honest about which one I actually had. Wanting to be a person with a doctorate is a very normal, very human thing to want. But a PhD is one of the few commitments where being eighty percent interested is simply not enough. Eighty percent can carry you through a master's. Eighty percent will not carry you through year four of something nobody is applauding.

So my relief is not me dodging the work. My relief is me knowing the size of fire that thing requires, checking my own fire honestly, and admitting it wasn't burning that hot for that particular fragment.

And that's exactly why I rate the people who do it. They found the one question they were willing to spend years pushing on. Most of us never even find the question. They found it, and then they went and put a dent in the edge of everything we know.
