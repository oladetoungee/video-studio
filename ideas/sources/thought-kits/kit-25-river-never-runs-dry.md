# KIT 25 — A River That Never Runs Dry

**Premise:** For the African Christian who travels out: resist the temptation to rank your home faith against the Western one. The gospel isn't tied to any culture — the Christ of Peter is the Christ of Paul. He meets each child at the point of their unique need from an endless supply. The "our abroad church did XYZ, Nigerian churches could never" person is the older brother, standing outside the party keeping score.

**Register:** soft, pastoral, a little worshipful, building to the river image (like the one-size-fits-all kit, not argumentative). Faith-centered throughout by nature.

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

If you've been a Christian in Nigeria or anywhere in Africa and you travel out of the country, it becomes really tempting to find loopholes in the way we practice the faith back home in comparison to the western way. Do the best you can to resist this temptation.

One of the things that I am now finding thrilling about the Christian faith is how its practices are not tied to particular cultures or languages or expressions. Our faith doesn't come in to rip off us our unique expressions and situations. The Christ of Peter is still the Christ of Paul. The Christ of the Jews is still the Christ of the gentiles. Our needs of him are unique, but he's multibreasted like that — he knows how to satisfy his children in their unique ways. He doesn't class us like that. He answers his child with economic needs in Nigeria the same way he does his child with emotional needs in America. He's the father of the prodigal son and also the father of the son who stayed. There's a father that meets us all at the point of our need.

We don't have to think less of ourselves in that way. When your church abroad says or does something, you don't have to point out that your church in Nigeria doesn't. "Our abroad church did xyz, Nigerian churches can never." Don't be that person.

There's grace available for us all. He's a river that never runs dry.

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**Her instinct has a real name: contextualization.** The gospel is trans-cultural — it belongs to no single culture and can be truly expressed inside every one without being owned by any. The faith has no home country. Her line "the Christ of Peter is still the Christ of Paul, the Christ of the Jews is still the Christ of the gentiles" is the New Testament wrestling with exactly this — Acts 15, the Jerusalem council, the fight over whether gentiles had to become culturally Jewish to follow Christ. The answer was no. The gospel didn't come to make everyone Jewish (or later, European). Her thrill is theologically solid and baked into the origin story.

**Her best original image — "multibreasted" — has deep roots: El Shaddai.** One traditional reading of El Shaddai is "the many-breasted one" — God who nourishes, who is more than enough, who supplies. A maternal image of provision inside a faith usually pictured as fatherly. Keep the word; rest it lightly on El Shaddai so it doesn't seem to come from nowhere. The richness: he meets the economic need in Nigeria and the emotional need in America from the same infinite supply.

**The prodigal son line is the sharpest theological move — make it a peak.** Luke 15 is about TWO lost sons: the wild one who ran, and the resentful one who stayed home doing everything right and still missed the father's heart. The father goes out to BOTH — runs to the returner, and leaves the party to plead with the bitter one outside. The son who stayed spends the ending COMPARING ("I did everything right and you never threw me a party; this son of yours wasted everything and gets the feast"). Comparison is the sickness in the story. Her "our abroad church did XYZ, Nigerian churches could never" person IS the older brother — keeping score, standing outside the joy. Make the connection explicit.

**The pastoral warning, framed precisely (not just "don't judge").** The specific spiritual trap of the migrant: using the encounter with a different expression of faith to feel superior about the new and ashamed of the old. It LOOKS like growth/enlightenment, but it's often the same old comparison instinct in a new outfit. The tell that it's not real growth: direction. Real growth makes you more generous toward where you came from, not more embarrassed by it. Contempt for your origin is almost never maturity — usually insecurity that found a respectable target.

**Honesty guard (so it isn't "everything everywhere is fine").** Not every practice is beyond critique — control, manipulation, things that hurt people exist in churches everywhere, home and abroad, and naming a genuine wrong is NOT the sin she's describing. The trap is the reflexive cultural ranking: treating home worship as embarrassing simply because it's less polished than the Western version; mistaking cultural preference for spiritual superiority. Critiquing a real wrong = fine. Despising your own people's sincere worship because it now looks unsophisticated = the trap.

**The landing — her river image resolves it via scarcity vs abundance.** Comparison assumes scarcity (if their church got the good thing, mine is lesser; if grace showed up there, there's less here). The river demolishes that: grace for the church abroad AND at home, for the economic need AND the emotional one, from a source that never runs down. You were never choosing whose expression was better, because he was never rationing himself. The peace is discovering you were never in a competition.

**Opening framing.** Keep her direct address ("if you traveled out, resist this temptation") but let warmth arrive fast — a fellow-traveler warning, not a wagging finger. "I feel this pull too; here's what I found on the other side."

**Decisions:** faith-centered by nature; soft/pastoral/worshipful, building to the river; confessional; El Shaddai and the two-sons parable made explicit; honesty guard kept.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

And then you travel out of the country, and it becomes really tempting to find loopholes. It can be really tempting to find loopholes in the way we practice the faith back home, in comparison to the Western way. And if you let yourself go, you might start to rank home versus abroad in terms of faith.

Our abroad church did XYZ. Nigerian churches can never. Please, don't be that person. Do your best to resist that temptation.

One of the thrilling ideas of the Christian faith is that it is not tied to any one culture, or any one language, or any one expression. The gospel of Christ doesn't strip us naked and give us one standard cultural costume to wear. We could be different people, with different languages.

And it will still be the same Jesus Christ. The Christ of the Jews is still the Christ of the gentiles. And not surprisingly, our needs of him are unique. But he's enough for all of them.

He's many-breasted in that way. He knows how to feed each of his children in the exact way that that child needs to be nourished. He doesn't class us. He doesn't rank us.

He answers his child with an economic need in Nigeria the same way he answers his child with an emotional need in America. He answers his child crying out for safety in a war-torn country the same way he answers his child fighting quiet loneliness in a big, busy city. He answers his child praying for a child of her own the same way he answers his child praying for the strength to forgive. He answers the one asking for their daily bread the same way he answers the one asking for meaning in a life that already has everything. He answers his child struggling to belong in a new country the same way he answers his child who has never left the village they were born in. Nobody's need is less spiritual, or more spiritual. No one need is more sophisticated than the other.

He's the father that steps out and runs to meet the prodigal son, but also the same father that steps out to meet the son who stayed. He's the father of the prodigal son, and also the father of the son who stayed.

Of course, this is not to say that every single thing the church does is beyond question. There are real things that are worth questioning. Control. Manipulation. Hurt. And those things exist everywhere, not just at home. So stop looking at the foundation of your faith as less. Don't let your newly found faith be an avenue for you to look down on the people who first taught you how to pray, even if you now have better, or more profound, ways of doing things.

There's grace for us all. His grace is a river that never runs dry. There is enough of him for all of us to drink, in the way that we each need to drink.

## REFERENCES
- **Acts 15 (Jerusalem Council)** — the early church's ruling that gentile believers need not adopt Jewish cultural/ceremonial law to follow Christ; the scriptural root of the gospel being trans-cultural. Represented accurately.
- **"Christ of Peter / Christ of Paul; Jew / gentile"** — reflects Galatians 3:28 ("neither Jew nor Greek… you are all one in Christ Jesus") and 1 Corinthians 12 (one Spirit, many members). Accurate.
- **El Shaddai / "many-breasted one"** — one traditional (contested) etymology derives Shaddai from *shad* (breast), reading it as the God who nourishes / is all-sufficient; the mainstream translation is "God Almighty." Presented in the script as "one of the ways people read it," which is the honest framing — it's a devotional reading, not settled scholarship.
- **Prodigal son / two sons** — Luke 15:11–32. The elder brother's resentment and the father going out to plead with him (v28) are in the text. Accurate; the "two lost sons" reading is standard.
- **River that never runs dry** — resonates with Psalm 46:4, John 7:38 ("rivers of living water"), Ezekiel 47 (the river from the temple). Her own image; scripture echoes noted for depth, not quoted.
