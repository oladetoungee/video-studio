# KIT 12 — 150 Seats, Millions in the Room

**Premise:** In 1991 the World Wide Web went public and access multiplied by billions. Our capacity multiplied by zero. Not a morality problem — a capacity problem. We're the first humans who have to manufacture our own limits, because geography used to do it for us.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

In the year something something, something very interesting happened to humanity. The internet happened, and so many things changed. Before the internet we were limited in several ways. The people you knew were limited. The knowledge you had access to was limited. Your circle of influence was significantly limited. But then the internet happened, and all of a sudden we have billions of people that we can access through our phone. I can open my phone and access billions and billions of people. I can access multiples of knowledge. And as much as it sounds — and I think it is — a good thing. Imagine, look at how much change has happened to the world since then. It was something so evolutionary.

But on the flip side, I think that the internet did so well that we now know more than we should know. We know more people than we should know. We have more access to information than we should have. We have…

*(trailed off)*

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The blank she left.** Candidate dates each tell a different story: 1969 (ARPANET, first message between two computers — the engineer's answer), 1983 (protocol switch-on), **1991 (World Wide Web goes public)**. 1991 is the right one for this essay: it's when the internet stopped being infrastructure for institutions and became something an ordinary person could open. The essay isn't about cables — it's about the day the door opened for everybody. **Her call: use 1991.**

**Defending the word "should."** Not a moral should — she's not asking for censorship or nostalgia. It's a capacity claim: a person should not drink forty litres of water. Not because water is bad or forty litres is sinful, but because the system that processes it was never built for that volume. Framed that way, the argument is very hard to attack.

**The number that is the spine — Dunbar.** Robin Dunbar found that across primates, the social group size a species can hold tracks neocortex size. Run the human numbers: **about 150.** Real relationship = you know them, know your history with them, know how they connect to everyone else. Layers: ~5 innermost, ~15 close friends, ~50, ~150, ~500 acquaintances, ~1,500 recognisable faces. The 150 recurs across hunter-gatherer clans, Neolithic villages, Roman army units, modern military companies, communities that split once they exceed it. Different centuries, different continents, same ceiling — that's hardware, not culture.

**Dunbar's own line on social media, which the essay hangs on:** when people have thousands of followers, those aren't new friends — they're the outer layers (500 and 1,500) filling up. **The technology didn't expand capacity. It expanded exposure. Your brain still has 150 seats and now there are millions of people standing in the room.**

**What happens to everyone without a seat — parasocial.** They don't get filtered out; the brain has no "this person is content, not a human" setting. Horton & Wohl named it in **1956**, watching people with televisions: parasocial interaction, the illusion of a face-to-face relationship with someone who doesn't know you exist. The internet industrialised it. So finite relational capacity gets spent on strangers who can't reciprocate — visible in how people grieve when a creator quits, or feel betrayed when someone they've never met changes their opinion. Not stupidity: a 150-seat brain doing what it was built for, in an environment it wasn't.

**The heavier half — the awareness/agency gap.** For almost all of history, what you could know about suffering was roughly the size of what you could do about it. The child was sick down the road; you could carry food down that road. Awareness and agency were the same circle. The internet snapped them apart. Now you know about a war, a famine, a collapsed building on four continents before breakfast and can act on none of it. Moral concern scaled to the planet; moral capability still the size of a street. **The exhaustion isn't compassion failure, it's arithmetic.**

**The daily version — comparison.** You used to be the best singer in your area, genuinely, because your area was the whole measurable world. Excellence was local, therefore achievable, therefore felt. Now every domain has a visible global ceiling that loads in a second. Nobody in history has had to hold ordinary competence against the top 0.001% of the species daily. Self-esteem built for a village comparison set, now benchmarked against eight billion.

**The frame — E.O. Wilson:** "The real problem of humanity is the following: we have paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, and god-like technology." The mismatch isn't good vs bad tech; it's the speed of the tool vs the speed of the animal holding it.

**The analogy that makes it land — sugar.** We crave sweetness because sugar was rare and meant survival; the appetite was correctly calibrated for scarcity. Then we built abundance and the same appetite started killing us — and nothing about the appetite was ever wrong. Our hunger to know things about other people is the same instinct: in a village, knowing everything about everyone was survival information. Now supply is infinite and nobody told the appetite.

**Fairness, and it must be substantive, not a token disclaimer.** The people who gained most from the internet are those furthest from centres of power. Access used to be gatekept by geography and birth — a brilliant person in the wrong town never found out what they could have been. The internet destroyed that, most of all for people like us. Any essay mourning the old smallness must admit the old smallness also trapped people, gossiped about them, and gave them nowhere to go. **The claim isn't "internet was a mistake." It's that access scaled by billions and capacity scaled by zero, and we've never acknowledged the gap.**

**The landing — the only honest one.** Not "log off"; nobody does that and it's a cop-out. Every generation before us had limits set for them: geography decided who you knew, distance decided what you heard. Nobody had to choose, because the world chose. **We are the first humans who have to manufacture our own limits, by hand, against a system built and funded to keep giving us more.** Curation is a survival skill nobody's parents could teach, because nobody's parents needed it.

**Finishing her trailed-off sentence.** Options: we have more access to other people's opinions of us than any human has ever had / more people to compare ourselves to than we have hours to live / **more information about the world than we have power to change it** — that last one is the real one, and possibly the title line.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

In 1991, something happened to humanity that I don't think we have ever fully sat down to process.

The World Wide Web went public. And I'm being specific about that year on purpose, because the internet had existed before then in some form, but 1991 is when it stopped being infrastructure for institutions and became something an ordinary person could open. That's the difference. Not scientists, not governments, not universities. You. Me. Anybody with a connection.

And so many things changed.

Because before that, we were limited in several ways. The people you knew were limited, basically to whoever was physically near you. The knowledge you had access to was limited, to whatever books reached your town and whatever the people around you happened to know. Your circle of influence was significantly limited. And then the internet happened, and all of a sudden there are billions of people I can reach from my phone. Billions. I can open this thing in my hand and access more human beings than every ancestor I've ever had could reach combined. And more knowledge than existed in any library in history.

And it is a good thing. I want to say that clearly before I say anything else, because I mean it, and I'll come back to it properly later. Look at how much has changed. It was evolutionary.

But.

I think the internet did so well that we now know more than we should know. We know more people than we should know. We have more access than we should have.

Now let me explain what I mean by "should," because I don't mean it in a moral way at all. I'm not saying there's forbidden knowledge. I'm not saying go back to the village. What I mean is closer to this: a human being should not drink forty litres of water. Not because water is bad, not because forty litres is a sin, but because the system that processes it was never built for that volume. That's all I'm saying. It's not a morality problem. It's a capacity problem.

And there's an actual number for this, which is the part that shook me when I found it.

There's an anthropologist called Robin Dunbar, and he studied primates, and he found that the size of the social group a species can hold matches the size of a particular part of their brain. So he ran the numbers for humans. And the answer he got is about 150.

You can maintain real relationships with about 150 people. And by real I mean you actually know them, you know your history with them, you know how they connect to everybody else. And it's not even flat, it's in layers. About five people in your innermost circle, the ones you'd call at 2am. Around fifteen close friends. About fifty. Then 150. Then 500 people you'd call acquaintances, and about 1,500 faces you can simply recognise.

And this number keeps showing up everywhere in history. Hunter-gatherer clans. Neolithic villages. Roman army units. Modern military companies. Communities that literally split themselves in two once they grow past it. Different centuries, different continents, same ceiling. That's how you know it's not culture. That's hardware.

Now here's the sentence that got me. Dunbar was asked about social media, and he basically said, when people have thousands of followers, those extra people are not new friends. They're just your outer layers filling up. The 500 and the 1,500.

Meaning the technology never expanded your capacity. It expanded your exposure.

Your brain still has 150 seats, and now there are millions of people standing in the room.

And this is where it gets uncomfortable, because what happens to everybody who doesn't get a seat? They don't just get filtered out. Your brain does not have a setting that says "this person is content, not a human being." Sociologists actually named this in 1956, before the internet even existed, just from watching people with their televisions. They called it parasocial interaction. The feeling of a real relationship with somebody who does not know you exist.

The internet took that and industrialised it.

So now people are spending real relational capacity, the finite kind, the 150 kind, on strangers who cannot reciprocate. And you see it. You see how people grieve when a creator they've never met stops posting. You see how betrayed people feel when someone they've never spoken to changes their opinion on something. And I don't think that's stupidity. I think that's a 150-seat brain doing exactly what it was designed to do, in an environment it was never designed for. You're not imagining that you know these people. Some part of you actually does. That's the whole problem.

But honestly, the information side might be heavier than the people side.

Because think about how it used to work. For almost all of human history, what you could know about suffering was about the same size as what you could do about it. You heard the child was sick because the child lived down your street, and you could carry food down that street. Awareness and ability were the same circle. They matched.

The internet snapped them apart.

Now, before breakfast, before you've even brushed your teeth properly, you can know about a war, a famine, a building that collapsed, a child that died, on four different continents. And you can do essentially nothing about any of it. Your moral concern has been scaled to the size of the planet, and your moral capability is still the size of one street.

So when people say they feel numb, or they feel tired, or they feel guilty for scrolling past something terrible, I don't think that's compassion failure. I think it's arithmetic. You're being handed a quantity of grief calibrated for a species with unlimited reach, and you have one body. One salary. One afternoon.

And then there's the smaller version of this that hurts people every single day, which is comparison.

You used to be the best singer in your area. And I mean genuinely, actually the best, because your area was the entire measurable world. Excellence was local, which meant excellence was achievable, which meant people actually got to feel it sometimes. Now every single thing you do has a visible global ceiling and it loads in under one second. There is always somebody doing your thing better, and you can watch them do it, for free, at 2am, in bed.

No human being in history has ever had to hold their ordinary, decent, normal competence up against the top zero point zero zero one percent of the entire species, every day, before they've even gotten out of bed. Our self-esteem was built for a village-sized comparison. It's now being benchmarked against eight billion people.

There's a biologist, E.O. Wilson, who said the real problem of humanity is that we have paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, and god-like technology. And I think that's the whole thing in one sentence. The mismatch is not between good technology and bad technology. It's between the speed of the tool and the speed of the animal holding the tool.

And the way I understand it best is sugar.

We crave sweetness because for hundreds of thousands of years sugar was rare, and it meant survival, so the people who wanted it badly are the ones who lived long enough to become our ancestors. That appetite was correct. It was perfectly calibrated for a world where sugar was scarce. Then we built abundance, and the same appetite that saved us started killing us. And nothing about the appetite was ever wrong. The environment changed and the appetite didn't get the memo.

Our hunger to know things about other people is exactly the same instinct. In a village, knowing everything about everybody was survival information. You needed to know who to trust, who owed who, who was dangerous. Now the supply is infinite, and nobody has told the appetite to stop.

Now let me be fair, and I don't want this to sound like a small disclaimer at the end, because I actually mean it.

The people who gained the most from the internet are the people who were furthest from power. Access used to be gatekept by geography and by birth. A brilliant person in the wrong town simply never got to find out what they could have been. Their whole life, gone, because of where they were standing. The internet destroyed that, and it destroyed it most for people like us. Everything I do, I could not do without it. I have learned things from strangers on the internet that nobody physically around me could have taught me.

So please don't hear me romanticising the old smallness. The old smallness also trapped people. It gossiped about them. It told them who they were and gave them nowhere else to go.

So the point is not that the internet was a mistake. The point is that our access multiplied by billions and our capacity multiplied by zero, and we have never once, as a species, sat down and acknowledged that gap.

And I'll tell you what I keep landing on, because it's the only honest ending I can find.

The answer is not log off. Nobody is logging off. I'm not logging off, you're not logging off, let's stop pretending.

The real thing is this. Every generation before us had their limits set for them. Geography decided who you knew. Distance decided what you heard about. Nobody had to sit down and choose their limits, because the world chose on their behalf. They were protected by their own smallness.

We are the first human beings in history who have to manufacture our own limits. Deliberately. By hand. Against a system that is specifically built, and specifically funded, to keep giving us more.

And nobody taught us how to do that. Our parents couldn't teach us, because they never needed it. There's no wisdom passed down for this. There's no proverb for it. We're the first ones, and we're doing it live.

So if you're tired, if you feel like your head is too full, if you feel like you know too much and can do too little, I don't think there's anything wrong with you. I think you're a paleolithic person holding a god-like machine, and you're doing your best.

We have more information about the world than we have power to change it. That's the sentence. That's the whole thing.

And figuring out what to let in, that used to be geography's job. Now it's yours.
