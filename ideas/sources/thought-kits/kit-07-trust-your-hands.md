# KIT 07 — Trust Your Hands, Not Your Mouth

**Premise:** Words come cheap *because they come natural* — they cost nothing, so they prove nothing. Action costs something, so only action is evidence. And the real move is turning this on yourself: the gap between what you said and what you did is integrity, in the literal sense — integrated, undivided, mouth and hands saying the same thing.

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The core distinction, sharpened.** Not just "talk is cheap" — talk is *effortless*, it flows out almost involuntarily, and that ease is exactly what makes it untrustworthy. The buy-you-a-house example: the words cost nothing to say, which is precisely why they prove nothing. Actions carry a price tag (effort, time, discomfort, follow-through); words don't. Only the thing with a price tag can be evidence of intent. "If something doesn't cost you much, it cannot be the evidence of much."

**The inward turn (this is what makes it hers, not the cliché).** Most people use "trust actions not words" to judge *other people* and protect themselves from liars. She turned it on herself — her own words vs her own actions. Easy to demand other people's hands match their mouths; much harder to hold your own hands to your own mouth, because you hear all your own good intentions and give yourself credit for the saying. But the world (and your own life) only runs on the doing. Intentions are invisible; the record is what's left.

**Faith anchor — the two sons (Matthew 21:28–31).** Father tells both sons to work the vineyard. First says "no," then goes and does it. Second says "yes sir," and doesn't go. Which did the father's will? The one who *did* it — hands moved, not the mouth that said the right thing. "Trust your hands not your mouth" sitting in scripture two thousand years early. (Also in the family: James — "faith without works is dead," "be doers of the word and not hearers only.")

**The deepening that keeps it from self-flagellation.** The goal isn't to speak less — the silent person isn't more honest, just quiet. The aim is to *close the gap*. Integrity in the literal, old sense: *integrated* — whole, undivided, mouth and hands the same thing, claim and record the same thing. Not silence. Alignment.

**The tender landing.** Trust isn't given to the mouth, it's *earned by the hands and then lent to the mouth afterward*. When your hands keep a long enough record, your word starts to mean something again because it's backed by evidence. So the work isn't to talk less — it's to build the track record that makes your words trustworthy again.

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

I started to pay more attention to my words in comparison with my actions. Because at the end of the day, it is not about what I say I'm going to do. It's about what I actually do.

And this is not about work, or career, or any of that. It's simpler and it's bigger than that. It's just about doing what you say you'll do. Being who you claim to be. Because words are so sweet, and here's the thing I keep coming back to, words come natural to us. It is so easy for us to say something. In many cases it feels like saying a thing doesn't even cost us anything, except, I don't know, the movement of the mouth, whatever the biology of it is. It's effortless.

If I come here right now and tell you I'm going to buy you a house and a car tomorrow morning, those are my words. Beautiful words. And I probably won't do any of it. It cost me nothing to say, and that is exactly the point. Action costs you something. Effort. Time. Discomfort. Follow-through. And if something doesn't cost you much, it cannot be the evidence of much. It just can't.

We say actions speak louder than words. It sounds cliché, everybody's heard it. But I think it's actually deeper than "louder." It's not just that actions are louder. It's that we need to learn to trust actions more than words, and then evaluate our own words against our own actions. That's the part. What is the gap between what I say and what I do? How close are my words to my hands? How far apart are they?

I saw a comment on social media the other day and it just said, trust your hands, not your mouth. And bro. That was it. That said everything.

And you know this is not a new idea, it's an old, old one. Jesus told a parable about this exact thing, the two sons, in Matthew 21. A father goes to his two sons and tells both of them, go and work in the vineyard. The first son says, no sir, I'm not doing that. Flat out. But then later he goes and actually does it. The second son says, yes sir, absolutely, I'll go. And he doesn't go. And Jesus asks the question, which of the two did the will of their father? And the answer is the first one. The one who did it. Not the one who said the right thing. The one whose hands moved. Two thousand years ago and it's the same message, trust the hands, not the mouth.

Now here's what I've had to be honest with myself about. We are always imposing this thing on other people. We're quick to check everybody else, oh he said he'd do this and he didn't, you can't trust her word. Very quick. But it's a whole different thing to turn it on yourself. To sit down and ask, this thing I said, did I do it? The gap between what I said and what I did, what is living in that gap?

Because that gap actually has a name. That gap is integrity. And I mean integrity in the literal sense of the word, not the surface sense. We hear integrity and we think, oh, I don't steal, I don't cheat, I don't lie, okay, fine. But the word integrity means integrated. Whole. Undivided. Your mouth and your hands being the same thing. Your claim and your record being the same thing. What you say and what you do, one thing, not two. That's what the word actually means. Integrated.

And this is something I am increasingly convicted about as I grow, because I've realised it's not a one-time achievement. It's a gap you have to keep closing. Again and again, in different situations, in different contexts. New promise, new gap, close it again. It doesn't end.

But this is my actual desire, so let me just say it plainly. I want to become someone whose word is worth something. And not because I talk well, anybody can talk well. I want my word to be worth something because my hands have a track record of backing it up. Because when I say a thing, there is a history behind me of me being someone who does the thing. That's the whole goal. Not to talk less. To close the gap until my mouth and my hands are finally saying the same thing.

Trust your hands, not your mouth. Even when the mouth is yours. Especially when the mouth is yours.
