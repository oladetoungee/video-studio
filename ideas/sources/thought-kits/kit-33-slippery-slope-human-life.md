## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The case, verified.** Nidal Hasan, a former Army major and psychiatrist, killed 13 people and wounded dozens at Fort Hood, Texas, on November 5, 2009. A military jury convicted him in 2013 and sentenced him to death. The Army set his execution for December 3, by firing squad, at Fort Hood. A Pentagon official said it will be livestreamed, and Defense Secretary Hegseth said it should act as a deterrent. The last public execution in the US was in 1936. The military's last firing-squad execution was in 1945.

**The reaction crossed every line.** Democratic Senator Chris Murphy called it disgusting. Republican Senator Thom Tillis also criticised it. The US Conference of Catholic Bishops condemned it. The UN called it abhorrent, Amnesty said it showed "an appalling disregard for human dignity," and legal experts raised concerns about minors watching and about international bans on degrading punishment. Her draft said "the Pope"; no papal statement turned up, so the final credits the Catholic bishops.

**Her strongest ground is the spectacle.** Many people who support the death penalty are still against broadcasting a death, which is why the backlash crossed parties. She doesn't need to win the whole death penalty argument to make this point. The final says this out loud so it doesn't read as a dodge.

**She already honours the victims.** Thirteen lives, taken and not given back. That has to come early, before any word about Hasan himself, so no one hears this as sympathy for what he did.

**History is her "we are going back."** Public executions used to draw crowds, sometimes thousands of people. Societies moved them out of public view partly because watching death as entertainment was seen as damaging to the people watching. A livestream puts it back on a screen for anyone, including children.

**The other side's best case.** Supporters say a sentence this serious honours the victims, and the Pentagon says showing it will deter attacks. The final names both and answers them. Families of victims may want justice seen, and that wish is understandable. But a punishment can be carried out without becoming a show, and the evidence that watching executions deters violence is weak. The final keeps that claim soft rather than citing studies.

**The hard cases.** Her "regardless of the situation" will be pushed on. Self-defence, war, a man who murdered 13 people. The final admits the hard cases exist and says the principle still has to be held, because the moment dignity depends on deserving it, it isn't dignity anymore, it's a reward.

**The tiger video.** Several incidents exist and old videos get reposted, so it stays "I saw a video." Honest guard: people who say they're glad the tiger is fine often mean the animal didn't choose to be there and would be killed for doing nothing wrong. Her point survives: when relief for the animal comes before grief for a dead man, something has shifted.

**Human life above animal life.** Her clearest statement. Grounded in Genesis 1:27, people are made in the image of God. Loving animals is good, but a person's death is not the same loss as a pet's.

**The scroll.** Desensitisation isn't only these two stories. We scroll past death every day. That connects her slope to ordinary life without repeating her earlier kit on psychic numbing.

**Faith, both directions.** Genesis 1:27 for dignity. Ezekiel 33:11, God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. Proverbs 24:17, don't rejoice when your enemy falls. Fair note: some Christians cite Genesis 9:6 to support the death penalty, using the same image-of-God reasoning. The final names that honestly and lands on the point both sides share: even if a life is taken by law, it should never be enjoyed.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice, teleprompter format)

**[OPEN]**

The civilized world is treading a very slippery slope with human life.

A very slippery slope.

And it will not end well.

The dignity and the value of human life has to keep being emphasized, regardless of the situation.

And I'm saying this because of two things I saw this week.

**[SECTION 1: THE LIVESTREAM]**

The first one is this.

The United States is planning to livestream an execution.

The man is Nidal Hasan.

In 2009, he opened fire at Fort Hood, an army base in Texas, and killed 13 people.

He was sentenced to death, and the execution is set for December 3, by firing squad.

And a Pentagon official has said it will be livestreamed.

The last time America held a public execution was in 1936.

So this is going back almost a hundred years.

**[SECTION 2: THE PEOPLE HE KILLED]**

Before I say anything else, I want to be very clear about the people he killed.

Thirteen people.

Thirteen human lives.

What he did was terrible, and those lives are part of my whole argument.

Because human life is so critical.

When it's taken, it cannot be given back.

So I'm not saying this because I'm on his side.

I'm saying this because I'm on the side of human life, and that includes theirs.

**[SECTION 3: THIS IS ABOUT THE SPECTACLE]**

You might support the death penalty, or you might not.

That's a big conversation, and I'm not trying to settle it in one video.

But even if you believe a person should be executed, turning their death into a livestream is a different thing.

That's turning a death into something to watch.

And it's not only me who feels this way.

Senators on both sides of American politics have criticized it.

The US Catholic bishops condemned it.

The UN called it abhorrent.

When people who agree on almost nothing all feel that something is wrong, I think we should listen.

**[SECTION 4: WE HAVE BEEN HERE BEFORE]**

There was a time in society when human life didn't have the value it has now.

Executions used to be public.

People would gather in crowds, sometimes thousands of them, to watch someone die.

And over time, society decided that this was damaging.

Not only to the person being killed, but to the people watching.

We moved it out of the public eye because we understood what it does to us.

So when we put it back on a screen, for anyone to watch, including children, I don't see that as progress.

We are going back.

And I keep asking, why?

**[SECTION 5: THE OTHER SIDE]**

I want to be fair to the people who disagree with me.

The Pentagon says showing the execution will deter people from doing what he did.

And some people feel that the victims' families deserve to see justice done.

I understand that.

If someone killed a person I loved, I can't say what I would feel.

But a sentence can be carried out without becoming a show.

And I find it hard to believe that the answer to the loss of human life is to make death into content.

**[SECTION 6: REGARDLESS OF THE SITUATION]**

When I say regardless of the situation, I know it sounds too simple.

There are hard cases.

Self-defence.

War.

A man who killed 13 people.

It is hard in certain contexts, I won't pretend it's not.

But we have to try as much as we can to resist the urge to value human life less.

Because once dignity depends on whether a person deserves it, it isn't dignity anymore.

It's a reward.

And then we start deciding who earns it and who doesn't.

That's the slope.

**[SECTION 7: GLAD THE TIGER IS FINE]**

The second thing I saw was a video of a young man who went into a tiger enclosure.

He was mauled to death.

And in the comments, there were people saying, I'm glad the tiger is fine.

Now, I understand part of that.

The tiger didn't choose to be there.

And often when an animal kills someone, the animal gets killed too, for doing nothing wrong.

But a human being had just died.

When relief for the tiger comes before grief for the man, something in us has shifted.

When did we become so desensitized to the value and the dignity of human life?

**[SECTION 8: HUMAN LIFE IS NOT A CHICKEN'S LIFE]**

Human life is not a chicken's life.

It's not a dog's life.

I like animals.

We keep them as pets, we enjoy them, they're lovely.

But based on everything a civilization is supposed to stand for, our lives should still carry more dignity than theirs.

Loving animals is good.

Loving them more than people is where it starts to go wrong.

**[SECTION 9: WE SCROLL PAST DEATH]**

And honestly, it's not just these two stories.

We scroll past death every day.

A video of someone dying, then a cooking video, then a joke.

We watch, we react, and we keep going.

And the more we see it like that, the easier it becomes to treat a human life as one more thing on a screen.

A livestreamed execution doesn't come out of nowhere.

It comes from a world that is already used to watching.

**[SECTION 10: MADE IN THE IMAGE OF GOD]**

For me, this goes back to faith.

Genesis 1:27 says that God created human beings in his own image.

That's why a human life is not just another life.

Every person carries something of God, even the worst person, even the person who did something terrible.

Now, I know some Christians support the death penalty, and they use the same idea.

Genesis 9:6 says whoever sheds man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed, because man is made in the image of God.

So I'm not saying every Christian has to agree with me on the death penalty.

But listen to this.

In Ezekiel 33:11, God says, I take no pleasure in the death of the wicked.

No pleasure.

And Proverbs 24:17 says, do not rejoice when your enemy falls.

So even if a life is taken by law, it should never be something we enjoy.

It should never be something we watch for entertainment.

**[CLOSE]**

We are treading a very, very slippery slope.

Human life, once it's taken, cannot be given back.

The 13 lives taken at Fort Hood.

The young man in the tiger enclosure.

And yes, even the life of the man who did something terrible.

The dignity and value of human life has to keep being emphasized, regardless of the situation.

Because the way we treat the worst of us shows what we think of all of us.
