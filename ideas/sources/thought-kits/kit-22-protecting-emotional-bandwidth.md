# KIT 22 — Reserve It for What You Can Reach

**Premise:** After a season of consuming world news all day, her emotions went cold — not because she stopped caring, but because compassion doesn't scale up with numbers, it scales down (psychic numbing). The feed drains the finite feeling that turns into action and leaves nothing for the neighbour at your own junction. Guarding your intake isn't selfishness — it's protecting compassion from decaying into numbness.

**Register:** warm, reflective, first-person, "I sat with this and worked something out" — like the not-knowing kit, arriving at something rather than a sharp debate. Faith in.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

There was a particular year — I think 2023 or 2024 — where I really enjoyed listening to news from around the world, news from the US. I was subscribed to so many American politics podcasters. The major thing that attracted me to some of them was their eloquence — I love when people present their thoughts on an issue in a very profound way, I admire that. And by extension that led to me listening to news from America, Britain, this country, that country.

One thing I experienced that season was an overwhelm of things that were not concerned to me and were not within my sphere of influence. I was consuming so much going on around the world — things I cannot explicitly have a say or any influence over. So I started to protect my emotions, because I understand we don't have that much range over a day. If I expose myself to a hundred pieces of bad news in a day, I find (maybe right, maybe wrong) that the level of emotion or urgency I express toward things close to me — things I can actually influence — reduces. It feels like I'm sharing my attention with so many other things, and my emotions started to go cold. Something would happen close to me, something I might have influence over, and I'd realise I'm not as sad as I should be, because I've exposed myself to so much sad news across the day.

It's like what's happened to us in Nigeria — we hear "fifteen people killed, thirty-six killed, five hundred killed," and you get so used to it that when someone says someone died at the junction of your house, you're sad, but you move past it very quickly. So I really wanted to protect the sanctity of my emotions. Not that I won't express sadness at world news — but I'm learning to reserve my energy for the things I can [reach].

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The name that turns guilt into mechanism: compassion fatigue / the collapse of compassion / psychic numbing.** The counterintuitive, documented finding (Paul Slovic and others): compassion does NOT scale up with the number suffering — it scales *down*. One identifiable person, full feeling; add more, empathy measurably drops; by the time it's a statistic ("500 killed") the response is often *lower* than for the single face. People donate more to one named child than to two, more to two than to eight. The number goes up, the feeling goes down. (Often anchored to the "if I look at the mass I will never act; if I look at the one, I will" line associated with Mother Teresa.) So her going numb to big numbers while a death at the junction still lands = standard human wiring, not brokenness. Built to feel for the one; the feed hands us the mass all day; the mass switches the feeling off.

**The mechanism = the comfort.** Empathy is costly — it taxes you. The mind self-protects: when suffering exceeds what you could act on, it down-regulates feeling to keep you functional. A circuit breaker, not cruelty. But it was designed for a village and the rare encountered tragedy — not a device showing every disaster on earth before lunch. So the breaker stays permanently tripped; empathy dialled down as a default setting.

**Her "sharing" metaphor is a folk version of a real thing.** Emotional energy/attention spent over here isn't available over there. And the cruel twist that sharpens her point: the suffering she spends it on (500 killed elsewhere) is exactly the suffering she can do nothing about — so she arrives depleted at the one place feeling could become help. Emotionally bankrupt at the doorstep of the thing she could change, because she gave it away at the door of the things she can't.

**Her Nigeria example is the perfect, central illustration — keep it central.** "15 killed, 36, 500," until numbers stop meaning anything, then a death at your junction and you move past it fast. Psychic numbing in its purest local form. The cost isn't abstract: the numbers spent the grief she needed for her own neighbour. More honest and devastating than any US-podcast example.

**The honesty guard (must close this door or it reads as selfishness).** "Protecting my emotions" can slide into "I've decided not to care about anyone outside my circle" = selfishness in a wellness outfit. The distinction: she's not reducing how much she cares — she's refusing to let caring be harvested and wasted by a feed that converts it into pure exhaustion. Feeling that never becomes action decays into numbness, and numbness is the real death of compassion. Paradox: the person who consumes every disaster often does the least (too flooded to move); the one who guards intake stays able to act. Goal isn't to feel less — it's to keep feeling translating into action somewhere.

**The organizing frame — Covey's circle of concern vs circle of influence** (she already used "sphere of influence"). Concern = everything you care about (huge). Influence = what you can affect (small: your street, people, work). The feed inflates concern to planet-size while influence stays the same. Pour energy into concern → helplessness, and influence actually shrinks (you're depleted). Proactive people work inside the circle of influence, and it grows. So guarding intake = pulling energy back into the circle where it can do something.

**Faith turn (fits genuinely well).** "Love your neighbour" — the *near* one, the one next to you. The command was always local, never "have a feeling about all 8 billion." The Good Samaritan loved the one bleeding man in front of him, not humanity in the abstract. Scattered feed-driven concern for everyone can become a distraction from — even an avoidance of — the concrete, costly love owed to the actual neighbour. Loving the mass on the screen as an excuse not to love the neighbour at the junction.

**The reframe that makes it not-selfish (the landing).** She used to think consuming all of it was itself caring — staying informed about every tragedy as a moral act that honoured the victims. Came to see it as almost a trick: feeling gutted about 500 untouchable things, then having nothing for the one she can touch, doesn't help the 500 and fails the one. The appearance of compassion with none of its substance. Not protecting herself because she doesn't care — protecting the part that turns pain into action, because it's finite and the feed drains it for free. "Caring about everything is often how you end up doing nothing." Rather feel fully for what's near and move, than feel a thin grey wash for everything and move for nothing.

**Decisions:** faith IN; warm reflective first-person register; Nigeria example central; keep her "sphere of influence," her sharing metaphor, her "sanctity of emotions."

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

Let me tell you about a season I went through, and something I worked out from it.

There was a particular year, I think it was around 2023, 2024, somewhere there, where I really got into listening to news from around the world. News from the US especially. I was subscribed to so many American politics podcasters. I would just listen to these guys. And honestly, the thing that pulled me in for a lot of them was the eloquence. I really love it when somebody can take an issue and lay out their thoughts on it in a profound, beautiful, well-arranged way. I admire that so much. So I'd listen for the eloquence, and by extension I ended up just deep in it, news from America, news from Britain, news from this country, news from that country. What happened here, what happened there.

And somewhere in that season, something started to feel off.

Because I realized I was consuming an overwhelming amount of things that had nothing to do with me. Things that were nowhere near my sphere of influence. I would sit with this feeling like, I am taking in so much, so many things happening all over the world, things I do not have and cannot have any say over, any influence on whatsoever. And it started to do something to me.

And here's the thing I actually noticed, and it disturbed me a little. I noticed my emotions were going cold.

Because think about it. In a single day, if I expose myself to a hundred pieces of bad news, disaster here, tragedy there, this many people died here, this terrible thing happened there, another, another, another, I find, and I might be right or I might be wrong about the mechanism, but I find that the amount of feeling I have left for the things actually close to me starts to drop. Something would happen right near me, something I could actually do something about, and I'd notice, wait, I'm not as sad about this as I should be. And it was because I'd already spent the day pouring out sadness over a hundred things far away. It felt like I had shared my emotion out so thin, across so many things, that there wasn't enough of it left for the thing at my own door.

And it turns out this is an actual known thing, which made me feel less crazy about it. Psychologists have found something they call psychic numbing, and the finding is honestly disturbing. Our compassion does not go up as the number of suffering people goes up. It goes down. One person suffering, you feel it completely. But show people a tragedy with a huge number attached, five hundred, a thousand, and the feeling is often lower than it was for the single face. There's a reason we can cry over one child and go numb at "five hundred killed." We were built to feel for the one. We were never built to feel for the mass. And the news feed hands us nothing but the mass, all day long.

And we know this in Nigeria intimately, don't we. You hear it. Fifteen people killed. Thirty-six people killed. Five hundred killed. And you keep hearing these numbers, over and over, until they stop landing. Until they're just numbers. And then somebody says, oh, someone died at the junction near your house, and yes, you're sad, but notice how fast you move past it. Notice how quickly you're okay again. That's not because you're wicked. That's because the numbers already spent the grief you were supposed to have for your own neighbor. That's the part that actually frightens me. It's not just that the far-away suffering stopped moving me. It's that it quietly stole the feeling I needed for the person down my own road.

So I started doing something about it. I started protecting my emotions. And I need to be really careful here about what I mean, because this can be misunderstood so easily.

I am not saying I decided to stop caring about anybody outside my little circle. I'm not talking about becoming that person who tunes out the whole world's suffering and calls it self-care, that thing where selfishness just puts on a wellness outfit. That's not it. If something sad happens in the world, of course I'll feel it, of course I'll express whatever I genuinely feel. I'm not shutting the door on human suffering.

What I'm protecting is different. I'm protecting the part of me that actually turns feeling into action. Because here's what I came to understand. Feeling that never becomes action doesn't just sit there harmlessly. It decays. It curdles into numbness. And numbness is the real death of compassion, not busyness, not distance, numbness. So the person who consumes every single disaster on earth is very often the person who ends up doing the least, because they're too flooded, too drained, too numb to actually move for anyone. They mistake the feeling for the doing. Feeling terrible about a hundred things you can't touch feels like compassion, but it produces nothing. It's the appearance of caring with none of the substance.

There's this idea I found that helped me organize it. You have a circle of concern, which is everything you care about, everything you're aware of, and it's huge. And then you have a circle of influence, which is the much smaller circle of things you can actually affect. Your street. Your people. Your work. Your actual reach. And what the feed does is blow up your circle of concern to the size of the whole planet, while your circle of influence stays exactly the same size it always was. So you end up carrying the weight of the entire world with the two hands you were given for your own little corner. And the people who pour all their energy into that giant outer circle, the stuff they can't touch, they end up helpless and depleted, and ironically their circle of influence actually shrinks, because they've got nothing left to act with.

So what I'm doing is not going cold. It's pulling my energy back into the circle where it can actually do something. Which is the only place it was ever going to matter anyway.

And honestly, this is where my faith met me on it. Because the command was always love your neighbor. Neighbor. The near one. The one actually next to you. It was never "have a feeling about all eight billion people." Even the Good Samaritan, he didn't love humanity in the abstract, he stopped for the one bleeding man on the road right in front of him. And I've started to think that scattering my concern across everything on my phone can actually become a way of avoiding the real, costly, specific love I'm supposed to give the person right beside me. Loving the mass on the screen can quietly become an excuse for not loving the neighbor at the junction.

So I used to think consuming all of it was itself a kind of caring. Like staying informed about every tragedy everywhere was a moral act, like it honored those people somehow. And I've come to believe that was almost a trick I was playing on myself. Because feeling gutted about five hundred things I cannot touch, and then having nothing left for the one thing I can, does not help the five hundred, and it fails the one.

So no, I'm not protecting myself from the world's pain because I don't care. I'm protecting the part of me that still knows how to turn pain into action, because that part is finite, and it's precious, and the feed will drain it completely, for free, and leave me too numb to help the person standing right in front of me.

I'd rather feel it fully for what's near me and actually move, than feel a thin gray wash of sadness for everything and move for nobody.

That's what I'm learning to do. Reserve my energy for the things I can actually reach.

## REFERENCES
- **Psychic numbing / collapse of compassion** — Paul Slovic and colleagues: "Psychic Numbing and Genocide" (2007) and related work; Västfjäll, Slovic et al., "Compassion Fade" (PLOS ONE, 2014) — empathy and willingness to help can *drop* as the number of victims rises; people often help a single identifiable victim more than a larger group. Represented accurately ("compassion goes down as numbers go up").
- **Identifiable victim effect** — the documented tendency to feel and give more for one named individual than for statistical groups. Underpins the "cry over one child, numb at 500" line.
- **"If I look at the mass I will never act; if I look at the one, I will"** — widely attributed to Mother Teresa and used by Slovic to frame the research. Not quoted verbatim in the script (attribution is contested), used as the underlying idea only.
- **Circle of concern vs circle of influence** — Stephen Covey, *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* (1989). Proactive focus on the circle of influence tends to expand it; reactive focus on the circle of concern breeds helplessness. Represented accurately.
- **Love your neighbour / Good Samaritan** — Mark 12:31; Luke 10:25–37. The Samaritan aids the specific man on the road. Accurate.
- **Accuracy guard kept in script:** whether empathy is *strictly* a finite "budget" is debated; the script frames the depletion as her felt experience ("I find, and I might be right or wrong about the mechanism") rather than asserting settled science, while the numbing/compassion-fade findings (which ARE well supported) carry the hard claim.
