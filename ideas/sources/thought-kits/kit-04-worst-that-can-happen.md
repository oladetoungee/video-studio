# KIT 04 — What's the Worst That Can Happen

**Premise:** "What's the worst that can happen" is never a real question — it's a confidence ritual. And sometimes the worst gets served anyway, plated, on a Versace dish with the Medusa staring back. The irony, and what's on the other side of it.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

"Common, what's the worst thing that can happen today?" And then the worst, unimaginable thing actually gets served to you on a nice plate — a nice, well-known, classy, expensive plate. *(Chosen plate: Versace / gold Medusa.)*

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The phrase is never a real question.** It's a confidence ritual — we say it to shrink the day, to set the ceiling of possible damage low. And every culture treats saying it as dangerous: English speakers knock on wood; Nigerians shout *God forbid, it's not your portion* before you finish. Universal folk instinct that naming the worst invites it.

**The research behind the instinct.** Risen & Gilovich (2008), "Why People Are Reluctant to Tempt Fate." Students judged a fictional applicant's chances at Stanford *lower* when he wore the Stanford t-shirt his mum sent while awaiting the decision (tempting fate) than when he stuffed it in a drawer. Rational students, still felt the universe would punish the presumption. Mechanism: tempting fate makes the negative outcome more mentally *accessible*, so it *feels* more likely. Important: this shows *our minds* take the order, not that the universe does.

**The image IS the essay: it gets SERVED.** Disaster, when it comes, arrives with strange elegance — on time, well organised, beautifully formatted email, calm professional voice. Nobody serves catastrophe in a rubber plate. It comes fine dining, garnished, like the universe has a Michelin kitchen and your table was reserved without your knowledge. The politeness of catastrophe. **Plate pick:** Versace gold Medusa — instantly recognisable, and the Medusa face at the bottom staring back is a gift.

**Job breaks the superstition.** Job 3:25 — "the thing I greatly feared has come upon me." And crucially Job was *careful* — habitually offering sacrifices for his children's possible sins, covering every base, saying all the right things. The plate still arrived. So the knock-on-wood logic collapses: careless words don't summon disaster, careful words don't prevent it. The mouth was never the mechanism. The essay can't honestly land on "guard your mouth and you'll be fine."

**The real landing = gratitude by statistics.** Count the mornings you've asked/lived "what's the worst that can happen" and *nothing* came. Thousands. We make stories about the one day the plate arrived; nobody makes content about the ten thousand times fate declined the invitation. Nothing happening is itself something — mercy with no garnish, so we don't notice we were served.

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

"Common, what's the worst thing that can happen today?"

And then the worst, most unimaginable thing actually gets served to you. Hot. Garnished. On a Versace plate, gold rim, Medusa face at the bottom looking directly at you.

Because have you noticed that "what's the worst that can happen" is never actually a question? Nobody asking it is waiting for an answer. It's a confidence ritual. We say it to shrink the day, to tell ourselves the ceiling of today's damage is low, and then we walk out of the house with our chest. And every culture on this earth seems to agree that saying it is dangerous. Oyibo people knock on wood. In Nigeria, you haven't even completed the sentence before somebody shouts God forbid, it's not your portion, why are you talking like that. Everybody, everywhere, has this instinct that naming the worst is placing an order for it.

And the funny part is psychologists have studied this exact thing. Two researchers, Risen and Gilovich, ran experiments where students judged a guy's chances of getting into Stanford. Same guy, same application. But when they were told he wore the Stanford t-shirt his mum sent him before the decision came out, tempting fate, people rated his chances lower. Wearing a t-shirt! Rational university students, and they still felt the universe would punish the presumption. The researchers' explanation is that once you tempt fate, the bad outcome jumps to the front of your mind, and because you can now see it clearly, it starts to feel more likely. So no, the universe is probably not taking orders. But your own mind is, and it delivers.

Except. Except that sometimes the plate really does arrive. You said the thing in the morning, casually, with your full chest, and by evening the day has gone so spectacularly wrong that you're looking up at the sky like, so you were listening? And notice how the disaster comes o. It doesn't come scattered. It comes plated. The terrible email is beautifully formatted. The bad news is delivered in a calm, professional voice, at a reasonable hour. Nobody serves you catastrophe in a rubber plate. It arrives fine dining, five star presentation, like the universe has a Michelin kitchen somewhere and your table was reserved without your knowledge.

Now, if I stopped here, the lesson would be simple: guard your mouth. And plenty people live exactly like that, tiptoeing around their own sentences, scared to say things are going well in case the roof hears. But there's a man in the Bible who scatters that whole theory, and his name is Job. Job said "the thing I greatly feared has come upon me." Read his story well. Job was not tempting fate. Job was the most careful man in the entire region. He was offering sacrifices for sins his children might have committed, just in case. Covering every base, saying all the right things, holding his tongue properly. And the plate still arrived at his table, full course. So the superstition doesn't even hold its own weight. Careless words don't summon disaster, and careful words don't prevent it. The mouth was never the mechanism.

Which frees us to see the actual truth, and the actual truth is on the other side of the joke. Count how many mornings you have said "what's the worst that can happen" or lived it without saying it, just waltzing into the day with zero protection. Hundreds. Thousands, maybe. And on almost all of those days, nothing happened. No plate. The kitchen was closed. The day just passed, ordinary, uneventful, boring even. We make stories about the one day the worst arrived, but nobody is making content about the ten thousand times fate declined the invitation. Those days don't trend because nothing happened, but nothing happening is itself something. It's mercy with no garnish, so we don't even notice we were served.

So say it if you want. Ask the day what's the worst that can happen. Not because your mouth controls the menu, we've established that it doesn't, in both directions. But because whichever plate arrives, the gold-rimmed disaster or another quiet plate of nothing, you were never the chef, and the One who is has kept you through every single worst day you've had so far. Your record of surviving your worst days, check it, is currently one hundred percent.

> **Note:** the closing line ("record of surviving your worst days is 100%") is a known internet aphorism, not original to you. Swap for your own words if you want the ending fully yours — the essay loses nothing.
