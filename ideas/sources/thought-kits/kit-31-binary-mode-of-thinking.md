## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The real subject is a mode, not a mistake.** Binary thinking isn't only "you liked A so you hate B." It's a whole way of processing: everything gets a sign, plus or minus. Good or bad, for or against, ally or enemy, success or failure, saved or cancelled. What disappears is everything in between: degrees ("a little," "mostly," "not yet"), time (people change), both-and (two true things at once), the third option nobody put on the poll, and "it depends." Rice and beans is just the smallest everyday example of it.

**Her computer instinct, made precise.** Ones and zeros in the hardware aren't really the cause. The better version is in her own field, HCI: interfaces are built from binary inputs. Like or don't. Follow or unfollow. Upvote or downvote. Keep scrolling or stop. Block, report, yes/no polls. The feed learns you as a long list of yeses and nos. You make these two-sided judgments hundreds of times a day, so you're practising binary judgment without noticing. "We shape our tools and thereafter our tools shape us" (John Culkin, 1967, in an essay about McLuhan; usually misattributed to McLuhan). That gives her authority to speak on it as someone who studies human-computer interaction.

**Why the brain goes along with it.** Two boxes are cheap and fast and feel like clarity. "It depends" is slow and uncomfortable. Under speed and social pressure (status, tribe, being seen), quick categories win. Context collapse (Marwick and boyd, 2011) adds to it: online, a sentence arrives with no surrounding context, so readers fill the gap with the worst guess. And engagement rewards it: "why do you hate beans?" gets more replies than "nice."

**When it gets serious it has a clinical name.** All-or-nothing (black-and-white) thinking is one of the classic cognitive distortions described in cognitive therapy (Beck; Burns, *Feeling Good*, 1980): one mistake means I'm a failure, one bad day ruins the week, one wrong opinion makes a person completely bad. Used as a description, not a diagnosis of anyone.

**The proof it's inside heavy users (the line she liked).** People write defensively before anyone replies: "I like rice, no shade to beans." "Not to say X is bad, but." We put disclaimers on our own preferences because we expect to be read in two boxes. We're writing for an audience we expect to read us that way.

**How it spreads.** Opinions come as packages (if you hold this, you must hold that). You can't disagree with a friend on one thing without it feeling like you're against them. You can't criticise something you love, because in two boxes criticism means hatred. And the hardest one: we do it to ourselves. Succeeding or failing, good or bad, with no room for "I'm growing."

**Honest guard (so it doesn't become "everything is grey").** Some things really are binary: a fact is true or not, consent is yes or no, right and wrong are real. Two boxes are fine for things that truly come in two. The trouble is forcing two boxes onto things that come in many shades. The skill is discernment, knowing which is which.

**Faith, done properly.** Scripture isn't anti-binary (light and darkness, life and death, "let your yes be yes," Matthew 5:37). But Jesus repeatedly refused false binaries. John 9:2-3: the disciples ask "who sinned, this man or his parents?" and Jesus says neither, and gives an answer that wasn't on their list. Matthew 22:17-21: the Caesar tax question, built as a yes/no trap, answered with "give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's." He honoured real binaries and stepped out of fake ones, which is exactly the discernment the guard describes.

**Landing.** A practice, not a slogan: when you feel yourself reaching for one of two boxes, ask whether the thing is actually shaped like two boxes. Most of the time it isn't. Most of life is "and."

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice, teleprompter format)

*(Each section is written to make sense on its own, so it can be cut into shorts.)*

**[OPEN]**

I don't have the perfect words for this.

But there's something I like to describe as the binary classification of knowledge.

And social media promotes it.

If you use social media a lot, you've probably started thinking in binary, whether you meant to or not.

You think in two ways.

Good or bad.

For or against.

Right or wrong.

With me, or against me.

It's the reason someone can say, I like rice, and another person in the comment section will reply, why do you hate beans?

Nobody said anything about beans.

But in a binary mind, liking one thing means you must be against the other.

And honestly, I don't find it surprising at all.

**[THE BUTTONS]**

At its root, social media is algorithms.

Computer programs.

And computers run on binary, ones and zeros.

Maybe I'm blabbing here.

But the more I think about it, the less I think it's the ones and zeros.

I think it's the buttons.

Look at what the apps actually let you do.

Like, or don't like.

Follow, or unfollow.

Upvote, or downvote.

Keep scrolling, or stop.

Block. Report. Yes or no.

Almost every choice the app gives you has two sides.

And you make those choices hundreds of times a day.

So you're practising binary judgement, over and over, without even noticing.

I study human-computer interaction, and one idea I keep running into is that the tools we use shape the way we think.

There's an old line people usually credit to Marshall McLuhan, but it was actually his friend John Culkin who wrote it.

We shape our tools, and thereafter our tools shape us.

I think that's what's happening to us.

We were given a thumbs up and a thumbs down for everything, and slowly we started looking at the whole world that way.

**[WHY IT'S SO EASY]**

Our brains don't mind binary thinking, honestly.

Two boxes are easy.

Putting something in a box is quick, and it feels like clarity.

"It depends" is slow.

"It's a bit of both" is uncomfortable.

And when you're scrolling fast, and everyone around you is reacting fast, you don't have time for slow.

So the quick box wins.

Psychologists even have a name for it when it gets serious.

All-or-nothing thinking.

Black-and-white thinking.

It's the kind of thinking where one mistake means you're a failure.

One bad day means the whole week is ruined.

One wrong opinion means a person is completely bad.

**[THE PROOF]**

Here's what convinced me that social media is changing how heavy users think.

Look at how people write now.

People write defensively before anybody has even replied.

I like rice, no shade to beans.

Not to say this other thing is bad, but.

We add disclaimers to our own preferences, because we already expect to be read in two ways.

That's the binary thinking getting inside us.

We're not only reading other people that way.

We're writing for an audience we expect to read us that way.

**[HOW IT SPREADS]**

And once binary thinking gets inside you, it spreads.

Opinions start coming in packages.

If you believe this, you must also believe that.

You can't disagree with a friend on one thing without it feeling like you're against them.

You can't criticise something you love, because in two boxes, criticism means you hate it.

And the hardest part is when we start doing it to ourselves.

I'm either succeeding, or I'm failing.

I'm either a good person, or I'm a bad one.

There's no room for, I'm growing.

I'm learning.

I'm better than I was, and I'm not where I want to be yet.

**[WHAT WE LOSE]**

Think about everything that lives between the two boxes.

Degrees.

A little. Mostly. Not yet.

Time.

A person who was wrong then, and is right now.

Both.

Two things that are true at the same time.

And the third option, the one nobody put on the poll.

That's where most of real life happens.

Relationships. Faith. People.

Most of the important things in life are not ones and zeros.

**[THE FAIR PART]**

Now, let me be fair.

Some things really are binary.

A fact is true, or it isn't.

Consent is yes, or no.

Right and wrong are real.

So I'm not saying everything is grey and nothing can be judged.

Two boxes are fine for the things that really come in two.

The trouble starts when we force two boxes onto things that come in many shades.

And the skill is knowing which one you're looking at.

**[FAITH]**

And this is where Jesus fascinates me.

Because Jesus wasn't afraid of a real binary.

He said, let your yes be yes, and your no be no.

But when people handed him a false one, he refused it.

His disciples once saw a man who was born blind, and they asked him, who sinned, this man or his parents?

Two options.

And Jesus said, neither.

Then he said this happened so that the works of God would be shown in him.

An answer that wasn't on their list at all.

Another time, people tried to trap him with a yes or no question.

Should we pay taxes to Caesar, or not?

And he said, give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and give to God what belongs to God.

Again and again, he stepped out of the boxes people built for him.

And I think that's the kind of mind we're meant to grow.

One that says yes where it's really yes.

No where it's really no.

And "neither, it's bigger than that" when the question itself is too small.

**[CLOSE]**

So this is what I'm trying to practise.

When I feel myself reaching for one of two boxes, I'm trying to stop and ask, is this actually a two-box thing?

Most of the time, it isn't.

You can like rice and like beans.

You can love something and still see what's wrong with it.

You can disagree with someone and still be for them.

Most of life is "and."
