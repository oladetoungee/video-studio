# KIT 13 — Compliment the Decision, Not the DNA

**Premise:** Rule of thumb for complimenting people, especially strangers: compliment something they chose. Braids, jewellery, perfume, jacket, bag, hair colour. Not something they can't change. Because a choice compliment praises the mind that made it, and an attribute compliment just puts them on a scale they can't get off.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

I read this somewhere. One rule of thumb for complimenting people — I want to be more intentional about complimenting people. One rule of thumb when you're complimenting people, especially strangers, is: try to compliment something that they were intentional about, something they had control over, an actual active choice that they made.

For example, you can compliment someone that had new braids — oh yeah, I love your braids — because they intentionally went out of their way to make that hair. You can compliment their jewellery or their necklace, or the way they smell, their scent. I love your piercing. Something creative that they've done with their appearance, their shirt, their tie.

Not something that they don't have control over. Compliment what they actively chose to do. Their music taste. So try to avoid complimenting, say, the shape of their head or the length of their legs. Except of course there are exceptions to the rule. But anything they do intentionally are the easiest, safest compliments to give: outfits, jewellery, they changed their hair colour, their bag, their tie, their shirt, their suit, their jacket, their backpack.

I've been receiving a lot of compliments here since I got to Estonia. I receive compliments too, but I want to be more intentional about giving compliments as well. So yeah, rule of thumb. Thank me later.

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**Why the rule works, mechanically.** A choice compliment doesn't compliment the body — it compliments *the mind that made the choice*. The braids were hours in a chair. The jacket was a decision. The perfume was someone standing in a shop asking "is this me?" Every one is a person who stood at a mirror, decided, and quietly hoped it would land. Saying "I love your braids" means: *the thing you hoped for happened. It worked.*

**Why attribute compliments fail — three mechanisms.** (1) There's nothing to receive; they can't take credit, so they just stand there and get assessed. (2) It signals you've been examining their body — a different feeling entirely. (3) The quiet one: if you can rank them up, you can rank them down. A compliment on something unchangeable reveals they're on a scale in your head and can't get off it. That's why those land cold even when meant warmly.

**The stranger part deserves honesty.** For a woman, a body comment from an unknown man isn't a compliment, it's an evaluation, and it can make her want to leave. A comment about her boots is friendliness. Same intention, opposite landing. So the rule isn't etiquette — it's the line between making someone's day and making them want to get away from you.

**The research, and it's better than expected — Boothby & Bohns (2021), five studies.** Compliment givers underestimate how positively recipients feel, and overestimate how bothered/uncomfortable they'll be — and get it wrong *even in hindsight*. **The killer detail:** the misprediction is driven by the giver's own anxiety and concern about their own competence, not by anything about the recipient. Third-party forecasters watching the same exchange were accurate. So the awkwardness isn't in the room, it's in your head, and it's about you. Also: givers felt *better* afterwards, despite the dread going in. → We're all holding kind words hostage over a fear the research says isn't real.

**Zhao & Epley (2021)** extends it: mistaken expectations are the barrier to expressing compliments; aligning expectations with reality increases willingness to give them.

**The tiers — this handles her "there are exceptions."**
- **Tier 1 (safest, always available):** choices — braids, jewellery, scent, shoes, tie, bag, piercing, hair colour, the song they're playing.
- **Tier 2 (middle):** things not chosen but *done* — a laugh, a smile, the energy someone brings into a room. A smile is an action, not a feature.
- **Tier 3 (highest, rarely reached):** character. "You were so patient with that woman at the counter." "You explained that so clearly." Nobody forgets those; they follow people home. Harder with strangers (needs a moment of observation), but the deepest choice a person makes is not their jacket.

**Faith beat (light, one line).** Proverbs 25:11 — a word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver. Old, beautiful way of saying the right words at the right time are worth something. Not free air. Gold.

**The Estonia detail is the actual origin of the essay.** Receiving compliments is what made her notice she wasn't giving them. Keep that turn — it's the honest engine. Also worth noting: in a more reserved culture a compliment costs the giver more, so it carries more weight; it isn't social lubricant, it means they meant it.

**The landing.** The rule isn't etiquette, it's an unblocker — it removes the excuse. And: you WILL think something nice about a stranger today, and you'll say nothing, and the thought will die in your head for no reason. Say it.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

I read this somewhere, and I've been thinking about it since, so let me pass it on.

There's a rule of thumb for complimenting people, especially strangers, and it's this. Compliment something they were intentional about. Something they had control over. An actual, active choice that they made.

So for example, somebody has new braids. You can say, oh my God, I love your braids. Because they went out of their way to make that hair. Somebody's necklace, their earrings, their rings. The way they smell, I love your perfume. Their piercings. Something creative they've done with their appearance, their shirt, their tie, their jacket, their bag, their shoes. They dyed their hair a new colour. Their music taste. All of that is fair game and all of it is safe.

What you're avoiding is the opposite. The shape of their head. The length of their legs. Things they did not choose and cannot change.

And I want to explain why this rule works, because when I sat with it, it's actually deeper than just manners.

When you compliment somebody's choice, you're not really complimenting their body. You're complimenting their mind. Because that jacket was a decision. Those braids were hours in a chair. That perfume was somebody standing in a shop, sniffing things, thinking, is this me? Every one of those is a person who stood in front of a mirror and made a decision and quietly hoped it would land. So when you say I love your braids, what you're actually telling them is, the thing you were hoping for happened. Somebody noticed. It worked.

That's a gift. It costs you nothing and it lands directly.

Now here's why the other kind doesn't work, and it took me a minute to understand this properly. If you compliment something a person didn't choose, they can't receive it. There's nothing to accept, because they didn't do anything. They just have to stand there while you assess them. And it also quietly tells them that you've been studying their body, which is a whole different feeling. And the last one, which is the real one: if you can rank them up, you can rank them down. A compliment about something they can't change tells them they're on a scale in your head, and they cannot get off that scale. That's why those ones land cold even when they were meant warmly.

And let's be honest about the stranger part especially. For a woman, a comment about her body from a man she doesn't know is not a compliment. It's an assessment, and it can make her want to leave. A comment about her boots is just a nice moment. Same intention, completely different result. So this rule is not just politeness. It's actually the difference between making somebody's day and making them want to get away from you.

Now let me tell you the thing I found that shocked me a little.

Some researchers, Boothby and Bohns, ran five studies asking why people don't give more compliments. And what they found is that we consistently underestimate how good the person will feel, and we overestimate how bothered and uncomfortable they'll be. And we still get it wrong even afterwards, even in hindsight.

But this is the part. They found that the wrong prediction was coming from the giver's own anxiety. Our own worry about whether we'll sound weird, whether we'll do it right. Meanwhile, when they had neutral people watch the same interaction and predict how it would go, those people got it right. So the awkwardness is not in the room. The awkwardness is inside your head, and it's about you, not about them.

And they also found that the people who gave the compliments felt better afterwards. Despite the dread going in.

So basically we are all walking around holding kind words hostage because of a fear that the research says isn't real.

And I like this rule specifically because it removes the excuse. If the only thing stopping you is not knowing what's safe to say, fine, here's your list. Braids, jewellery, perfume, shoes, jacket, bag, piercing, hair colour, the song they're playing. Go.

There are levels to it though. The choices are level one, the safe ground, always available. Then there's a middle level, things that aren't chosen exactly but are still done, like somebody's laugh, or their smile, or the energy they bring into a room. Because a smile is technically something you're doing, not something you have.

And then there's the highest level, which most people never bother to reach, and that is complimenting somebody's character. You were so patient with that woman at the counter. You explained that so clearly. The way you handled that was really kind. Nobody forgets those. Those follow people home. They're harder with strangers because you need to have actually watched something, but when you can, please do, because the deepest choice a person makes is not their jacket.

And the Bible has this line I love, that a word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver. Which is just a very beautiful old way of saying the right words at the right time are worth something. They're not free air. They're gold.

Now let me tell you where all of this came from, honestly. Since I got to Estonia, I've been receiving a lot of compliments. And receiving them is exactly what made me realise that I'm not really giving them. I've been on the receiving end enough times to know what it does to your day, and then I checked myself and realised I'm walking around having nice thoughts about people and saying almost none of them out loud.

And I don't want to be like that. I want to be intentional about it.

Because here's the thing. You are going to think something nice about a stranger today. Almost guaranteed. Their bag, their hair, their shoes, something. And you'll think it, and then you'll say nothing, and you'll walk past, and they'll never know. That thought will just die in your head for absolutely no reason.

So say it. Say the thing. It costs you two seconds and one small moment of feeling silly, and the research says the silly feeling isn't even real.

Compliment what they chose. Compliment the decision, not the DNA.

Rule of thumb. Thank me later.
