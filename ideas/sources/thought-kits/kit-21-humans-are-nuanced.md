# KIT 21 — There's Always More Than the Caption

**Premise:** Humans are nuanced, never black and white — and social media makes us forget it because the format can't hold nuance. The proof is your own body: even the belly button, the most universal "everyone-has-it-the-same-way" thing, has innies, outies, and none. If even the flesh won't hold to yes-or-no, why did we think the soul would?

**Register:** warm musing with gentle shape (both, per her call) — thinking-out-loud but with a clear point underneath. Faith woven in light.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

We are human beings, everything about us is nuanced. Never black and white. Social media makes us forget this sometimes…

*(Steer: continue in her tone and structure; use the example of a normal body part that seems like everyone has it in a normal way, yet some people have it so differently from the rest. It's never yes or no with us humans. Both faith thread and gentle shape requested.)*

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The thesis, sharpened.** Social media doesn't just simplify us — the format *requires* flattening. A take fits a caption; a person fits a label; an opinion picks a side. Nuance doesn't perform — "it's complicated, it depends on the person" has never gone viral. The platform selects for the flattest version of every human thing, not by decision but because the flat version travels and the nuanced version dies with nine likes. We start thinking in the shape of the medium: people as yes/no, good/bad.

**The body as the perfect example (her instinct).** Pick a body part that reads as maximally universal, then reveal it's a spectrum. Best pick: **the belly button** — as universal as it gets (everyone was born, everyone had a cord), yet innies, outies, and some people (infant surgery) have none. Even the mark of the one thing every human shares — being born — varies. Reinforcers: ~1 in 8 people are missing the *palmaris longus* forearm muscle and never know; some people are born without wisdom teeth; attached vs free earlobes; rib count. The "standard-issue" defaults are quietly optional; everyone assumes their version is universal.

**Why the body is the Trojan horse.** If even the flesh — the thing with a textbook diagram, the thing you'd most assume is standardized — is a spectrum on nearly everything, then how much more everything softer (personality, reactions, capacities, contradictions). Nobody disputes that bodies vary; it's obvious once you look. So the undeniable physical spectrum carries the harder point: if we're not even physically one standard thing, we were never going to be moral/emotional yes-or-no things.

**Honesty guard (so it doesn't tip into "nothing is true").** Nuance ≠ relativism. Some things are wrong; some questions have answers. "Humans are nuanced" doesn't mean nothing is true — it means a person is almost never captured by the single word we're about to reduce them to. You can hold that someone did a bad thing AND that they're not reducible to the worst thing they did. Nuance isn't the absence of judgment — it's refusing to let one fact become the whole file.

**The landing (ties to flattening).** Social media hands us people as finished verdicts — good, cancelled, genius, trash — which feels efficient and is almost always a lie by omission. A human doesn't fit in a verdict. The practice: when you catch yourself filing a whole person under one word, remember the belly button — even the most standard-issue human thing has innies and outies. Nobody is one thing.

**Faith turn (light).** John 7:24 — "do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment." The appearance is the caption / the flat version; right judgment is the slow one that looks at the whole person. One line, woven in.

**Decisions:** faith IN; both musing tone AND gentle shape; belly button as the anchor example.

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

We are human beings. And everything about us is nuanced. Never fully black and white. And I feel like social media makes us forget this sometimes.

Let me think through why, because it's been bothering me.

The thing about social media is that it can't actually hold nuance. It's not built for it. A take has to fit in a caption. A person has to fit in a label. An opinion has to pick a side. And nuance just doesn't perform, you know? "It's complicated, it depends on the person" has never once gone viral. Nobody's sharing that. So the platform quietly rewards the flattest possible version of every human thing, not because somebody sat down and decided to, but just because the flat version travels and the nuanced version dies with nine likes. So slowly we start thinking in the shape of the thing we're using. We start seeing people as yes or no. Good or bad. This or that.

And humans are just not built like that. We never were.

And I want to give you the clearest example I can think of, and it's your own body. Because here's the thing. There are parts of the body that we treat as completely standard. Like, everyone has it, everyone has it the same way, it's not even a question. The belly button. Everybody has a belly button, right? It's about as universal as it gets. You were born, you had a cord, you have a navel. It feels like the one thing there's nothing to discuss about.

And yet. Innies. Outies. And some people, through surgery when they were babies, don't have one at all. So even the belly button, the little mark of the one single thing every human being on earth has in common, that we were all born, even that shows up differently from person to person.

And it's not just that. You think everybody has the same muscles in their arm, but roughly one in eight people is just missing a particular muscle in their forearm and has no idea, it does nothing they'd ever notice, it's simply not there. You think everybody has wisdom teeth, some people are just born without them. Attached earlobes, free earlobes. The number of ribs. These are the things we assume are standard-issue, the same in everybody, and they're quietly not. Everybody assumes their own version is the universal version. And it just isn't.

And that's the part that gets me. Because if even the body won't hold still for yes or no, if even the flesh, the thing that has an actual textbook diagram, the thing you'd assume above all else is standardized, is actually a spectrum on almost everything, then how much more everything softer than the body? Our personalities. Our reactions. What we're capable of. Our contradictions, the way a person can be two things at once that don't seem like they should fit in the same human. If the body is this varied, why on earth did we ever think the soul would be simpler?

The body is basically the proof. Nobody argues that bodies differ, it's obvious the second you actually look. So it makes the harder point for me. If we're not even physically one standard thing, we were never going to be moral or emotional yes-or-no things either.

Now let me be clear about one thing, because I know how this can be misheard. Saying humans are nuanced is not the same as saying nothing is ever true or nobody is ever wrong. That's not what I'm saying at all. Some things are just wrong. Some questions do have answers. Nuance isn't me throwing judgment out the window. It's just refusing to let one single fact become the entire file on a person. You can believe someone did a genuinely bad thing and also believe that they are more than the worst thing they did. Both. At the same time. That's not confusion, that's just accuracy. That's just what a person actually is.

And I think that's what we lose on these apps. They keep handing us people as finished verdicts. This one's good, this one's cancelled, this one's a genius, this one's trash. And it feels so efficient, it feels clean, and it's almost always a lie by leaving things out. Because a human being does not fit inside a verdict. You don't. The person you're judging doesn't. The person judging you doesn't either.

There's a reason the Bible says do not judge by appearances but judge with right judgment. Because the appearance is the caption. The appearance is the flat version. And right judgment, real judgment, is the slow one. The one that actually looks at the whole person before it decides anything.

So honestly, this is just something I'm trying to hold onto. When I catch myself about to file a whole human being under one word, I'm trying to remember the belly button. Remember that even the most standard, everybody-has-it, surely-this-one-is-simple thing turns out to have innies and outies. Nobody is just one thing. It was never yes or no with us. It was always, it depends, let me actually look at the person in front of me.

We're human beings. There's always more. There's always more than the caption.

## REFERENCES
- **Palmaris longus muscle** — absent in roughly 10–15% of people (varies by population and by side), with no functional deficit; commonly used as a tendon graft donor precisely because it's expendable. "Roughly one in eight" is within the accepted range.
- **Belly button variation** — innie/outie is determined by how the scar heals, not by how the cord was cut; some people who had umbilical surgery as infants (e.g. for omphalocele/hernia) have no navel. Accurate.
- **Congenitally absent wisdom teeth / attached vs free earlobes / rib count variation** — all real, common examples of ordinary anatomical variation. (Earlobe attachment is a spectrum, not the simple single-gene trait it's often taught as — which, fittingly, is itself an example of the essay's point; not belaboured in the script.)
- **John 7:24** — "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment." Quoted accurately, paraphrased in delivery.
