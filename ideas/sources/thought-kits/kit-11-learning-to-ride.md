# KIT 11 — I Am a Freaking Adult Who Cannot Ride a Bike

**Premise:** Decided to learn to cycle in one week, having never ridden in my life. The class turned out to be for experts. Went home angry, posted the embarrassing sentence publicly, and was riding within two days. The barrier was never the bicycle. It was the silence.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

Two weeks ago — last week — I decided that I must learn how to ride a bicycle before the week ran out. Keep in mind I've never ridden a bicycle before in my entire life. I do not know how to ride a bicycle or anything. But in the spirit of living a balanced life, I have come to realise that the physical part of my life is so messed up. I completely suck at doing any physical activity that goes beyond doing my dishes.

So I took matters into my own hands and tried to sign up for a cycling class. I got to the class and it turns out it was for experts, not beginners. So I got back home so pissed, picked up my phone, opened Facebook, and put it up on a Facebook group that I'm a freaking adult that cannot ride a bike and I need to learn how to ride a bike. I had so many amazing people coming to my DM offering to help, and in less than two days I was riding a bike.

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The idiom is the cruellest part.** "It's like riding a bike" is English's phrase for permanent, unforgettable competence. So the culture's metaphor for a skill so basic it's burned into you forever is the exact skill she never had. Every casual use of it quietly excludes a group of adults nodding along who've never done it. That's the shame layer, and it's why so many adults never learn — admitting it feels like admitting you missed a step in becoming a person.

**Far more common than the silence suggests.** Formal adult learn-to-ride programmes exist in London, Newcastle, New York, and beyond. In the Netherlands — the most bike-saturated country on earth — there are bike schools for adults, often serving immigrant women. Nobody talks about it, so everyone who can't ride assumes they're the only one. That's how a common thing stays invisible.

**The philosophical core: riding a bike is THE textbook case of tacit knowledge.** Michael Polanyi's *The Tacit Dimension* (1966) opens with "we can know more than we can tell," and the standard illustrations are recognising a face and riding a bicycle. The knowledge cannot be transferred through words — the body computes balance in a way the mouth can't describe. So there's a real, named reason no class, video, or diagram can hand you this. **Reframe:** she wasn't failing to find a class; she was seeking an explicit answer to a tacit problem. The strangers in her DMs weren't a downgrade from the class. They were the correct technology.

**The class rejection is the sharpest structural point.** Not bad luck — a pattern. Nearly all infrastructure is built for the intermediate: gyms assume you know the machines, bootcamps assume you've coded, language classes assume the alphabet, church groups assume the vocabulary. Nobody serves zero-to-one, and zero-to-one is exactly where the shame lives — so the people with the most fear get the least support. She walked in a genuine zero into a room built for fives and sixes. That anger is the turn of the story.

**The Facebook post is the hinge, and it proves the thesis.** She did what most adults won't: said the embarrassing sentence publicly, name attached. Within 48 hours she was riding. What that demonstrates: the barrier was never the difficulty of cycling — it was the silence. The resources existed the whole time, in the DMs of people she already knew. Shame was doing all the gatekeeping. Drop it and supply appears instantly and in abundance. People *want* to help; they're waiting to be told what you need.

**Two days isn't a miracle, it's method.** The modern adult method turns the bike into a balance bike: drop the saddle so both feet are flat on the ground, remove the pedals, scoot and glide until balance arrives on its own; pedals return only once you can coast several seconds. The old method (someone running behind holding the saddle) is worse. Most adults get the basics in ~2–4 hours with balance-first.

**Her own instinct was pedagogically correct.** The single biggest cause of adult learners failing is a bike that's too tall — if you can't put your feet flat down while seated, every wobble is a fall waiting to happen and fear takes over. Every guide says the same: feet flat first, everything else second. So hunting for a shorter bike was the right move, not a fussy one.

**Almost too on-the-nose:** a London instructor wrote that beginners always try to make the bike go straight, but *the bike needs to wobble in order to balance*. The correction IS the balance. Stability isn't the absence of wobble, it's continuous small wobbling.

**The frame she gave it herself.** Not really about a bicycle. "In the spirit of living a balanced life" — she noticed one whole domain of her life sitting at zero while the others thrived, and refused to leave it there. The deadline was arbitrary and worked *because* it was arbitrary: she didn't wait to feel ready. Nobody feels ready to be visibly bad at something at her age, with her CV, in public.

**Tallinn cooperated:** flat, quiet, proper cycle paths, and July is the window.

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

Two weeks ago, no, last week, I decided that I must learn how to ride a bicycle before the week runs out.

Now keep in mind. I have never ridden a bicycle before in my entire life. Not as a child, not as a teenager, never. I did not know how to ride a bike at all.

But this came from somewhere. In the spirit of living a balanced life, I had come to realise that the physical part of my life is so messed up. Like completely messed up. I suck at any physical activity that goes beyond washing my dishes. That's the level. And when you sit down and look at your life properly, in domains, you can see it. The work part is moving. The mind part is moving. The people part is moving. And then there's this one whole area of my life just sitting at zero, and I have been walking past it for years like it's furniture.

So I took matters into my own hands. I went and signed up for a cycling class. I show up, I'm ready, I'm excited. And it turns out the class was for experts. Not beginners. For people who could already ride a bike.

I went back home so pissed. So pissed.

And can I just say something about that, because the more I think about it, the more I realise it wasn't bad luck, it's a pattern. Almost everything is built for the people who are already somewhere. Gyms assume you know how to use the machines. Coding classes assume you have written code before. Language classes assume you already know the alphabet. Everybody is catering to the intermediate. Nobody is catering to zero. And zero is exactly where all the shame is, so the people who are most afraid get the least help. I walked in there as a genuine zero and the room was built for people at five and six.

So I got home, still angry, picked up my phone, opened Facebook, and I typed it out on a Facebook group. That I am a freaking adult who cannot ride a bike, and I need to learn how to ride a bike.

And my people. The DMs. So many amazing people came into my DM offering to help. Offering their time, offering their bikes, offering to come and stand with me. And in less than two days, I was riding a bike.

Less than two days.

And I've been thinking about what actually happened there, because it's not what I expected. The barrier was never the bicycle. Cycling is not that hard, apparently, when somebody who knows what they're doing is standing next to you. The barrier was the silence. The help was there the whole time. It was sitting inside people I already knew, one post away, and the only thing standing between me and it was whether I was willing to type an embarrassing sentence with my name attached to it.

Shame was doing all the gatekeeping. Not difficulty. Shame.

And I think that's the thing about being a beginner as an adult. When you're a child, being bad at something is your job. Nobody is watching, nobody is judging, you fall off, you get up, that's childhood. But as an adult, you have built a whole identity around being competent. You know things. People come to you for things. So being visibly, publicly terrible at something simple is a very specific kind of exposure, and most adults will just quietly avoid it forever. They'll go their whole life not knowing how to swim, not knowing how to ride, not knowing how to dance, because learning would require being seen not knowing.

And here's the other thing I found out, which made me feel a lot better about myself. This is not rare. There are actual learn-to-ride programmes for adults in London, in New York, all over. In the Netherlands, the most bicycle-obsessed country on this earth, there are bike schools for adults. It's common. It's just silent. Everybody who can't ride thinks they're the only one, which is exactly how a common thing stays invisible.

And there's a reason a class was never going to save me anyway. Riding a bicycle is actually the classic philosophical example of something called tacit knowledge. There's a philosopher, Michael Polanyi, who wrote that we can know more than we can tell, and the two examples people always use are recognising a face and riding a bike. Meaning: nobody can hand you this through words. Not a video, not a diagram, not a lecture. Your body has to work it out while somebody stands beside you. So I was going around looking for an explanation to a problem that cannot be explained. Those strangers in my DMs weren't a downgrade from a class. They were the correct technology.

Oh, and one more thing I learnt that I think is funny and also not funny. Somebody who teaches adults to ride said that beginners always try to force the bike to go straight, but the bike actually needs to wobble in order to balance. The wobble is not the failure of balance. The wobble is the balance. I'm going to stop there before I turn it into a whole sermon, but you saw it too.

So that's it. One week, one arbitrary deadline, one rejected class, one embarrassing Facebook post, and a bunch of kind strangers. And I can ride a bike now.

The deadline was random, by the way. There was no reason it had to be that week. But it worked exactly because it was random, because I did not sit around waiting to feel ready. Nobody feels ready to be publicly bad at something at my age. You just have to go and be bad at it in front of people, and then two days later you're not bad at it anymore.

## REFERENCES
- **Tacit knowledge** — Michael Polanyi, *The Tacit Dimension* (University of Chicago Press, 1966). Opens: "I shall reconsider human knowledge by starting from the fact that we can know more than we can tell." Riding a bicycle and recognising a face are the standard illustrations across the literature (and in David Autor's later "Polanyi's Paradox," 2014). Accurate as used.
- **Balance-first method for adults** — REI, CyclingSavvy, Cycling UK/Bikeability-style guides all converge: lower the saddle until both feet are flat on the ground, remove the pedals, scoot and glide, reinstall pedals once the learner can glide confidently for ~5–10 seconds. Most adults get the basics in a couple of hours.
- **Bike fit** — every adult learn-to-ride guide names "both feet flat on the ground while seated" as the single most important factor; a bike that's too big makes balancing scary and hard.
- **"The bike needs to wobble to balance"** — from an adult cycling instructor quoted in a London Cyclist piece on adults learning to ride. Used as an observation, not a physics claim.
- **Adult bike schools** — adult learn-to-ride programmes are well documented in the UK, US, and the Netherlands (where adult courses, often serving immigrant women, are long established).
