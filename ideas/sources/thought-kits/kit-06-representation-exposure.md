# KIT 06 — You Cannot Become What You Don't See

**Premise:** The first person who ever paid me a salary was a woman who owned the place and ran it with drive. I didn't know it was doing anything to me at the time. Representation isn't a feelings thing — it's a possibility thing: exposure sets the ceiling of your probability. Not about her; she's the evidence, the mechanism is the subject.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

The first person that hired me in my life that ever paid me a salary was a woman. Not my manager — she owned the establishment I was working with, and at the time I was pretty much a teenager. I was talking to a friend about incidents that happen in our lives that contribute to certain things, but in the moment they're happening, or even long after, you might not think they contributed to a part of you — but they did. We were talking about female empowerment and that woman just came to my mind immediately. I was young, but there was just so much drive in her. Representation matters. Intentional work toward empowerment is important, but representation is so important. You cannot become what you don't see. If you're not exposed to a possibility, it's so hard to become something you do not see.

*(Sharpened by her follow-ups: it revolves around exposure to possibilities — if you're not exposed to a possibility, there's little to zero probability you achieve it. And: don't make it about her; just working with her that young, seeing a woman run a thing and do it with drive, was enough.)*

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The spine, said twice:** you cannot become what you don't see. Lineage worth knowing (not for quoting — she arrived at it herself through the memory, which is stronger): most associated with **Marian Wright Edelman** (founder, Children's Defense Fund) — "it's hard to be what you can't see" — and **Sally Ride** used a version for women in STEM.

**The real subject underneath the subject.** Incidents shape who you become, but in the moment (and for years after) you don't know they're doing it. That's the quiet mechanism of representation — it doesn't announce itself. That woman wasn't trying to be anyone's representation; she was running her business, and teenage-her was just there absorbing something with no name for it. Representation isn't a lesson someone teaches — it's a possibility installed by proximity, silently, that only surfaces years later. **That's the essay:** not "representation matters" as a statement, but representation as a delayed-release thing you only understand looking backward.

**Exposure sets the probability (the cold, sharp version).** If you're never exposed to a possibility, the probability you achieve it is close to zero — not for lack of ability, but because you can't aim at what you don't know is reachable. Exposure is what writes options onto the list. No exposure, no option, no attempt, no outcome.

**Intentional empowerment vs representation.** Intentional = mentorship, scholarship, programme, panel — someone deliberately reaching to lift you. Vital. But representation is cheaper, quieter, more foundational, because it happens whether anyone intends it or not. That woman empowered her by accident, just by existing competently and visibly in a role of ownership. Every woman who simply does her thing in view of a girl is doing empowerment work she'll never be credited for.

**The unfair edge.** The biggest thing between many people and their potential isn't ability — it's *inventory*: the list of possibilities they were exposed to. Two equally capable kids, one saw women own businesses and one didn't; lives diverge, not by ability but by what got written onto the list of the achievable. So representation isn't charity or decoration — it's **redistribution of possibility**.

**Craft decisions:** her kept soft-focus, one repeated image only ("a woman who owned the place and did it with drive"), no invented/identifying detail. She's the evidence; the mechanism is the topic. Bookended on the spine line.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

The first person who ever hired me, the first person who ever paid me a salary in my life, was a woman.

And she wasn't my manager. She owned the place. This was her establishment, her thing, and I was, I think I was basically a teenager at the time. I wasn't thinking about any of this then. You don't, when you're that young. You're just there, doing the work, collecting your salary, going home.

I only started thinking about it recently because I was talking to a friend about female empowerment, and out of nowhere, that woman just came to my mind. Immediately. And it made me realise something about how we're actually formed, which is this. There are incidents that happen in our lives that contribute to who we become, but in the moment they're happening, and sometimes even long after they've happened, you don't recognise that they contributed anything. You don't feel the deposit going in. But it went in.

Because I was just working for her. That's all I thought was happening. But I was also watching a woman own a thing and run a thing and do it with so much drive. And I didn't sit there thinking "I am being empowered right now." Nobody announced it. She certainly wasn't trying to be anybody's representation, she was just trying to run her business. But something got installed in me in that place, quietly, that I would only understand years later.

And that's the thing I actually want to say about representation, because I feel like we've made it sound soft, like it's just about feeling nice, feeling seen, feeling proud. It's deeper than that. Representation is about possibility. It's about the list of things you even know are possible.

Let me put it plainly. If you are not exposed to a possibility, the probability that you achieve it is close to zero. Not because you're not capable. Because you cannot aim at something that is not on your list of things that exist. And the only way things get onto that list is exposure. You see it, so now it's possible. You don't see it, so it never even becomes an option you could reach for. You can't become what you don't see. You really cannot become what you don't see.

And when you understand it like that, you realise something almost unfair about life. The biggest thing standing between a lot of people and everything they could have been is not talent. It's inventory. It's just the list of possibilities they happened to be exposed to. Take two people, equally capable, equally hungry. One of them, somewhere along the line, saw a woman own the place. The other never did. And years later their lives look different, and it's not because one was more able. It's because one of them had that possibility written onto their list and the other one didn't. Same engine, different map.

So yes, the intentional work matters. The mentorship, the programmes, the scholarships, people deliberately reaching down to lift somebody up. Please, we need all of it. But do not underestimate the quieter thing. Do not underestimate what happens when a young person simply gets to see someone who looks like them doing the thing. Because that woman never mentored me. She never sat me down to empower me. She just existed, in front of me, doing her thing well. And that was enough to widen what I thought was possible for someone like me.

That's why representation is not decoration. It is redistribution. Every time somebody shows up and does their thing in a room where a young person can see them, they are handing out possibilities that person might never have generated on their own. They are writing new options onto somebody's list, and they will probably never even know they did it.

So if you are ever wondering whether it matters that you show up, whether it matters that you take up the space, that you do your thing visibly even when it's hard, let me tell you from the other side of it. There is a teenager somewhere, not thinking about you at all, just watching. And something is going in. You cannot become what you don't see. So be the thing somebody gets to see.
