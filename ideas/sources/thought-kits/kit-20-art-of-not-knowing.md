# KIT 20 — The Art of Not Knowing

**Premise:** We've lost the art of being okay with not knowing. The gap between realizing you don't know something and knowing it used to be long — and curiosity lived in that gap. Close it in five seconds and you never get to be curious, you just get to be informed. A thinking-out-loud piece, deliberately unresolved.

**Register:** thinking out loud, arriving at nothing sharp. Reflective, circling, "I don't know, I'm still thinking about it." NOT a debater's argument. Keep her tentativeness — it's the form.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

I feel like we have lost the art of not knowing — the art of just being okay with not knowing. I think about it in different directions: there's not knowing what's going on in the world (we have access to everything, you can know what happened everywhere today almost immediately), and there's being okay with not knowing in terms of knowledge — not just an event but just not knowing a thing. I think it's the same.

In reality the state of not knowing still exists — we don't all know everything — but in our conscious experience, not knowing has become a state we no longer stay in for long. If I don't know something and become conscious that I don't know it, it feels like the next thing to do is know it. Boom, search, done. The gap between realizing you don't know and knowing — that gap used to be long. It could be days. You'd find a book, go to the library, ask someone, talk to people, and you might just never find out. Now that gap has closed, and I think we've lost that gap. And not knowing is what powers curiosity. There's a difference between being curious about something and just retrieving knowledge about it. Curiosity needs that gap to exist. If you notice you're no longer as curious, I think it's because that gap no longer exists as much. Curiosity needs the question to stay open for a while. If you close it immediately you never really get to be curious, you just get to be informed.

We all now have an opinion about everything — every topic, every news story, every field — almost immediately. And it makes sense, because you can look something up in five, ten seconds and have a position. So there's no reason to say I don't know anymore, because not knowing is now technically a choice. People say, don't ask me stupid questions, ask Google. But there is more that I don't know than I do, and that's practically true for everyone. I'm not romanticizing not knowing — access to information is genuinely good, one of the best things we have. I don't want to go back to a time where a question sits unanswered for months because nobody around me knew. I've spent my whole life being someone who wants to know things; I'm not going to say ignorance is better. There's a version of not knowing that's just outright laziness. My point is that the prolonged state of not-knowing before you actually know — I feel like we've lost it, and I think it was doing something for us. It powers curiosity in a unique way. So I'm learning to be comfortable with just not knowing sometimes. Like, oh, I don't know. Tell me about it — I don't know.

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The core distinction is the whole essay: curiosity ≠ retrieval, and curiosity needs the question to stay open.** Curiosity isn't the desire to have an answer — it's the state of living inside an open question. It needs duration. Retrieval collapses the question the instant it appears. Close the gap immediately → never curious, just informed. They feel similar from outside; completely different experiences, and only one does anything to you.

**The deeper unlock: the gap was never empty.** The mistake is treating the not-knowing → knowing interval as dead waiting time tech mercifully deleted. But things happened in it: you carried the question, guessed, formed and revised theories, noticed related clues (an open question makes you scan the world), asked people and got their half-answers and stories. By the time the answer arrived days later, you'd built a structure around it — a room prepared for it to land in. That's why it stuck. Close the gap to five seconds and the answer arrives with nowhere to land, sits on the surface, slides off. **Instant answers are forgotten instantly** because you never built the room.

**The named idea (light):** Loewenstein's "information-gap" theory of curiosity — curiosity IS the feeling of a gap between what you know and what you want to know; the gap is the engine. No gap, no curiosity, by definition. Curiosity peaks when you know a little but not enough — mid-gap — exactly the state instant answers abolish. She reasoned her way to the actual mechanism.

**The opinions half.** Because not-knowing collapsed, everyone has an instant take on everything. Mechanism: we've confused having *access* to a position with having *earned* one. When forming an opinion took time (gathering, sitting in the gap, changing your mind), it had weight because you paid for it. Now: a fully-formed-sounding take on a war/case/country in the time to read one post. Feels like knowledge; it's borrowed — the shape of a conclusion without the process. Strong opinions, weak foundations; loud certainty on five seconds of retrieval; fragile because the gap where conviction forms got skipped.

**"I don't know" as a lost sentence.** Not-knowing is now a *choice*, so refusing to look something up feels like a moral failing ("ask Google"). "I don't know" stopped being an honest report and started feeling like laziness/stupidity. But there is more any of us doesn't know than we do — permanently true for every human ever. A culture that can't tolerate "I don't know" is at war with the basic condition of being a person; the shame is what drives people to the instant fake-answer instead of sitting honestly in the gap.

**Her honesty guard — already in her words, protect all of it.** Not romanticizing ignorance; spent her whole life wanting to know things; doesn't want questions sitting unanswered for months; access is one of the best things we have; there's a version of not-knowing that's pure laziness. This is what makes the piece trustworthy. The distinction is surgical: not against knowing — against the death of the gap. Lazy not-knowing = refusing to ever close the gap. Her practice = choosing, sometimes, not to close it *immediately*, on purpose, because the open question is doing something.

**Connections to prior kits (kept LIGHT in final, per her one-thought-stands-alone rule).** Same shape as the internet-capacity kit and the comparison kit — a tool offers to carry the very thing you were only strong from carrying. Also her boredom/refilling instinct (cycling with no headphones, filming things she'd normally pass) is the lived proof that leaving a gap open isn't misery. Did NOT foreground these; the piece stands alone.

**Faith turn (light, woven in).** "Iron sharpens iron" (Proverbs 27:17) — friction/resistance produces the edge; the open question is a kind of friction, and something gets sharpened in the rubbing. Broader: scripture treats struggle as what builds ("perseverance produces character," Romans 5), the inverse of the modern promise to escape all struggle. Used as one image, not a section.

**The landing — deliberately unresolved (her instruction).** Not a neat conclusion. She's learning to sit in "I don't know" on purpose, let the gap breathe before closing it, because the wondering was the good part. Ends open: "I don't know. I'm still thinking about it. And maybe that's the point." — the ending performs the thesis.

**Decisions:** thinking-out-loud register; faith woven in light; keep her exact hedges and phrasings; do NOT sharpen into a thesis.

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

I feel like we've lost the art of not knowing. The art of just being okay with not knowing. And I've been sitting with this for a while, turning it over, so let me just think out loud about it.

I mean it in a few directions. There's the obvious one, not knowing what's going on in the world. We have access to everything now. You can know what happened everywhere on earth today, almost immediately, all of it, before you've even finished your breakfast. But I also mean it in the sense of just, not knowing a thing. Not an event. Just a fact, a piece of knowledge, anything. And the more I think about it, the more I feel like it's the same thing underneath.

Because here's what I keep noticing. In reality, not knowing still exists. Obviously. None of us knows everything, that hasn't changed. But in our actual conscious experience, not knowing has become a state we no longer stay in for very long. Almost not at all. If I don't know something, and I become aware that I don't know it, the very next thing, immediately, is to close it. Boom. Search. Done. The not-knowing barely gets to exist before it's gone.

And I've been thinking about that gap specifically. The gap between realizing you don't know something and actually knowing it. That gap used to be long. It could be days. You'd have to find a book. Go to the library. Ask somebody, and they'd half-know, and they'd point you to somebody else. You might talk to three people about it. And sometimes, honestly, you would just never find out. You'd go from not knowing straight to, well, I guess I'll never know. The gap could stay open for a very long time, and sometimes it never closed at all.

And now that gap is basically zero. And I think, I think we've lost something that used to live inside that gap. And I'm still working out what it was, but I think it was curiosity. I think the gap is where curiosity actually lived.

Because there's a difference, right, between being curious about something and just retrieving it. Those are not the same thing. And I don't think we talk about that difference enough. Curiosity isn't the wanting of an answer. Curiosity is the state of having an open question. It's the wondering itself. The turning it over in your mind. And that state needs time. It needs the question to stay open long enough for you to actually sit inside it. But if you close the question the second it appears, you never get to be curious. You just get to be informed. And those feel similar, but they're not the same experience at all.

And I've started to think the gap was never actually empty. That's the thing. We treat that waiting time like it was just dead time that technology kindly deleted for us. But things were happening in there. While the question stayed open, you'd carry it around with you. You'd guess. You'd come up with a theory and then throw it out. You'd notice things you wouldn't have noticed otherwise, because an open question makes you sort of scan the world for pieces of the answer. And then when you finally found it, days later, it landed somewhere. It had a place to sit. You'd built the room for it while you waited, and that's why it stayed.

And I wonder if that's why the things I look up in five seconds don't stick at all. Because the answer arrives and there's no room built for it. Nowhere for it to land. So it just sits on the surface and slides right off. I look it up, and an hour later it's gone, and I look it up again. Because I never actually got curious about it. I just retrieved it.

There's apparently a whole idea in psychology about this, that curiosity is literally the feeling of a gap between what you know and what you want to know, and that the gap itself is the engine. Which, when I heard that, I was like, yeah. That's it. If there's no gap, there's no curiosity. By definition. So if you notice you're just not as curious about things as you used to be, I don't think there's anything wrong with you. I think it's that the gap doesn't get to exist anymore. Something's engine has nothing to run on.

And then there's the other side of it, which I've also been thinking about, which is opinions. Because since not-knowing collapsed, everybody now has a take on everything. Instantly. Every news story, every field, every country, every court case, everybody has a fully formed position within about ten seconds. And it makes sense, because you can look anything up in five, ten seconds and have a stance ready to go. So there's no reason to ever say I don't know anymore. Not knowing is now technically a choice. People will literally say it to you, don't ask me stupid questions, ask Google.

But here's what I keep coming back to. There is more that I don't know than there is that I do know. And that's not me being humble, that's just true. It's practically true for every single person alive. There has never been a human being for whom that wasn't true. So a world where we can't tolerate saying I don't know, where it feels like laziness or stupidity to admit it, that's a world kind of at war with just, the basic condition of being a person. Because not knowing most things is the normal state. It's everybody's normal state. We've just made it feel shameful.

And let me be clear, because I don't want to be misunderstood here. I am not romanticizing not knowing. I'm really not. I have spent my whole life being someone who wants to know things. Access to information is genuinely one of the best things we have, one of the best things about being alive right now, and I am not going to sit here and pretend ignorance is some beautiful lost paradise. I do not want to go back to a time where a question just sits unanswered for months because nobody around me happened to know the answer. That was not better. And there's absolutely a version of not knowing that's just pure laziness, just refusing to ever find out, and I'm not talking about that either.

What I'm talking about is narrower than that. It's that prolonged state of not-knowing, the one that used to come before you finally knew, that stretch in the middle, I think we've lost that specific thing, and I think it was quietly doing something for us. It was feeding curiosity in a way I don't think anything else does.

There's that old line, iron sharpens iron. And it's friction that does it. The grind of one thing against another, the resistance, that's what makes the edge. And I think the open question was a kind of friction. It rubbed against you while it stayed open, and something got sharpened in the rubbing. Close the gap instantly and there's no friction, and I don't know if anything gets sharpened at all.

So honestly, where I've landed, and I don't think this is a neat conclusion, it's more just a thing I'm trying, is that I'm learning to be comfortable with not knowing sometimes. On purpose. Just, oh, I don't know. And leaving it there for a bit. Not forever. I'll still look it up eventually, I'm not going to pretend I won't. But letting the gap stay open a little longer before I rush to close it. Letting myself actually wonder about something before I let the answer end the wondering.

Because I have a feeling that the wondering was the good part. And we've been so focused on getting the answer faster that nobody stopped to ask what we were rushing through to get there.

I don't know. I'm still thinking about it. And maybe that's kind of the point.
