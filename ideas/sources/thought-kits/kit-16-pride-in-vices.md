# KIT 16 — They Handed You a Leash and Called It Armour

**Premise:** "Be capable of violence, just keep it in reserve" fails, because a vice is an appetite, not a tool — and pride is the ingredient that turns a capacity into a disposition. The tell is that these people flip out when it isn't needed. The goal was never to be capable of violence; it was to almost never need it.

---

## 1. THE ORIGINAL (what I said first)

When we tell people to take pride in certain vices to protect themselves, we think we are doing good. You hear things like, oh, you should be violent, you should be capable of violence, you should be wicked. It's a very interesting perspective to me, because you cannot consistently tell someone to have a vice and then defend it with selective usage. These vices are not things that you can just have — they tend to want to propel themselves.

You tell someone, be capable of violence because you need to do this, you need to do that — no, not because you want to be a bad person, you just need to be capable of it. But you sprinkle in a little bit of pride in that vice, and in many cases it ends up not being something they reserve for certain occasions. It's something they are very quick to flip out with, even in most scenarios that do not require it. I tend to believe that only very few scenarios in life actually require it.

---

## 2. THE EXPLORATION (before the final)

**The core insight, sharpened.** People think "be capable of violence, use only when needed" installs a tool you keep in a drawer. But a vice is not a tool — it's an appetite, and appetites don't wait, they look for reasons to be fed. Attach *pride* to a capacity and it stops sitting quietly and starts hunting for occasions, because now using it feels good, feels like identity, feels like proof. The "reserve it for emergencies" plan fails because pride — the emotional setup they gave — is the exact thing that makes them reach for it when there's no emergency.

**The unlock: capacity vs disposition.** A capacity sits still until a genuine situation calls it. A disposition *leans* — tilts you, pattern-matches the world into occasions for itself. People think they're teaching capacity, but you can't teach capacity through pride, because pride is what converts capacity into disposition. The proud fighter doesn't own a tool, he owns a lean — and a man leaning toward violence starts seeing threats a calmer person wouldn't, because some part of him needs the threat to exist so the identity has somewhere to go.

**Aristotle gives it gravity.** Virtue is the mean between extremes; courage sits between cowardice and recklessness — the person here is coached toward recklessness and told it's courage. And "you become what you repeatedly do": character is hardened habit. You don't rehearse aggression and end up gentle. So "be capable of violence" delivered through pride is a training program for *becoming* violent, dressed as a safety course.

**The tell = flipping out when it isn't needed.** If truly held in reserve, you'd almost never see it. You see it constantly (traffic, arguments, small disrespect, nothing) — so it was never in reserve; it's a hair trigger looking for a reason. **Darkest mechanism:** the proud person has an *incentive to escalate*, because de-escalation robs them of the rehearsed moment. So they escalate what a calm person would dissolve — the capacity manufactures the emergency it claimed to protect against.

**Honesty guard (must stay, or it's naive).** A few scenarios genuinely require it — self-defence, protecting a child, real danger. Not pacifism. But the people who handle those rare moments best are usually *not* the ones proud of their capacity — it's the calm one with nothing to prove, because for them it's a capacity not an identity, so it stays in the drawer. The loud/proud one has to keep withdrawing it to remind everyone (and himself) it's there — the pride forces the usage.

**Why pride specifically corrupts it.** Pride needs an audience and repetition — it wants to be witnessed and confirmed. So a vice you're proud of keeps seeking display. "Have it but stay humble about it" is nearly impossible: the pride you sprinkled in is exactly the ingredient that won't stay humble. Not a safety — a craving with a justification attached.

**Faith turn (light, one move, serves the thesis).** "Turn the other cheek" misread as weakness. Stronger reading: the one who absorbs an offence without flipping out isn't owned by the provocation. The proud-of-violence person can be *operated* by anyone who knows which button to press — insult him, he must respond, no choice, identity requires it. A puppet with predictable strings. Biblical meekness = strength under control, power that doesn't need to prove itself; the person who doesn't need the capacity for self-worth is the only one who can't be puppeted by it.

**The landing.** The goal was never to be someone capable of violence — it was to be someone who almost never needs it and, on the rare true occasion, can. You get there by refusing to build identity on it, so it stays a capacity and never curdles into a disposition. They think they're handing you armour; they're handing you a leash and keeping the other end.

**Decisions:** faith thread IN; measured/reflective tone with edge where it earns it.

---

## 3. THE FINAL (spoken, in my voice)

When we tell people to take pride in certain vices so they can protect themselves, we think we're doing them good.

You hear it all the time. You should be capable of violence. You should be a little bit wicked. You need to be able to be cruel when the situation calls for it. And it always comes with the same reassurance attached: not because you want to be a bad person, just so you have it, just so nobody can mess with you. Keep it in your back pocket. Only bring it out when you need it.

And I find that perspective very interesting, because I don't think it works the way people think it works.

Here's the problem. You cannot consistently tell someone to take pride in a vice and then expect them to only use it selectively. Because a vice is not a tool. A tool sits in a drawer and waits. A vice is an appetite, and appetites do not wait. They look for reasons to be fed. So the moment you attach pride to a capacity, that capacity stops sitting quietly and starts hunting for occasions, because now using it feels good. It feels like identity. It feels like proof.

And that's the mistake buried in the whole thing. People think they're teaching a capacity. Being capable of violence. But you cannot teach a capacity through pride, because pride is the exact thing that turns a capacity into a disposition. And those are two different things. A capacity sits still until a real situation calls for it. A disposition leans. It tilts you. It makes you start reading the world as a series of occasions for the thing you're proud of.

So the proud fighter doesn't actually own a tool. He owns a lean. And a man leaning toward violence will start seeing threats that a calmer person simply would not see, because some part of him needs the threat to be real, so that the identity has somewhere to go.

There's an old idea from Aristotle that fits this perfectly. He said you become what you repeatedly do. Your character is just your habits, hardened. So you don't get to rehearse being aggressive and somehow end up gentle. Whatever you practise is what you become. Which means "be capable of violence," when you deliver it with pride, is not a safety course. It's a training program for becoming a violent person, wearing the costume of a safety course.

And you can see the proof, because it gives itself away. If this capacity were truly held in reserve for the rare genuine emergency, you would almost never see it. It would be invisible for years at a time. But that's not what happens. You see these people flip out in traffic. You see them escalate an argument that was already ending. You see them ready to fight over being looked at wrong, over small disrespect, over nothing. And that tells you everything. It was never in reserve. It's a hair trigger walking around looking for a reason to be pulled.

And here's the darkest part of the mechanism. A person who is proud of their capacity for violence actually has an incentive for things to escalate. Because de-escalation robs them of the moment. It takes away the scene they've been rehearsing in their head. So they escalate the very situations that a calm person would have quietly dissolved. Do you see what that means? The capacity ends up manufacturing the emergency it claimed to be protecting you from. You didn't get armour. You got a machine that generates the exact danger it was supposed to guard against.

Now let me be fair, because I'm not naive about this. There are real scenarios in life that genuinely require it. Self-defence is real. Protecting your child is real. Actual danger exists, and the ability to meet it is not a bad thing. I'm not preaching that everyone should be soft and defenceless. That's not the point at all.

The point is this. The people who actually handle those rare real moments the best are almost never the ones walking around proud of their capacity. It's the opposite. It's the calm one. The one with nothing to prove. The one you'd never suspect. Because for them it genuinely is a capacity and not an identity, so it stays in the drawer, because they don't need you to know they have it. The loud one, the proud one, has to keep taking it out and showing it, to remind everyone, and honestly to remind himself, that it's still there. The very act of being proud of it forces him to keep using it.

And there's something in scripture that I think gets misread on exactly this point. Turn the other cheek. People read that as weakness. But look at it again with everything we just said. The person who can absorb an insult without flipping out is not the weak one in that scene. He's the powerful one, because he is not owned by the provocation. Think about the alternative. The man who is proud of his capacity for violence can be operated by anybody who knows which button to press. Insult him and he has to respond. He has no choice. His identity requires it. That's not strength, that's a puppet with very predictable strings. You know exactly how to move him.

Meekness in the Bible was never weakness. It was strength under control. Power that does not need to prove itself. And that's the freedom the proud person will never have, because the person who doesn't need the capacity for their self-worth is the only one who can't be puppeted by it.

So here's where I land. The goal was never to become someone capable of violence. The goal was to become someone who almost never needs it, and who, on the rare true occasion that it's actually required, can. And you do not get there by taking pride in the vice. You get there by refusing to build your identity on it, so that it stays a capacity, sitting in the drawer, and never curdles into a disposition that runs your life.

So when people tell you to be proud of your wickedness, to wear your capacity for cruelty like a badge, understand what is actually being handed to you. They think they're giving you armour. They're really giving you a leash. And they're keeping the other end, for whoever figures out how to pull it.

## REFERENCES
- **Aristotle** — *Nicomachean Ethics*. Two ideas used: (1) virtue as the mean between extremes, with courage between cowardice and recklessness; (2) character formed by habituation — "we become just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts." Both represented accurately, paraphrased.
- **"Turn the other cheek"** — Matthew 5:39. Reframed (not as passivity but as refusing to be controlled by provocation) — a defensible, widely-held reading, presented as interpretation ("I think it gets misread"), not as the only meaning.
- **Meekness = "strength under control"** — a common description of the Greek *praotes* (the bridled-horse image is the traditional illustration). Presented as interpretation, accurate to the common understanding.
- No empirical/statistical claims — this is a philosophical/ethical argument, so nothing to fact-check beyond the sources above.
