**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT CONTENT MODERATORS DO AND WHY IT HAPPENED IN NAIROBI]**

All the Kenya cases against Meta start with a job most people have never heard of. It's called content moderation.

When someone reports a post on Facebook, or a system flags it, very often a human being has to look at it and decide if it breaks the rules.

That means looking at the worst things people upload, every day, as your job.

That can be violent videos, abuse, or people hurting themselves.

Meta doesn't do most of this work with its own staff. It pays outsourcing companies to do it.

In Nairobi, one of those companies was Sama, an American outsourcing company with a big office in Kenya.

Sama's Nairobi office moderated Facebook content for the region until early 2023.

In February 2022, TIME magazine published an investigation by Billy Perrigo called "Inside Facebook's African Sweatshop."

It reported that some of the moderators were paid as little as $1.50 an hour.

**[ON SCREEN: TIME, Feb 2022 / "as little as $1.50 per hour"]**

Sama said it paid well above Kenya's minimum wage, and it said it was "completely inaccurate" to suggest people were hired under false pretenses.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the $1.50 figure and the person at the centre of it are the reason to stay; the next line introduces the man who started the first case]**

One of the people in that story was Daniel Motaung, a moderator from South Africa.

He had tried to organise his colleagues to push for better pay and conditions, and he was fired in 2019.

Sama denies that it fired him for organising.

And in May 2022, he went to court.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: CASE ONE, DANIEL MOTAUNG VS SAMA AND META]**

The first Kenyan case against Meta was filed in May 2022 by Daniel Motaung, a former Facebook content moderator from South Africa.

He sued Sama and Meta at the Employment and Labour Relations Court in Nairobi.

His case includes claims of human trafficking, because he says people were recruited without being told clearly what the job was.

It also says he was fired for trying to form a union, and that the work gave him post-traumatic stress disorder, which is PTSD, without enough mental health support.

These are his claims, and the court hasn't decided them yet.

Meta's first answer was that it shouldn't be in the case at all.

It argued that it's a foreign company that doesn't operate in Kenya, and that Sama employed him, not Meta.

In February 2023, the judge disagreed and ruled that Meta was a "proper party" to the case. That means Meta has to answer the claims, alongside Sama.

**[ON SCREEN: Feb 2023 / Meta is a "proper party" to the case]**

Amnesty International said that meant Meta would be "significantly subjected to a court of law in the global south" for the first time.

In February 2026, the court was supposed to give rulings in his case and in the moderators' case, and it postponed them.

As of October 2026, his case still hasn't been decided.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: CASE TWO, THE 185 FACEBOOK MODERATORS WHO LOST THEIR JOBS]**

The biggest Kenyan case against Meta is about the Facebook content moderators who lost their jobs in 2023.

In January 2023, Sama announced it was leaving content moderation work, and around 200 moderators in Nairobi lost their jobs.

Meta moved the work to another outsourcing company, called Majorel.

In March 2023, 43 of the moderators went to court, and the group later grew to about 185.

Their lawyer is Mercy Mutemi, a Kenyan lawyer who now runs a group called The Oversight Lab Africa.

They say the layoffs were unlawful, and they say Meta told Majorel not to hire them, which they call blacklisting.

Again, those are their claims, and they haven't been tested at trial.

In 2023, the labour court ordered Sama to keep paying the moderators while the case went on, and ordered Meta to provide "proper medical, psychiatric and psychological care."

The Court of Appeal later set aside the part about working conditions, because it said the judge had made final orders at an early stage of the case, when he shouldn't have.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And then the case went all the way to the Court of Appeal, on the question of whether a Kenyan court can judge Meta at all.]**

On 20 September 2024, three judges of Kenya's Court of Appeal threw out Meta's appeal, and said the case can be heard in Kenya.

**[ON SCREEN: Court of Appeal, 20 Sept 2024 / Meta can be sued in Kenya]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the Court of Appeal ruling lands here, and the next segment explains why it's such a big deal]**

In December 2024, the moderators' lawyers filed medical reports with the court.

Dr Ian Kanyanya, the head of mental health services at Kenyatta National Hospital, assessed 144 of the former moderators, and 81% of them were classed as having severe PTSD.

Meta told CNN it takes the support of moderators seriously, and that its contracts with outsourcing companies set expectations on counselling, training and fair pay.

It didn't comment on the medical reports, because the case is still going on.

Since then, the case has moved slowly.

In April 2026, the court ordered a Meta director, Laura O'Hanlon, to come and be cross-examined about a sworn statement she gave for Meta.

That same month, one of the moderators told Capital FM, "To date, we have not been receiving any mental health care."

As of October 2026, the main case still hasn't been decided.

**[SEGMENT 5: CAN YOU SUE META IN KENYA?]**

The biggest question in all the Kenya cases is whether a Kenyan court can judge Meta at all.

Lawyers call this jurisdiction. It means a court's power to hear a case.

Meta's answer has been no, again and again.

In the moderators' case, Meta argued that "There is no permissive law that allows this court to exercise jurisdiction" outside Kenya's borders.

It says it's registered in the United States and Ireland, it doesn't have staff in Kenya, and the moderators worked for Sama.

So let me give you an example to make it simple.

Imagine an estate in Lagos hires a security company to guard the gate.

The guards wear the security company's uniform, and the security company pays them.

But the estate decides their shifts, gives them the list of who to let in, checks their work every day, and one day tells the security company it doesn't need them anymore.

If those guards are treated badly, can they sue only the security company, or the estate too?

That's basically the question Kenyan courts have been answering about Meta and Sama.

**[ON SCREEN: the estate = Meta / the security company = Sama / the guards = the moderators]**

So far, Kenyan courts have said Meta can be part of these cases.

In 2023, the labour court treated Meta as the moderators' main employer for that stage of the case, partly because they worked on Meta's own systems.

In 2024, the Court of Appeal said the labour court was right to step in, because the layoffs were happening in Kenya and affecting people in Kenya.

One thing to be clear about, though.

These rulings only decide that the cases can be heard in Kenya.

Who is right, and whether Meta owes anybody anything, gets decided later, at trial.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: CASE THREE, THE ETHIOPIA HATE SPEECH CASE AGAINST META]**

The third Kenyan case against Meta is about Facebook's algorithm and the war in northern Ethiopia.

Abrham Meareg's father, Professor Meareg Amare Abrha, was a chemistry professor at Bahir Dar University in Ethiopia.

In 2021, during the war, Facebook posts shared his name and his photo, with false claims linking him to a rebel group, because he was Tigrayan.

Weeks later, he was killed by gunmen outside his home.

His son says he asked Facebook several times to take the posts down, and NPR reported that some were still up when he filed the case.

The second person bringing the case is Fisseha Tekle, a legal adviser at Amnesty International, who says his reports on the violence made him a target of online abuse.

The third is the Katiba Institute, a Kenyan rights organisation.

They filed in December 2022, at the High Court in Nairobi, because they say content from Ethiopia was being moderated from Meta's hub in Nairobi.

They're asking the court to make Meta change its algorithm so it stops promoting hate, hire enough moderators who speak the local languages, and set up a fund for victims.

When they filed, that fund was reported as about 1.6 billion US dollars.

**[ON SCREEN: what they're asking for / change the algorithm / more moderators who speak local languages / a fund for victims]**

Meta told NPR that "Hate speech and incitement to violence are against these rules, and we invest heavily in teams and technology" to find and remove that content.

Meta also argued that a Kenyan court can't hear this case, because it's governed by American law, and Facebook's terms say disputes go to US courts.

On 3 April 2025, Justice L. N. Mugambi of the High Court rejected that.

He wrote, "The issues raised are substantial and transcend the interests of the parties involved in the Petition."

And he sent the case to the Chief Justice to set up a bench of judges to hear it.

Abrham said afterwards, "Mark Zuckerberg may imagine that justice begins and ends at the US border."

**[~6:00 BEAT: the Zuckerberg quote is the high point; the next segment is the 2026 story most viewers will have half-heard about]**

Meta appealed that ruling.

Foxglove, a UK group that supports the case, says there was a hearing in Nairobi on 16 July 2026, and it expects a result in the coming months.

As of October 2026, I couldn't find a decision.

**[SEGMENT 7: META'S SMART GLASSES, SAMA, AND KENYA'S DATA REGULATOR]**

In 2026, Meta and Kenya were in the news again, and this time it was about Meta's smart glasses.

Ray-Ban Meta glasses have a camera and Meta's AI assistant built in.

In late February 2026, two Swedish newspapers, Svenska Dagbladet and Göteborgs-Posten, published interviews with workers at Sama in Nairobi who reviewed footage from the glasses.

The workers said that footage included people undressing, using the toilet and having sex.

A Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch that photos and videos stay on the user's device unless the user shares them.

He said that when content is shared with Meta AI, Meta "sometimes" uses contractors to review it, and that it filters the data to protect people's privacy.

In March, a Nairobi digital rights group called The Oversight Lab asked Kenya's Office of the Data Protection Commissioner, the ODPC, to investigate.

On 31 March 2026, the ODPC said it had opened an investigation into whether this follows Kenya's Data Protection Act.

**[ON SCREEN: ODPC investigation opened 31 March 2026]**

Then Meta ended its contract with Sama.

Meta said Sama "don't meet our standards." Sama said it was "surprised and disappointed."

On 16 April 2026, Sama gave redundancy notices to 1,108 workers in Nairobi.

So this investigation is the one part of the story where Kenya, the state, is looking into Meta.

As of October 2026, I couldn't find any published findings.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: WHERE THE KENYAN GOVERNMENT STANDS ON META]**

If you only read the headline, you might think the Kenyan government is leading the fight against Meta. The real picture is more mixed.

Kenya wants outsourcing jobs, and President William Ruto has talked about making Kenya an outsourcing hub like India or the Philippines.

In December 2024, at a town hall in Nairobi, Ruto said Sama had come to him about its legal troubles.

And he said, "Now I can report to you that we have changed the law, so nobody will take you to court again on any matter."

**[ON SCREEN: President Ruto, Dec 2024 / "nobody will take you to court again on any matter"]**

TIME looked at the bill he meant and reported that its text would keep clients like Meta from being pulled into lawsuits against their Kenyan contractors.

Meta told TIME, "Meta has not advocated for changes to these laws."

Tech workers went to court to stop that bill, and in June 2026 the High Court declined to step in.

On 19 August 2026, Kenya's National Assembly passed the outsourcing changes.

According to an investigation by The Continent and Business Day published in September, they were waiting for the president's signature, and the report didn't give the final wording, so I'm not going to guess what it says.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: Now put that next to the smart glasses investigation.]**

So through its data regulator, the government is investigating Meta, and through Parliament, it's changing the rules in a way that could protect outsourcing companies and their clients.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the turn to Nigeria starts next, which is the payoff for a lot of this audience]**

---

**[SEGMENT 9: WHAT KENYA VS META MEANS FOR NIGERIA AND AFRICA]**

So what does Kenya versus Meta mean for Nigeria and the rest of Africa?

First, the work moves.

After the Kenya layoffs, Foxglove said, drawing on reporting by The Guardian and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, that Meta's moderation for the region had moved to Accra in Ghana, through Majorel.

So whatever Kenyan courts decide about who is responsible could matter to workers in Ghana, or in Nigeria if this kind of work comes here.

A Kenyan ruling doesn't bind a Nigerian court.

But Nigeria and Kenya both use the common law system, and courts in common law countries sometimes look at each other's decisions when they're deciding something new.

Second, Nigeria has mostly gone after Meta through regulators.

In 2024, Nigeria's consumer protection commission, the FCCPC, fined Meta 220 million dollars over WhatsApp's data practices, and a tribunal upheld that fine in April 2025.

And in 2025, Nigeria's data protection commission ordered Meta to pay 32.8 million dollars, and later that year it settled with Meta out of court, which Premium Times reported set those orders aside.

So that's two different tools. In Kenya, it's mostly people taking Meta to court. In Nigeria, it's mostly regulators, and both take years.

I think if a company sets the rules of the job, the work happens on its systems, and it decides when the job ends, it should share responsibility for the people doing that job, whatever name is on their payslip.

The strongest argument on the other side is jobs.

If companies like Meta can be sued in Kenya over their contractors' workers, some might move the work to countries where they can't be, and Ruto said Sama had been planning to move to Uganda.

Meta has also said it requires its partners to provide "industry-leading pay, benefits and support."

And none of the workers' or victims' claims has been proven at trial yet.

So tell me in the comments, who do you think should be responsible for these workers, the outsourcing company, Meta, or both?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Kenya vs Meta: Who Is Responsible for the People Who Clean Up Facebook?

### Subtitle:
> A plain guide to every Kenyan case against Meta as of October 2026, what Meta says, and what it could mean for Nigeria.

### SEO Title:
> Kenya vs Meta Explained: Every Case and What It Means for Africa

### SEO Description:
> A plain explanation of the Kenyan court cases against Meta, from the Facebook content moderators to the Ethiopia hate speech case and the 2026 smart glasses investigation, with Meta's position and what it means for Nigeria.

---

I saw a short TikTok about Kenya suing Meta, and I didn't even know the gist of it. So I read the court rulings and the reporting, and I found that there isn't one case. As of October 2026 there are three big cases against Meta in Kenyan courts, plus an investigation by Kenya's data regulator, and most of the cases were brought by former workers and victims rather than by the government.

All of them start with content moderation. When a post on Facebook gets reported or flagged, very often a person has to look at it and decide whether it breaks the rules. Meta pays outsourcing companies to do much of this work, and in Nairobi one of those companies was Sama. In February 2022, TIME published Billy Perrigo's investigation "Inside Facebook's African Sweatshop," which reported that some moderators were paid as little as $1.50 an hour. Sama said it paid well above Kenya's minimum wage and called the suggestion that people were hired under false pretenses "completely inaccurate." One of the people in that story was Daniel Motaung, a moderator from South Africa who had tried to organise his colleagues and was fired in 2019. Sama denies he was fired for organising. In May 2022 he sued Sama and Meta in Nairobi's Employment and Labour Relations Court, with claims that include human trafficking, union busting and the trauma of the work. Meta argued it should not be in the case at all, and in February 2023 the judge ruled that Meta was a "proper party."

The second case is larger. In January 2023 Sama left content moderation, around 200 moderators lost their jobs, and Meta moved the work to another outsourcer, Majorel. Forty-three moderators went to court in March 2023, and the group later grew to about 185. They say the layoffs were unlawful and that they were blacklisted from the new contractor. Mediation led by former Chief Justice Willy Mutunga collapsed in October 2023, and on 20 September 2024 Kenya's Court of Appeal rejected Meta's appeal and said the case can be heard in Kenya. In December 2024, Dr Ian Kanyanya of Kenyatta National Hospital reported that 81% of 144 former moderators he assessed had severe PTSD. Meta told CNN it takes the support of moderators seriously and that its contracts set expectations on counselling, training and fair pay. In April 2026 the court ordered a Meta director, Laura O'Hanlon, to be cross-examined on a sworn statement she gave, and as of October 2026 the case has not been decided.

The question underneath both cases is jurisdiction, which is a court's power to hear a case. Meta has argued that it is registered in the United States and Ireland, has no staff in Kenya, and did not employ the moderators. The way I explain it to myself is with an estate in Lagos that hires a security company. The security company pays the guards, but the estate sets their shifts, tells them who to let in and decides when it no longer needs them. If the guards are mistreated, the question is whether they can sue the estate as well. So far Kenyan courts have said Meta can be part of these cases, partly because of how much control it had over the work and because the layoffs happened in Kenya. Those rulings only decide that the cases can be heard. Who is right is still to be decided at trial.

The third case is about Facebook's algorithm and the war in Ethiopia. Abrham Meareg's father, Professor Meareg Amare Abrha, taught chemistry at Bahir Dar University. In 2021 Facebook posts shared his name and photo with false claims about him, and weeks later he was killed by gunmen outside his home. Abrham, Amnesty International legal adviser Fisseha Tekle and the Katiba Institute filed in Nairobi's High Court in December 2022, asking for changes to the algorithm, more moderators who speak local languages, and a victims' fund reported at the time as about $1.6 billion. Meta told NPR that hate speech and incitement are against its rules and that it invests heavily in teams and technology to remove them. On 3 April 2025, Justice L. N. Mugambi rejected Meta's argument that the case belongs in American courts and wrote that "the issues raised are substantial and transcend the interests of the parties involved in the Petition." Meta appealed, and Foxglove, which supports the case, says a hearing took place in Nairobi on 16 July 2026.

In 2026 the story moved to Meta's smart glasses. In late February, the Swedish newspapers Svenska Dagbladet and Göteborgs-Posten published interviews with Sama workers in Nairobi who said they had reviewed footage from Ray-Ban Meta glasses that included people undressing, using the toilet and having sex. Meta said media stays on the device unless the user shares it, and that when content is shared with Meta AI it sometimes uses contractors to review it and filters the data to protect privacy. Kenya's Office of the Data Protection Commissioner opened an investigation on 31 March 2026. Meta then ended its contract with Sama, saying Sama did not "meet our standards," and on 16 April Sama gave redundancy notices to 1,108 workers.

The Kenyan government's role is more mixed than the headline suggests. In December 2024 President William Ruto said Sama had come to him about its legal troubles and that "nobody will take you to court again on any matter." TIME reported that the bill he meant would keep clients like Meta from being pulled into lawsuits against their Kenyan contractors, and Meta told TIME it "has not advocated for changes to these laws." According to The Continent and Business Day, the National Assembly passed the outsourcing changes on 19 August 2026 and they were awaiting the president's signature in September. In June 2026 the ICT minister also told X, TikTok and Meta to open physical offices in Kenya.

For Nigeria, the comparison is useful. Nigeria has mostly dealt with Meta through regulators. The FCCPC fined Meta $220 million over WhatsApp's data practices, and a tribunal upheld the fine in April 2025, while the data protection commission's $32.8 million case ended in a settlement that Premium Times reported set the original orders aside. A Kenyan ruling would not bind a Nigerian court, but both countries use the common law, and courts in common law countries sometimes look at each other's reasoning. I think that if a company sets the rules of a job, the work happens on its systems, and it decides when the job ends, it should share responsibility for the people doing it. The strongest argument against that is jobs, since a company that can be sued over its contractors' workers may move the work elsewhere, and none of these claims has been proven at trial. I'd like to know where you land on it.
