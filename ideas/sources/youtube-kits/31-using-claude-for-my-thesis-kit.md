**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT CLAUDE SCIENCE IS]**

So what is Claude Science?

Claude Science is a desktop app from Anthropic for doing research, and Anthropic calls it an AI workbench for scientists.

It came out on the 30th of June 2026, and as of October 2026 it's still in beta, which means it's still being tested and changed.

You install it on your own computer, separately from the normal Claude app, and it works on Mac, Windows and Linux.

Then you describe a research task in plain language, and Claude writes code and runs it on your computer to do the work.

For example, you could say, "Here's a folder with my survey results. Clean them up and show me how the answers differ across the three groups."

**[ON SCREEN: "Here's a folder with my survey results. Clean them up and show me how the answers differ across the three groups."]**

Claude writes the Python or R code for that, runs it in a sandbox, and gives you back the table and the chart.

A sandbox is a closed-off space where the code can run without touching anything you haven't allowed.

And Claude has to ask you before it opens a new folder or connects to a new website.

Everything it makes, like a figure, a table or a notebook, gets saved with its history.

So you can see the code that made it, and the conversation that led to it.

Think of it like a lab notebook that writes itself while you work.

If your supervisor asks you where a number came from, the answer is right there.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the next point is the reviewer and the Pro setting, the most useful tip in the video]**

It also connects to scientific databases.

Most of those are for the life sciences, like genes, proteins and chemistry.

But some work for any field, like the one that searches research papers through OpenAlex and arXiv, and there's a literature review skill that helps Claude find papers, check them and pull them together.

Claude Science comes with the paid Claude plans, which are Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise, and it isn't on the free plan.

As of October 2026, Pro is 20 dollars a month in the US, and the price can be different where you live.

And it has a feature that checks Claude's own work, which I'll show you in a minute, along with a setting you should change if you're on Pro.

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT CLAUDE SCIENCE CAN DO FOR A THESIS]**

So what can Claude Science actually do for a master's thesis?

My thesis is for my master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University, and it's on the invisible cognitive cost of switching between productivity tools.

For a thesis like mine, these are the parts I think matter most.

The first one is finding papers.

It can search OpenAlex, which is a big free catalogue of research papers, and arXiv, where a lot of computer science papers go first.

The second one is reading them.

When a paper is open access, which means it's free to read, Claude can get the full text by itself.

For papers behind a paywall, you can add your library's login details in the app, and then it can reach what your university already pays for, and nothing more.

The third one is working with your own data.

If you have survey answers or measurements, it can clean them, run the analysis and make the figures, and keep the code that did it.

And the fourth one is writing.

You can draft in it, and it shows you a preview of Markdown and LaTeX, which a lot of theses are written in.

Anthropic describes Claude Science as an app that "integrates the tools and packages that researchers most commonly use."

And since my thesis is literally about switching between tools, I notice that.

Research can't tell me yet whether one app doing everything makes the switching cheaper for your brain, and that's the kind of question my field asks.

**[SEGMENT 4: THE CLAUDE SCIENCE REVIEWER, AND THE SETTING TO TURN ON]**

The feature in Claude Science I'd point students to first is the reviewer.

The reviewer is a second check that runs after Claude answers.

It reads back what Claude said, the plan you approved, the files it saved, and the record of the code that actually ran, and then it checks if they all match.

Anthropic's docs give some examples of what it catches.

A result reported as computed when nothing ran.

A citation that doesn't support the claim it's attached to.

And a reference whose DOI leads to a different article.

**[ON SCREEN: The reviewer can flag: a result reported when nothing ran / a citation that doesn't support the claim / a DOI that leads to a different article / a plan step that wasn't done]**

A DOI is like a permanent web address for a research paper.

So if the address in the reference opens a different paper, something is wrong with that reference.

This matters a lot for students, because AI tools have made up references before, and they've cited real papers for things those papers never said.

When the reviewer finds something, a small card shows up under the message, and Claude has to either fix the problem or explain why it doesn't apply.

Now the setting.

On the Max, Team and Enterprise plans, the automatic reviewer is on by default.

On the Pro plan, it starts off, and you turn it on for each session.

**[ON SCREEN: Auto-review: on by default on Max, Team, Enterprise / OFF by default on Pro, turn it on per session]**

So if you're on Pro, turn it on.

Reviews also use your plan's usage, so they count towards your limits.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer now has the one setting to change; the next segment promises the honest limits]**

**[SEGMENT 5: WHERE CLAUDE SCIENCE CAN GO WRONG]**

Claude Science can still get things wrong, and Anthropic says so in its own docs.

Their exact words are, "The reviewer reduces, but doesn't eliminate, errors."

And they tell you to verify results before you rely on them in research or publication.

So I want to go through the limits I'd want a friend to know about.

The first one is that the reviewer checks whether Claude's claims match what ran, and that's all.

It doesn't re-run the analysis, and it doesn't judge whether the method was the right choice for your research question.

For example, say you compare two groups with the wrong statistical test.

The reviewer can confirm the test really ran and the numbers in the answer match the output.

It won't tell you that a different test was the right one.

That part is still your job, and your supervisor's.

The second one is data from real people.

Your files stay on your computer, but the parts Claude reads are sent to the model as part of the conversation.

So if your thesis has interview recordings, survey answers or anything else from participants, check what your ethics approval and consent forms allow before any AI tool reads it.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And the next two are the ones you only find out after you've started.]**

The third one is usage limits.

Claude Science shares the same limits as the rest of your Claude plan, including normal chats.

On Pro and Max, there's a limit that resets every five hours and a weekly one, and when you hit them, a long analysis pauses until the reset, unless you pay for extra usage.

The fourth one is that it's built mostly for the life sciences.

Most of the ready-made database connections are for biology and chemistry, so if you're in HCI or the social sciences like me, you'll use fewer of them.

Anthropic's docs do say it supports the social sciences, computer science and other fields too.

And the last one is that it's in beta, so things can change or break while you're mid-thesis.

**[SEGMENT 6: CLAUDE SCIENCE VS ELICIT]**

So how does Claude Science compare with Elicit, the tool my supervisor recommended?

Elicit is an AI research assistant built around research papers.

Its help centre says it searches over 138 million academic papers from Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex and PubMed.

It can find papers, summarise them, let you chat with them, and write research reports.

It also has data extraction tables.

Say you have twenty papers on interruptions at work.

You add a column for the sample size, one for the method and one for the main finding, and Elicit fills them in for every paper.

**[ON SCREEN: a table, 20 papers as rows / columns: sample size, method, main finding]**

And it helps with systematic reviews, which are reviews that follow a strict, written-down method for finding and screening every relevant study, so someone else could repeat it.

As of October 2026, Elicit Basic is free, with unlimited paper summaries and chats with papers, and two research reports a month.

Elicit Plus is 12 dollars a month, and Elicit Pro is 49 dollars a month.

Elicit has limits too, and its help centre lists them.

For papers behind a paywall, it uses the title and abstract unless you have a subscription and its browser extension, and it doesn't include books or dissertations.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the fair comparison lands here, which is what the "vs" viewers came for]**

So this is how I'd put the difference.

Elicit is built for finding, screening and comparing papers.

Claude Science is built for doing the research work, like running the analysis on your data, making figures and writing, and reading papers is one part of that.

**[ON SCREEN: Elicit: finding, screening and comparing papers. Free plan. / Claude Science: analysis, figures, writing, with papers as one part. Paid Claude plan.]**

So if your thesis is mostly a literature review, Elicit is made for exactly that, and its free plan is a good place to start.

If your thesis has data you have to analyse, Claude Science does a lot more of that part.

And nothing stops you from using both.

**[SEGMENT 7: USING CLAUDE SCIENCE FOR YOUR THESIS WITHOUT GETTING IN TROUBLE]**

If you use Claude Science, or any AI tool, for your thesis, these are the things I'd do to stay out of academic integrity trouble.

First, read your university's AI policy before you start.

At my university, Tallinn University, the AI page says that "failure to make proper reference to the use of AI is considered academic fraud."

I went through my university's rules properly in my video on how I use AI as a master's student, so I'll link it.

Second, tell your supervisor which tool you're using and what for.

They might have rules for your department, and they're the one who signs off on your work.

Third, check every citation yourself, even with the reviewer on.

Open the paper, find the part that's supposed to say what Claude says it says, and read it with your own eyes.

The reviewer is a second pair of eyes, and you're still the first.

Fourth, use the history.

Because every figure and table keeps the code and the conversation behind it, you have a record of exactly how you used AI.

So when your university asks you to describe your AI use, you can be specific.

And fifth, write the parts you're graded on yourself.

The same Tallinn University page says the author is "fully responsible for the content and form of the presented work."

So whatever Claude Science helps with, your name is the one on the thesis.

My supervisor recommended Elicit for my thesis. The tool I'm using right now is Claude Science, the research app Anthropic released in beta on 30 June 2026. My thesis is for an MSc in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University, and it's about the invisible cognitive cost of switching between productivity tools, so research tools sit very close to my topic. This is my review, and it isn't sponsored.

Claude Science is a desktop app you install separately from the normal Claude app, and it runs on macOS, Windows and Linux. You describe a research task in plain language, and Claude writes Python, R or shell code, runs it in a sandbox on your computer, and saves what it makes as artifacts with a full history. A figure carries the code that drew it and the conversation that led to it, so when someone asks where a number came from, the answer is attached to the number. It connects to scientific databases, most of them in the life sciences, and some that work for any field, including a literature connector that searches OpenAlex and arXiv and a literature review skill that helps Claude find, verify and synthesise papers. It retrieves open access full text on its own, and reaches paywalled papers only through credentials or a library proxy you add, which gives it the access you already have and nothing more.

The feature I would point any student to first is the reviewer. Anthropic describes it as a verification step that re-reads Claude's responses, the approved plan, saved artifacts and the execution record, then checks whether the claims match what ran. Their examples include a result reported as computed when nothing ran, a citation that doesn't support the claim attributed to it, and a reference whose DOI resolves to a different article. Students have good reason to care about that last one, since AI tools have a record of inventing references and attaching real papers to claims they never made. There is a catch in the settings, though. Automatic review is on by default on the Max, Team and Enterprise plans, and on the Pro plan it starts off, so you have to turn it on in each session.

Anthropic is direct about the limits, and I think that's to its credit. The docs say the reviewer "reduces, but doesn't eliminate, errors," that it checks claims against the record without re-running analyses, and that it doesn't judge whether a method was the right choice for your research question. If you pick the wrong statistical test, the reviewer can confirm the test ran and the numbers match, and the choice of test is still yours to defend. Your files stay on your computer, but what Claude reads is sent to the model as part of the conversation, so anyone working with interviews or survey responses should check what their ethics approval and consent forms allow first. Usage counts against the same limits as the rest of your Claude plan, long analyses pause at those limits, and the app is still in beta.

Elicit deserves a fair description, because it's a good tool built for a different centre of gravity. Its help centre says it searches over 138 million academic papers drawn from Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex and PubMed. It summarises papers, lets you chat with them, writes research reports, supports systematic reviews, and builds extraction tables where each column pulls the same detail, such as sample size or method, from every paper in a set. Elicit Basic is free with unlimited summaries and two research reports a month, Plus is 12 dollars a month and Pro is 49, as of October 2026. Its own limits are listed plainly too, since paywalled papers are read from the title and abstract unless you have a subscription and its browser extension, and books and dissertations aren't included.

So the honest comparison is about what your thesis needs. Elicit is built for finding, screening and comparing papers, and if your thesis is mostly a literature review, it is made for exactly that and its free plan is a sensible place to start. Claude Science is built for doing the research work, such as analysing your own data, making figures and writing, with literature as one part. It comes only with paid Claude plans. I'd also be careful recommending it to someone who can't read any code, because the analysis is only as trustworthy as your ability to check it.

Whichever tool you choose, the same rules apply. Read your university's AI policy before you start; at Tallinn University, failing to properly reference the use of AI is treated as academic fraud, and the author stays fully responsible for the content of the work. Tell your supervisor what you're using. Open every cited paper and find the passage yourself, even with a reviewer switched on. If you use Claude Science, the history it keeps is also a good record of exactly how AI was involved, which makes disclosure easier to do properly. I'd like to know which AI tool you're using for your research, and what you use it for.
