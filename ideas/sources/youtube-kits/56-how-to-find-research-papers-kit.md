**[SEGMENT 1: WHY FINDING RESEARCH PAPERS IS THE HARD PART]**

If you're writing a thesis or a project, finding the right research papers can be harder than reading them.

You search your topic on Google, and you get blog posts and adverts.

Or you finally find the perfect paper, and it asks you to pay before you can read it.

So in this video, I'll show you where to search, how one good paper leads you to the rest, how to get the PDF for free and legally, and how to tell if a paper is worth your time.

We'll start with Google Scholar.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: HOW TO SEARCH GOOGLE SCHOLAR]**

Google Scholar is a free search engine for academic work, like articles, theses and books.

I think it's the best place to start, because it searches across a lot of publishers at once.

First, put a phrase in quotation marks.

If you're researching how people switch between apps, for example, search for "task switching" in quotes.

Then Google Scholar looks for those two words together, in that order.

**[ON SCREEN: a Google Scholar search for "task switching", with the quotation marks highlighted]**

Second, on the results page, you can choose to only see papers since a certain year.

If you're writing a thesis, recent papers matter, because they show you where the research is right now.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the alert is a tip that keeps working for the viewer long after the video ends]**

Third, once you have a search that gives you good results, set an alert.

Click the envelope icon, type your email, and click "Create alert".

Then Google Scholar emails you when new papers match that search, so you don't have to keep searching every week.

**[ON SCREEN: the envelope icon and the "Create alert" button]**
---

**[SEGMENT 3: FOLLOWING CITATIONS BACKWARD AND FORWARD]**

When you find one good research paper, you can use it to find many more, and this is called following the citations.

I like to think of it like a family tree.

Going backward means reading the paper's reference list.

Those are the older papers it was built on, like its parents and grandparents.

Going forward means looking at the newer papers that cited it, like its children.

On Google Scholar, under each result, there's a link that says "Cited by", with a number.

**[ON SCREEN: a Google Scholar result with "Cited by", "Related articles" and "All versions" circled]**

If it says "Cited by 300", it means 300 works that Google Scholar knows about have cited that paper, and you can click it to see all of them.

Some of those newer papers will build on it, and some will disagree with it, and you want to know both.

Next to it is "Related articles", which shows you similar papers.

And you can set an alert on a "Cited by" page too, so Google Scholar emails you whenever someone new cites that paper.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: KESHAV'S TIP FOR STARTING A LITERATURE REVIEW]**

If you're starting a literature review from zero, there's a tip I like from S. Keshav.

He's a computer scientist, and in 2007, while he was at the University of Waterloo, he wrote a short paper called "How to Read a Paper".

**[ON SCREEN: S. Keshav, "How to Read a Paper", ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 2007]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: this is the step-by-step method, and it builds to the moment you can stop searching]**

First, he says to use a search engine like Google Scholar to "find three to five recent papers in the area."

Do a quick first read of each one, which I show you how to do in my video on how to read a research paper.

Then read their related work sections.

The related work section is the part of a paper where the authors talk about the research that came before theirs.

So someone has already done a small search for you.

If you're lucky, one of those papers points you to a survey paper, which is a paper that reviews the research on a whole topic.

Keshav writes, "If you can find such a survey, you are done."

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: "And if you don't find one, the next step is the one I find most useful."]**

If you don't find a survey, look at the reference lists of those papers.

Look for the papers and the names that keep showing up again and again.

Keshav says, "These are the key papers and researchers in that area."

Then see where those researchers publish, because, in Keshav's words, "the best researchers usually publish in the top conferences."

---

**[SEGMENT 5: ARXIV AND PREPRINTS]**

If you're in computer science or AI, a lot of the papers people share online come from arXiv.

arXiv is a free, open access site where researchers post their papers, often before a journal or a conference publishes them.

The papers on arXiv are called preprints.

Peer review is when other experts in the field read a paper and check it before it gets published.

arXiv does check papers before they go up, but arXiv itself says, "the arXiv moderation process is not a peer-review process."

So a preprint can be very good, but you can't assume it has been peer reviewed.

Read it with a bit more care.

A lot of arXiv papers get published later, so search the title on Google Scholar and click "All versions".

If there's a published version, I would read and cite that one.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: RESEARCH DATABASES FOR YOUR FIELD]**

Most fields also have their own research database, and it's worth knowing the one for yours.

If you're in HCI or computer science, for example, it's the ACM Digital Library.

ACM publishes the proceedings of CHI, which ACM calls its flagship conference for human-computer interaction.

The proceedings are all the papers from a conference, collected together.

And as of the first of January 2026, ACM says all of its publications in the Digital Library are open access, so you can read them for free.

**[ON SCREEN: ACM Digital Library, all ACM publications open access since 1 January 2026]**

If you're in medicine or biology, there's PubMed, which is free to search, and it often links you to the full paper.

If you're in engineering, there's IEEE Xplore.

Anyone can search it, but a lot of the full papers need a subscription.

And Semantic Scholar is a free search tool for scientific papers, from the Allen Institute for AI.

If you ever ask an AI chatbot to find papers for you, open every single one, because a 2023 study by William Walters and Esther Wilder found ChatGPT making up references that don't exist.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: HOW TO GET A RESEARCH PAPER FOR FREE AND LEGALLY]**

**[~6:00 BEAT: this is the payoff people came for, and it opens on the paywall problem from the intro]**

When you find a research paper and it's behind a paywall, there are a few free and legal things to try before you pay.

The first is your university library.

Universities pay for journal subscriptions so their students and staff can read these papers.

So log in through your library website.

You can also add your university library in Google Scholar's settings, and then you'll see a link to get the paper through your library next to the results.

The second is "All versions" on Google Scholar.

Sometimes one of those versions is a free PDF, like a copy on the author's university website.

The third is the author's own website.

A lot of researchers put copies of their papers there.

The fourth is a free browser extension called Unpaywall.

When you open a paper's page, it checks whether a free, legal copy exists somewhere, and if it does, it gives you a link to it.

**[ON SCREEN: Unpaywall, unpaywall.org]**

And if none of that works, email the author.

Tell them who you are and what you're working on, and ask if they can share a copy.

I think most researchers are happy that someone wants to read their work, and the worst that can happen is that they don't reply.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: HOW TO TELL IF A RESEARCH PAPER IS A GOOD SOURCE]**

Before you build your work on a research paper, check if it's a good source.

The first thing I check is where it was published.

Is it a journal or a conference that people in your field know and respect?

For HCI, for example, CHI is one of those.

The second thing is whether it was peer reviewed.

A preprint can still be useful, but I'd say clearly that it's a preprint when you cite it.

The third thing is how many times it's been cited.

A lot of citations usually means people in the field know the paper.

But a paper from last year won't have many citations yet, because people haven't had time to cite it.

A paper can also be cited a lot because people disagree with it.

And citation numbers are very different from field to field.

The Leiden Manifesto, published in Nature in 2015, says to "account for variation by field in publication and citation practices."

So use citations as one clue, and read the paper to decide if it's good.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How to Find Research Papers (and Get Them for Free, Legally)

### Subtitle:
> Where to search, how one good paper leads you to the rest, and what to try before you pay for a paper.

### SEO Title:
> How to Find Research Papers for Free: A Student's Guide

### SEO Description:
> A plain guide to finding research papers for a thesis or literature review, with Google Scholar tips, citation chasing, S. Keshav's method, arXiv, field databases and five legal ways to get a paywalled paper.

---

If you're writing a thesis or a project, finding the right research papers can be harder than reading them. You search your topic on Google and get blog posts and adverts, or you finally find the perfect paper and it asks you to pay before you can read it. Both problems have fixes, and most of them are free.

I would start with Google Scholar, which is a free search engine for academic work like articles, theses and books, because it searches across a lot of publishers at once. A few small habits make it much better. Put your main phrase in quotation marks, like "task switching", so Scholar keeps the words together. Use the year filter on the results page so you see recent work, which matters for a thesis because it shows you where the research is now. Once a search gives you good results, click the envelope icon and create an alert, and Scholar will email you when new papers match it. And when you find a paper you like, click "Save", so it goes into "My library" and you don't lose it.

When you find one good paper, you can follow its citations to find many more. I think of it like a family tree. The reference list at the end of the paper is its parents and grandparents, the older work it was built on. The "Cited by" link under each Google Scholar result is its children, the newer papers that cited it. Some of those will build on it and some will disagree with it, and you want to know both. "Related articles" sits next to it and shows similar papers, and you can set an alert on a "Cited by" page so Scholar tells you whenever someone new cites a paper your thesis depends on.

If you're starting a literature review from zero, S. Keshav's 2007 paper "How to Read a Paper" has a method I like. He says to use a search engine like Google Scholar to "find three to five recent papers in the area," do a quick first pass on each one, and then read their related work sections, which is where authors describe the research that came before theirs. If one of them points you to a survey paper, he writes, "If you can find such a survey, you are done." If not, look for the papers and authors that keep appearing in their reference lists, because "these are the key papers and researchers in that area." Then check where those researchers publish, since "the best researchers usually publish in the top conferences," and look through those conferences' recent papers.

In computer science and AI, a lot of what gets shared online comes from arXiv, a free, open access site where researchers post papers, often before a journal or conference publishes them. Those papers are preprints. arXiv does check submissions, but its own moderation page says "the arXiv moderation process is not a peer-review process." So a preprint can be very good, and you still can't assume it has been peer reviewed. Many arXiv papers are published later, so it's worth searching the title on Google Scholar and checking "All versions" for a published version, which is the one I would cite.

Most fields also have their own database. For HCI and computer science, it's the ACM Digital Library, which holds the proceedings of CHI, the conference ACM calls its flagship for human-computer interaction. ACM says that as of 1 January 2026, all of its publications in the Digital Library are open access. PubMed is free to search for medicine and biology and holds more than 40 million citations, although it links out for full text. IEEE Xplore lets anyone search and read abstracts in engineering, with many full papers behind a subscription, and Semantic Scholar is a free search tool for scientific papers from the Allen Institute for AI. If you ever ask an AI chatbot for papers, open every one, because a 2023 study in Scientific Reports by William Walters and Esther Wilder found ChatGPT producing references that don't exist.

When a paper is behind a paywall, there are free and legal routes to try before paying. Your university library is the first, because universities pay for journal subscriptions so students and staff can read them, and you can add your library in Google Scholar's settings to see a library link next to results. "All versions" on Google Scholar sometimes turns up a free PDF on the author's university site. The author's own website often has a copy. Unpaywall, a free browser extension, checks whether a legal free copy exists and links you to it. And if none of that works, you can email the author, say who you are and what you're working on, and ask for a copy. I think most researchers are happy that someone wants to read their work.

Before you build your work on a paper, check where it was published, whether it was peer reviewed, and how often it has been cited. Citations need care. A paper from last year won't have many yet, a paper can be cited a lot because people disagree with it, and citation numbers vary a lot between fields. The Leiden Manifesto, published in Nature in 2015, asks anyone using research metrics to "account for variation by field in publication and citation practices." So I treat citations as one clue and read the paper to decide if it's good.

**9. Corrections / careful wording**
- **ACM open access.** ACM's announcement is about ACM's own publications. The Digital Library also has a paid Premium tier with extra tools and an index of other publishers' work, so the script says "all of its publications in the Digital Library are open access" and never "the whole ACM Digital Library is free".
- **arXiv and peer review.** arXiv does moderate submissions, so the script says "arXiv does check papers before they go up" and then quotes its own line about moderation not being peer review. It says "you can't assume it has been peer reviewed", because some arXiv papers are versions of papers that were later peer reviewed elsewhere.
- **"Cited by" numbers.** Google Scholar counts the citing works it has indexed, which can include theses, preprints and books. The script says "300 works that Google Scholar knows about" and not "300 peer-reviewed papers".
- **PubMed.** PubMed is free to search but doesn't hold full text itself, so the script says it "often links you to the full paper" and doesn't promise free PDFs.
- **Google Scholar library links.** Not every university is set up for this, so the script says "you can also add your university library" without promising a link for every school.
- **Emailing authors and author websites.** There's no study behind "most researchers are happy", so it's framed as your opinion ("I think"). "A lot of researchers put copies of their papers there" is hedged with "a lot", because publisher rules on self-archiving differ.
- **AI tools.** The Walters and Wilder study tested GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in 2023, and newer tools may do better or worse. The script only says the study found ChatGPT making up references, and the advice is to open every paper, which holds for any tool.
- **Citation counts.** "A paper can also be cited a lot because people disagree with it" is a general point about how citation works, and the field difference is backed by the Leiden Manifesto's own principle.
- **Left out on purpose.** the pirate site you said not to mention, ResearchGate (some copies there are uploaded without permission, so it needs more explaining than this video has room for) and predatory journals.
