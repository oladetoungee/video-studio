**[SEGMENT 3: WHY THE END OF THE YEAR FEELS LIKE A FRESH START]**

A lot of us feel a pull to start again when a new year is coming, and there's research on why that happens.

In 2014, three researchers, Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis, published a paper called "The Fresh Start Effect."

They looked at three kinds of real-world data.

First, they looked at Google searches for the word "diet" in the US.

Then they looked at how often students at one American university went to the gym.

And then they looked at goals people committed to on a goal-setting website called stickK.

All three went up right after what they called temporal landmarks.

A temporal landmark is a date that feels like a line in your calendar.

The start of a new year is one, and so is a new month, a new week, a new semester, your birthday, or the first day back after a holiday.

**[ON SCREEN: temporal landmarks / new year / new month / new week / new semester / birthday / after a holiday]**

For the "diet" searches, the biggest jump came at the start of a new year.


The researchers' explanation is that these dates help us put our old mistakes in an old chapter, and look at our lives from a bigger picture.

That part is their best explanation, and the paper didn't test it directly.

So if December makes you want to sit down and plan, that feeling is normal, and you can use it.

But you don't have to wait for the first of January.

The start of a new week and a new month showed the same pattern in their data, so any Monday can be your start.

And a fresh start gives you a push at the beginning, so the rest of this video is about what carries you after the push.

I start here because of how I define productivity.

For me, being productive is bigger than doing your nine to five well.

There are so many areas of your life where you can be productive.

So take a page, and write those areas down.

**[ON SCREEN: a page with the areas written down the side / work / studies / health and rest / relationships / money / faith / things I love]**


Under each area, answer two questions.

What went well this year, and what drained me?

Then ask one more question for each area.

What did I keep saying yes to that I don't want to carry into next year?

For example, under work, you might write that you finished a project you're proud of.

And under rest, you might write that you worked most weekends.

Be honest with yourself, because nobody else is going to read this page.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the page is done, and the next move is choosing what to focus on]**

When you're done, circle the one or two areas that need the most attention next year.

You don't have to fix every area of your life in one year.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: CHECK FOR BURNOUT BEFORE YOU PLAN MORE]**

Before you add new goals for 2027, it helps to check how tired you already are.

About four years ago, I experienced burnout.

That's how I ended up with a productivity coach, and it's a big part of why I think about productivity the way I do now.

So I want to tell you what burnout means, because a lot of us use the word loosely.


The World Health Organization describes burnout in its classification of diseases, which is called ICD-11.

It says burnout is a syndrome that comes from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

Chronic here means it's been going on for a long time.

The WHO describes three signs.

The first one is feeling drained, like your energy is used up, or you're exhausted.

The second one is feeling more distant from your job, or negative and cynical about it.

And the third one is feeling less effective at your work.

**[ON SCREEN: burnout, as the WHO describes it / exhaustion / distance or cynicism about your job / feeling less effective at work / source: WHO, ICD-11]**


The WHO says this word is about work, and it shouldn't be used to describe other areas of life.

And the WHO doesn't classify burnout as a medical condition. It lists it as an occupational phenomenon, which means something connected to your work.

I'm not a doctor, so I can't tell you if what you're feeling is burnout.

If you heard those three signs and saw yourself in them, please talk to a doctor or someone you trust before you plan a bigger 2027.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And if you're okay, the next part is for you, because this is where most plans go wrong.]**


What I would tell you is to put rest in your plan as its own item, the same way you'd put a deadline.

If you're working and studying, or working two jobs, leave space in the plan.

A plan that's full from the first week of January is very hard to keep going until March.

**[SEGMENT 6: STEP 2, MAP THE SEASONS OF YOUR 2027]**

The second step of the reset is to map the seasons of your year before you set any goals.

Every year has busy seasons and lighter seasons, and most of us already know some of them.

For example, this is what I already know about my own 2027.

I'm doing my master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University, and I also work full-time as a developer.

The plan is to clear all my other courses this autumn, so that from the spring of 2027, school is only my thesis.

That changes what my spring looks like, and the goals I set have to fit around it.

**[ON SCREEN: a 12-month strip / January to June marked "thesis + full-time work"]**


So take your calendar and mark what you already know about next year.

That could be exams, a busy season at work, a wedding you're travelling for, the months your kids are home, or a move.

Then, for each area you circled, set one or two goals, and put each goal where it fits.

A goal that needs a lot of time goes in a lighter season.

In a heavy season, your goal might be to keep something small going, like reading a few pages a day, and save the big new thing for later.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the goals are set, and the next step is the one with the strongest research behind it]**

And make each goal something you can check.

"Read more" is hard to check at the end of the year.

"Read one book a month" is easy to check.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: STEP 3, TURN EACH GOAL INTO AN IF-THEN PLAN]**

The third step of the reset is to turn each goal into an if-then plan, and it's the step with the strongest research behind it.

This idea comes from Peter Gollwitzer, a psychology professor who has studied goals for decades.

He calls these plans implementation intentions.

The form is, "If situation Y happens, then I will do Z."

So you decide in advance when and where you'll act, instead of deciding again every day.

**[ON SCREEN: IF (when and where) ... THEN I WILL (what I'll do)]**


For example, "read more" becomes, "If I sit down to eat, then I'll read instead of watching something."

That one is how I read. When I eat, I read, instead of putting something on to watch.

Another one could be, "If it's Sunday evening, then I'll plan my week."

Or, "If I open my laptop in the morning, then I'll work on my most important task before I open email."

The situation becomes your reminder, so the decision is already made when the moment comes.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And this has been tested many times, by many researchers.]**


In 2006, Gollwitzer and another researcher, Paschal Sheeran, combined the results of 94 tests of if-then plans.

People who made if-then plans reached their goals more often than people who only set the goal.

The researchers described the effect as medium to large.

In plain words, it made a clear difference.

One more thing I'd do is write an if-then plan for the moment your goal usually goes wrong.

For example, "If I get home tired and reach for my phone, then I'll put it on the charger in the other room first."

---

**[SEGMENT 8: STEP 4, PUT YOUR PLAN WHERE IT CAN REMIND YOU]**

The fourth step of the reset is to put your plan where it can remind you, because a plan only helps if it shows up at the right moment.

For some people, that's the calendar on their phone.

For other people, it's an alarm, or a sticky note on the fridge.

For me, I'm building myself a little habit app.

It has a Pomodoro timer, windows when social media is blocked, my wake-up and sleep times, and streaks for my habits.

**[ON SCREEN: a quick look at the habit app, if you want to show it]**


A Pomodoro timer comes from the Pomodoro Technique, which Francesco Cirillo came up with.

You work for 25 minutes, and then you take a short break.

You don't need to build an app to do any of this.

What matters is that your if-then plans have a place where they meet you during the day.

And keep it simple enough that you'll still be using it in March.

If your phone is the thing that keeps pulling you away from your plans, I made a video on what I do instead of doomscrolling, and I'll link it.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: STEP 5, A WEEKLY REVIEW KEEPS THE PLAN GOING]**

The last step of the reset is a weekly review, and I think it's the step that keeps a new year plan going after January.

A fresh start gives you a push, and that push doesn't last all year.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the habit that holds the whole plan together starts here]**

The weekly review comes from David Allen's book, Getting Things Done.

Once a week, you sit down and go through three things, which he calls get clear, get current and get creative.

Get clear means emptying everything out of your head, your notes and your inbox, so nothing is floating around.

Get current means updating your lists and your calendar, so they match what's true this week.

Get creative means looking ahead and thinking about what you want to do next.

**[ON SCREEN: weekly review (David Allen) / get clear / get current / get creative / + check your if-then plans]**


For your 2027 plan, I'd add one more thing to it.

Look at your if-then plans, keep the ones that worked, and change the ones that didn't.

And remember the fresh start research from Dai, Milkman and Riis.

The start of a new week was one of those fresh-start dates in their data too.

So if you do your review at the start of your week, you get a small fresh start every single week, instead of waiting a whole year for the next one.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How I'm Preparing for 2027

### Subtitle:
> Productivity has been a big deal in my life since my burnout. This is the end-of-year reset I'd do with you before the new year starts.

### SEO Title:
> How to Prepare for 2027: A Simple End of Year Productivity Reset

### SEO Description:
> A plain, research-backed end-of-year reset for 2027, covering the fresh start effect, a burnout check using the WHO's description, mapping your year's seasons, if-then plans and a weekly review.

---

For me, productivity has been a really big deal in my life for the past few years. I've always known what the word means, and I've always tried to live a productive life, in the ways that I define productivity. I don't think being productive is only about doing your nine to five or your job well. There are so many areas of your life where you should be productive and you can be productive. My first serious encounter with productivity was about four years ago. I experienced burnout that year, and one thing led to another, and I had a productivity coach. It really changed things for me, and ever since, productivity has been something I'm very conscious of in my day to day. I don't know what is waiting for me in 2027, but I'm really excited for it, and there are so many things I'm putting in place.

There's a reason so many of us want to plan in December. In 2014, Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis published a paper called "The Fresh Start Effect." They found that Google searches for "diet," gym visits at a university, and goals people committed to on the website stickK all went up right after what they called temporal landmarks, like a new year, a new month, a new week, a new semester or a birthday. For the "diet" searches, the biggest jump was at the start of a year. Their explanation is that these dates let us put old mistakes in an old chapter and look at our lives from a bigger picture, though the paper didn't test that part directly. The useful thing for me is that a new week showed the pattern too, so you never have to wait for January to start.

The first step I'd take is to look back at the year one area of life at a time. Write the areas down the side of a page: work, studies, health and rest, relationships, money, faith if that's part of your life, and the things you do because you love them. Under each one, ask what went well and what drained you, and then ask what you kept saying yes to that you don't want to carry into next year. When you're done, circle the one or two areas that need the most attention. Nobody fixes every area of their life in one year.

Before adding anything new, I'd check how tired you already are. The World Health Organization describes burnout, in ICD-11, as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, and it lists three signs: exhaustion, increased mental distance from your job or cynicism about it, and reduced professional efficacy, which means feeling less effective at work. The WHO says the term is specifically about work, and that burnout is not classified as a medical condition. I'm not a doctor, and if those signs sound like you, please talk to a doctor or someone you trust first. For everyone else, I'd put rest in the plan as its own item, the same way you'd put a deadline.

The next step is to map the seasons of the year before setting goals. Most of us already know some of them. I'm doing my master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University while working full-time as a developer, and the plan is to clear my other courses this autumn so that from spring 2027, school is only my thesis. My goals have to fit around that. Mark the busy and lighter seasons you already know about, put big goals in the lighter ones, and in a heavy season, let the goal be keeping something small going. Make each goal something you can check, so "read one book a month" instead of "read more."

Then turn each goal into an if-then plan. The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer calls these implementation intentions, and the form is "If situation Y happens, then I will do Z." You decide in advance when and where you'll act, so the moment itself becomes the reminder. My own version for reading is simple: when I sit down to eat, I read instead of putting something on to watch. In 2006, Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined 94 tests of if-then plans and found an effect they described as medium to large on whether people reached their goals. I'd also write one for the moment a goal usually goes wrong, like getting home tired and reaching for your phone.

A plan only helps if it meets you during the day, so put it somewhere it can remind you. For some people that's a calendar, an alarm or a sticky note.
