**[SEGMENT 2: HABIT 1, PREVIEW BEFORE YOU READ]**

The first habit that improves reading comprehension is to preview what you're about to read, before you read it.

This is the habit from the Closed Stacks video, and researchers call it schema activation.

A schema is the bundle of things you already know about something.

If I say "restaurant", you already know you'll sit down, get a menu, order, eat, and pay at the end.

When you read, your brain uses what you already know to make sense of the new thing.

There's a famous study from 1972 that shows how much this matters, and Closed Stacks cites it too.

John Bransford and Marcia Johnson had people listen to a short passage and then try to remember it.

The passage starts by saying the procedure is quite simple, and first you arrange things into different groups.

It goes on like that the whole way, and it never says what the procedure is.

Some people were told the topic before they heard it, which was washing clothes.

Some were told the topic after they heard it, and some were never told.

The people who knew the topic before understood the passage and remembered a lot more of it.

The people who were told the topic after didn't get that benefit.

**[ON SCREEN: told "washing clothes" before / it makes sense, they remember more · told after, or never / it doesn't]**

Everyone heard the same words.

The only difference was whether they knew it was about laundry while they were listening.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the viewer now knows why previewing works, and the next lines show how to do it in two minutes]**

So how do you do this with a real book or chapter?

Before you start a chapter, spend a couple of minutes on the headings, the pictures or diagrams, and the summary at the end if there is one.

Then ask yourself, what do I already know about this topic?

Say you're about to read a chapter on how vaccines work.

You might remember that a vaccine trains your body to recognise a germ before you get sick.

That's your starting point, and the chapter hangs new details onto it.

For a novel, it can be as small as reading the back cover and thinking about where and when the story is set.

There's one warning, and Closed Stacks talks about this too.

Their video is about how schema activation can help your reading, and also how it can distort it.

If you switch on the wrong idea, it can pull your understanding in the wrong direction.

For example, if you see an article called "Python" and you assume it's about the programming language, the paragraphs about snakes will confuse you, because you'll keep trying to fit them into code.

So treat your preview as a guess, and when the text tells you something different, let the text win.

**[SEGMENT 3: WHY REREADING FEELS LIKE IT WORKS]**

A lot of us try to improve our reading comprehension by rereading.

You read the chapter, you didn't get all of it, so you read it again.

In 2009, Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger asked 177 university students in the US how they study.

84% said they reread their notes or their textbook, and 55% said rereading was their number one strategy.

Only about 11% mentioned testing themselves, and only 1% said that was their main strategy.

**[ON SCREEN: 177 students · 84% reread · 55% said rereading was their #1 strategy · 11% tested themselves · 1% said testing was their #1]**

Rereading feels good because the second time, everything looks familiar.

The words flow, you recognise the examples, and that feeling makes you think you know it.

But recognising something when it's in front of you is a lot easier than pulling it out of your head when the book is closed.

In 2013, John Dunlosky and four other researchers reviewed ten popular study techniques and rated how useful each one is, based on the evidence.

Two got a high rating. Practice testing, which means quizzing yourself, and distributed practice, which means spreading your study out over time.

Three got a moderate rating. Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice.

And five got a low rating. Summarisation, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, using mental images for text, and rereading.

**[ON SCREEN: Dunlosky et al., 2013 · HIGH: practice testing, distributed practice · MODERATE: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice · LOW: summarisation, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading]**

A low rating means the benefits they found were small, or didn't show up consistently, so rereading can still help a little.

But the authors suggest that students would get more from their time by swapping some of that rereading for testing themselves.

So the next habit is that swap.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: HABIT 2, CLOSE THE BOOK AND RECALL]**

The second habit that improves reading comprehension is to close the book and try to recall what you just read.

Researchers call this retrieval practice, and in Dunlosky's review, it's the practice testing that got the high rating.

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke gave students a short science passage to learn.

One group studied it four times.

Another group studied it once, and then took three recall tests, where they wrote down everything they could remember, with no feedback.

Five minutes later, the group that studied four times remembered more. 83% of the ideas, against 71%.

But one week later, it flipped.

The group that studied four times remembered 40%, and the group that kept recalling remembered 61%.

**[ON SCREEN: studied 4 times / 83% after 5 min · 40% after 1 week · studied once, recalled 3 times / 71% after 5 min · 61% after 1 week (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006)]**

And when the researchers asked people to predict how well they'd remember it in a week, the group that studied four times was the most confident.

So the method that felt the best did the worst a week later.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the reason to stay is the "how to do it" that comes next, which anyone can try tonight]**

So this is how you can do it with a book.

When you finish a chapter, or even a few pages, close it, and say or write down what you remember.

No peeking.

You'll find the gaps straight away.

Maybe you remember there were three causes of something, and you can only name two.

Then open the book, check, and fill the gap.

It will feel harder than rereading, and that's normal.

Researchers think the effort of pulling it out of your head is part of what makes it stay.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And this next part is the one I like the most, because it works for novels too.]**

If you come back the next day and recall it again, you're also doing the other high-rated technique, spreading your study out over time.

And for a novel, you can do a softer version.

Before you start reading today, try to remember what happened in the last chapter, before you open the book.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: HABIT 3, ASK WHY WHILE YOU READ]**

The third habit that improves reading comprehension is to keep asking "why" while you read, and to explain things to yourself.

Dunlosky's review covers two techniques here, and both got a moderate rating.

The first one is elaborative interrogation.

It's a long name for asking yourself why a fact is true.

Say you read that plants grow towards the light.

You stop and ask, why would that be?

Because plants need light to make their food, so growing towards it helps them survive.

Now that fact is connected to something you already know, and it's easier to understand and remember.

The second one is self-explanation.

You stop after a sentence or a paragraph and explain to yourself what it means, and how it connects to what you read before it.

In a 1994 study, Michelene Chi and her colleagues had eighth graders read a text about the circulatory system, which is how blood moves around your body.

One group was asked to explain each line to themselves, out loud.

The other group read the text twice.

The group that explained learned more, and within that group, the students who explained more understood more.

**[ON SCREEN: explain each line to yourself / learned more · read it twice / learned less (Chi et al., 1994)]**

So explaining it once did better than reading it twice.

And this is explaining it to yourself, while you read.

Explaining what you read to someone else is harder, and I made a separate video on why.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: HABIT 4, SLOW DOWN WHEN THE TEXT GETS HARD]**

The fourth habit that improves reading comprehension is to slow down when the text gets hard, and to let yourself go back.

There are apps and courses that promise to teach you to read thousands of words a minute.

In 2016, Keith Rayner and four other researchers reviewed the science of how we read, and whether speed reading works.

They found that skilled readers average about 200 to 400 words a minute.

And they found a trade-off between speed and accuracy.

When people read faster, they understand less.

**[ON SCREEN: skilled readers / about 200 to 400 words a minute · read faster, understand less (Rayner et al., 2016)]**

Going back is also part of normal reading.

About 10 to 15% of the time, our eyes jump back to words we've already read.

In a 2014 study, Elizabeth Schotter, Randy Tran and Keith Rayner hid each word after people read it, so they couldn't look back.

Those readers understood less, and that was true for hard sentences and simple ones.

So if one sentence confuses you, going back to read it again is fine.

That's different from rereading the whole chapter as your way of studying.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the next segment is the honest one about summarising, which a lot of viewers do already]**

Skimming is fine when you only want the gist, but for a chapter you need to understand, read at your normal pace, or slower.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: HABIT 5, SUMMARISE IN YOUR OWN WORDS]**

The fifth habit that improves reading comprehension is to summarise what you read in your own words, and I have to be honest about this one.

In Dunlosky's review, summarisation got a low rating.

The authors said many learners, including children, high school students and even some undergraduates, would need a lot of training to summarise well.

And the results across studies were mixed.

A summary that copies sentences from the book doesn't do much, because you're moving words from one page to another.

So if you're going to summarise, I would do it like this.

Close the book first, so the summary comes out of your head. That way, it's also retrieval practice.

Keep it short, for example three sentences for a whole chapter, so you have to pick the main ideas.

Use your own words. If I can only say it in the author's words, I take that as a sign I haven't understood that part yet.

Then open the book and check your summary against it.

**[ON SCREEN: close the book · three sentences · your own words · check it]**

This is my own suggestion, built from what the review found, so I'm not claiming anyone tested this exact method.

It takes the low-rated technique and makes it work with the high-rated one.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: HABIT 6, READ HARD TEXTS ON PAPER WHEN YOU CAN]**

The sixth habit that improves reading comprehension is about whether you read on paper or on a screen.

In 2018, Pablo Delgado and three colleagues combined 54 studies, with over 171,000 participants, published between 2000 and 2017.

On average, people understood a bit more when they read on paper than when they read on a screen.

The difference was small, but it showed up in both kinds of study designs they looked at.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the caveats are where viewers learn whether this applies to them, so it's a good reason to stay to the end]**

There are three details in that study that tell you when this matters.

First, the paper advantage was bigger when people had a time limit.

When people read at their own pace, it was much smaller.

Second, it showed up for informational texts, like textbooks and articles.

For narrative texts, like stories, they found no difference, though they said only a few studies used stories.

This year, I've mostly been reading fiction, and I love Khaled Hosseini, so for novels, I don't think the format matters much.

Third, the paper advantage got bigger over the years they looked at, from 2000 to 2017.

**[ON SCREEN: paper vs screen (Delgado et al., 2018) · small advantage for paper · bigger with a time limit · informational texts, not stories · grew from 2000 to 2017]**

A later review from the same research group, published in 2024, looked at tablets and e-readers, and found an even smaller advantage for paper.

Researchers aren't fully sure why paper helps.

One idea they discuss is that we're used to skimming on screens, so we bring that habit with us when we read something serious.

So if the text is hard and informational, and you have the choice, I think paper is a good bet.

And if you're reading a novel on your phone on the bus, keep reading.

### SHORT 6: Speed reading (20-30 sec)

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Six Habits That Help You Understand What You Read

### Subtitle:
> A viral video gave one habit for better reading comprehension. These are the ones I'd add, with the research behind each.

### SEO Title:
> How to Improve Reading Comprehension: 6 Research-Backed Habits

### SEO Description:
> Six habits that research says improve reading comprehension and memory, including previewing, retrieval practice, self-explanation, slowing down, summarising well, and paper versus screen.

---

A video by Closed Stacks called "One Habit to Instantly Improve Reading Comprehension" went viral and landed on my feed. The habit is about activating what you already know before you read, which researchers call schema activation. My first thought was that for most of us, it might be several habits. I read almost every day, and as a master's student, a lot of what I read is material I have to understand, so I went looking for what the research says.

The Closed Stacks habit has an old and famous study behind it. In 1972, John Bransford and Marcia Johnson had people listen to a passage that began by saying the procedure was quite simple, and that first you arrange things into different groups. It never said what the procedure was. People who were told before listening that it was about washing clothes understood it and remembered much more of it, and people who were told afterwards didn't get that benefit. So before a chapter, I'd look at the headings and the diagrams and ask what I already know about the topic. Closed Stacks also points out that switching on the wrong idea can distort what you read, so a preview is a guess the text is allowed to correct.

Most of us reread. When Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger surveyed 177 university students in 2009, 84% said they reread their notes or textbook, and only about 11% mentioned testing themselves. Rereading feels productive because the second pass feels familiar. But in 2013, John Dunlosky and four colleagues reviewed ten study techniques and rated rereading low, along with highlighting and summarisation. Practice testing and distributed practice were rated high, and elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice were rated moderate.

The strongest habit is the simplest to describe. Close the book and try to recall what you just read. In 2006, Roediger and Karpicke had one group study a short passage four times and another study it once and recall it three times. Five minutes later, the group that studied four times remembered more. A week later, they remembered 40% of it, the recall group remembered 61%, and the group that had studied four times had been the most confident. For novels, there's a gentle version, which is to remember what happened in the last chapter before you open the book again.

While you read, it helps to keep asking why. If you read that plants grow towards light, ask why that would be, and connect it to the fact that plants need light to make food. Michelene Chi and her colleagues found in 1994 that eighth graders who explained each line of a text about the circulatory system to themselves learned more than those who read it twice. It also helps to slow down when the text gets hard. Keith Rayner and colleagues reviewed the science of speed reading in 2016 and found a trade-off between speed and accuracy, and a 2014 study by Elizabeth Schotter, Randy Tran and Rayner found that readers who couldn't look back at words understood less.

Summarising is trickier than it sounds. Dunlosky's review rated it low because many learners need a lot of training to do it well, and copying the author's sentences does little. My own approach, which isn't a tested method, is to close the book first, write three sentences in my own words, and then check them against the text, so the summary is also an act of recall.

Last, there's the question of paper versus screens. In 2018, Pablo Delgado and colleagues combined 54 studies with over 171,000 participants and found a small advantage for paper. It was larger when people read under time limits, it showed up for informational texts but not for narrative ones, and it grew between 2000 and 2017. A 2024 review from the same group, looking at tablets and e-readers, found an even smaller paper advantage. This year I've mostly read fiction, and I love Khaled Hosseini, so I'm not worried about where I read my novels. For a hard textbook chapter, I think paper is a good bet when you have the choice.

I'd like to know which of these you already do, and which one you'll try this week.

- Experiment 2: SSSS vs SSST vs STTT. Five minutes: 83%, 78%, 71%. One week: 40%, 56%, 61%. Predicted learning: 4.8, 4.2, 4.0 (SSSS most confident). The script compares only SSSS and STTT and says "studied once, then three recall tests, with no feedback". The passages were general science texts.
- "Researchers think the effort... is part of what makes it stay" is hedged on purpose, because why retrieval works is still debated.

**6. Self-explanation (Chi et al., 1994)**
Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994). "Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding." Cognitive Science, 18(3), 439-477. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3 (described at https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2020/2/20-1)
- Eighth graders, a text on the circulatory system. The prompted group self-explained each line; the control group read the text twice. The prompted group learned more, and high explainers understood more. Segment 5.
- The plants example for elaborative interrogation is an illustration, not from a study.

**7. Reading speed (Rayner et al., 2016) and looking back (Schotter et al., 2014)**
Rayner, K., Schotter, E. R., Masson, M. E. J., Potter, M. C., & Treiman, R. (2016). "So Much to Read, So Little Time: How Do We Read, and Can Speed Reading Help?" Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 17(1), 4-34. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100615623267 (APS release: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/speed-reading-promises-are-too-good-to-be-true-scientists-find.html)
- "there is a trade-off between speed and accuracy"; skilled readers "averaging 200 to 400 words per minute"; effective skimming means "prioritizing more informative parts of a text". Segment 6.
Schotter, E. R., Tran, R., & Rayner, K. (2014). "Don't Believe What You Read (Only Once): Comprehension Is Supported by Regressions During Reading." Psychological Science, 25(6), 1218-1226. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614531148 (APS write-up: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/read-this-blog-post-in-less-than-a-minute.html)
- Regressions happen "about 10 to 15 percent of the time"; a trailing mask that blocked looking back lowered comprehension for both ambiguous and simple sentences. Segment 6.

**8. Paper vs screen (Delgado et al., 2018; Salmerón et al., 2024)**
Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmerón, L. (2018). "Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension." Educational Research Review, 25, 23-38. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003 (PDF read: https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)
- 54 studies (38 between-participant, 16 within-participant), 171,055 participants, 2000-2017. Same paper advantage in both designs (g = -0.21), which is small. Larger under time constraints (g = -0.26 vs -0.09 self-paced). Informational g = -0.27, narrative g = 0.01, with few narrative-only studies. The advantage grew over the years. No age difference. Segment 8.
Salmerón, L., Altamura, L., Delgado, P., Karagiorgi, A., & Vargas, C. (2024). "Reading Comprehension on Handheld Devices Versus on Paper: A Narrative Review and Meta-Analysis of the Medium Effect and Its Moderators." Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000830 (accepted manuscript read: https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Salmeron%202023%20manuscript_tablets.pdf)
- Tablets and e-readers (smartphones excluded). Small print advantage, g about -0.11. Segment 8 says "an even smaller advantage for paper". Read from the accepted manuscript, so check the published numbers if you quote them.
- "One idea they discuss is that we're used to skimming on screens" is hedged on purpose. It's a discussed explanation (often called the shallowing hypothesis), not a settled finding.

**9. Corrections / careful wording**
- **"Instantly" is left out of our titles.** None of the six habits is proven to work instantly, so the titles don't promise it.
- **Low rating doesn't mean harmful.** The script says rereading "can still help a little", and the replies say the same.
- **Summarising is presented honestly.** It's rated low, and the "close the book, three sentences" method is clearly labelled as your own suggestion and untested.
- **Paper vs screen has caveats.** The script says "small", names the time-limit and text-type effects, notes that few studies used stories, and doesn't claim screens damage reading.
- **"Learning styles", "we only remember 10% of what we read" and the "learning pyramid" are left out.** They're common in reading-tip videos and aren't supported by good evidence.
- **Self-explanation here is explaining to yourself while reading.** The script doesn't cover the curse of knowledge or explaining to others, which is kit 41.
- **Unverified:** none of the numbers in the script. The only thing not checked against a primary source this session is the Closed Stacks transcript, which isn't quoted.
