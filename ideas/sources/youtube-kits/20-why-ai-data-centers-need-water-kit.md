## 1. YOUTUBE VIDEO SCRIPT

**How this script is built:** your 28 October read-through, with voice-to-text errors fixed. Every segment names its own topic in the first line or two. About 620 spoken words, roughly 4 to 4.5 minutes at a steady pace.

**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

**[VISUAL: hold up a glass of water]**

Why do AI data centers even need water?

One of the growing complaints I see on social media is how AI data centers are consuming water that should be for human usage.

And I, myself, I'm always thinking, why do they even need that much water in the first place?

So I looked into it a little bit.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHY AI CHIPS GET SO HOT]**

To understand why AI data centers need water, you have to start with heat.

Now, all these chips, AI chips, which include GPUs, they are made up of billions of tiny switches.

And when electricity flows through them, it turns into heat.

**[ON SCREEN: a chip, zoomed in, with "billions of tiny switches"]**

It's kind of a similar reason to why your phone gets a little bit warm when you're playing a game for too long.

With computers, nearly all the electricity that goes in ends up as heat.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: HOW MUCH HEAT AN AI DATA CENTER MAKES]**

Now, if you scale that up to an AI data center, a big AI data center can use, say, 100 megawatts of power.

A typical electric kettle uses about 2,000 watts.

So 100 megawatts is almost like 50,000 kettles boiling at the same time, all day, all night, inside the same building.

**[ON SCREEN: 100,000,000 W ÷ 2,000 W = 50,000 kettles]**

**[~1:00 BEAT: the kettle maths lands. "So where does the water come in?" is the reason to stay]**

If the heat stays in the building, the chips would slow themselves down to survive, and eventually they will fail and stop working.

So the heat has to leave the building somehow, and that's where water comes in.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHY WATER IS SO GOOD AT CARRYING HEAT]**

Now, data centers use water to cool down because water is very good at carrying heat.

One litre of water can soak up about 3,500 times more heat than a litre of air, for the same rise in temperature.

**[ON SCREEN: 1 litre of water ≈ 3,500 litres of air (same temperature rise)]**

And when water evaporates, it also carries away even more heat.

It's comparable to how sweating cools our body down.

Sweat evaporates off the skin, and it takes the heat away with it.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: HOW A DATA CENTER COOLING TOWER WORKS]**

So a lot of AI data centers cool themselves the same way that our body does, using cooling towers.

Warm water from the building gets sprayed down inside the tower, while fans pull outside air through it.

Some of that water evaporates, and that cools down the rest, you know, which goes back inside to pick up more heat.

**[ON SCREEN: simple cooling tower diagram, warm water down, air through, vapour out the top, cool water back to the building]**

I was trying to check the scale of how much water this uses.

According to the Environmental and Energy Study Institute, a large data center can use up to 5 million gallons of water a day.

That's roughly the daily water use of a small town of about 10,000 to 50,000 people.

**[ON SCREEN: up to 5 million gallons a day ≈ a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people (EESI)]**

So that's how much water, roughly.

**[~3:00 BEAT (lands around 2:40): the scale lands. "So why can't they just use any water?" is the next question]**

---

**[SEGMENT 6: WHY CAN'T DATA CENTERS USE SEAWATER?]**

Now, from a layman's perspective, if the water is only meant for cooling, I'm thinking, why can't data centers just use any other kind of water, like seawater?

Well, most of them can't.

**[VISUAL: the inside of a kettle with white scale]**

If you check your kettle at home, the white crust at the bottom is the minerals that were in your tap water, right?

Now, a cooling tower is evaporating water all day, so the minerals can build up.

If seawater evaporates, it just leaves salt everywhere, and salt can eat away at metal.

That's kind of why cooling towers need clean, fresh water.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: WHERE DATA CENTER WATER COMES FROM, AND WHERE IT GOES]**

Right.

The major crux of the discussion I've been seeing about AI data centers and water, mostly on social media at least, is that a lot of this data center water comes from the same public water systems that supply people's homes.

And you can make the argument that if the water is used, it evaporates, and again, it comes down as rain.

**[ON SCREEN: vapour leaves the tower, rain falls far away]**

But it doesn't go back to the same river or the same underground water that the people around it are using.

So I was just trying to understand the whole water and AI data center conversation, and that's just my understanding as of right now.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Why Do AI Data Centers Even Need Water?

### Subtitle:
> I kept seeing the complaints online, so I looked into why a building full of computers needs water in the first place.

### SEO Title:
> Why Do AI Data Centers Need So Much Water? How Cooling Works

### SEO Description:
> Why AI chips get so hot, how cooling towers use water, why data centers can't just use seawater, and where the water comes from.

---

One of the growing complaints I see on social media is that AI data centers are consuming water that should be for people. And I, myself, I'm always thinking, why do they even need that much water in the first place? So I looked into it a little bit.

To understand why AI data centers need water, you have to start with heat. All these chips, AI chips, which include GPUs, are made up of billions of tiny switches, and when electricity flows through them, it turns into heat. It's kind of a similar reason to why your phone gets a little bit warm when you play a game for too long. With computers, nearly all the electricity that goes in ends up as heat.

Now scale that up to an AI data center. A big one can use around 100 megawatts of power, and a typical electric kettle uses about 2,000 watts. So 100 megawatts is almost like 50,000 kettles boiling at the same time, all day and all night, inside the same building. If that heat stays in the building, the chips slow themselves down to survive, and eventually they fail and stop working. So the heat has to leave the building somehow, and that's where water comes in.

Data centers use water to cool down because water is very good at carrying heat. One litre of water can soak up about 3,500 times more heat than a litre of air for the same rise in temperature, and when water evaporates, it carries away even more. It's how sweating cools our body down, because sweat evaporates off the skin and takes the heat with it. A lot of AI data centers cool themselves the same way, using cooling towers. Warm water from the building gets sprayed down inside the tower while fans pull outside air through it, and some of that water evaporates and cools down the rest, which goes back inside to pick up more heat. According to the Environmental and Energy Study Institute, a large data center can use up to 5 million gallons of water a day, which is roughly the daily water use of a small town of about 10,000 to 50,000 people.

From a layman's perspective, I kept thinking that if the water is only meant for cooling, data centers could just use any other kind of water, like seawater. Most of them can't. If you check your kettle at home, the white crust at the bottom is the minerals that were in your tap water. A cooling tower is evaporating water all day, so those minerals can build up. If seawater evaporates, it leaves salt everywhere, and salt can eat away at metal. That's kind of why cooling towers need clean, fresh water.

The major crux of the discussion I've been seeing about AI data centers and water, mostly on social media at least, is that a lot of this water comes from the same public water systems that supply people's homes. You can make the argument that when the water is used, it evaporates and comes down as rain again. But it doesn't go back to the same river or the same underground water that the people around it are using. I was just trying to understand the whole water and AI data center conversation, and that's my understanding as of right now. If you've been seeing those complaints too, I'd love to know what you thought the water was for.

## 11. REFERENCES & SOURCES

All checked on 28 October 2026 unless marked.

**1. Chips, GPUs and heat (Segment 2)**
- "Nearly all the electricity a computer uses ends up as heat" is basic physics. The energy a chip uses has nowhere else to go, apart from a tiny amount that leaves as light, sound or signals.
- A GPU is a kind of processor chip, and AI data centers run mostly on GPUs and similar AI accelerator chips. Standard description. The script makes no numerical claim about them.

**2. Scale (Segment 3)**
- 100 MW for a big AI data center: Daily Maverick, 8 February 2026, "the larger AI-focused data centres can draw 100MW or more." https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2026-02-08-growth-spurt-of-data-centres-could-threaten-sas-electricity-and-water-supply.md (checked 27 October 2026). The script says "can use, say, 100 megawatts," which is your wording and sounds like an example, as it should.
- Kettle maths: 100,000,000 W ÷ 2,000 W = 50,000. Kettles are commonly 2,000 to 3,000 W, so "about 2,000 watts" is fair.
- Chips slow themselves down when too hot (thermal throttling) and fail if heat keeps building. Standard.

**3. Water and heat (Segment 4)**
- About 3,500 times: water holds about 4.18 kJ per litre per °C, and air about 1.2 J per litre per °C at room conditions, so about 3,480 times. Standard values.
- Evaporation carries far more heat than warming the water (about 2,400 kJ per kg at room temperature, against 4.18 kJ per kg per °C). Standard values. The sweat comparison is the usual one.

**4. Cooling towers and how much water (Segment 5)**
- **Environmental and Energy Study Institute (EESI), "Data Centers and Water Consumption," Miguel Yañez-Barnuevo, 25 June 2025:** "Large data centers can consume up to 5 million gallons per day, equivalent to the water use of a town populated by 10,000 to 50,000 people." Also "Most data centers use a combination of chillers and on-site cooling towers," and data center water can come from "piped sources such as municipal water." EESI's own source for the 5 million figure is a Washington Post article from 25 April 2023. https://eesi.org/articles/view/data-centers-and-water-consumption
- Backup: the Susquehanna River Basin Commission (Stacey Hanrahan, August 2025) says "a single data center facility can use more than 5 million gallons of water per day." https://www.srbc.gov/about/news/docs/data-centers-lets-talk-about-water-demands.pdf

**5. Why it has to be clean water (Segment 6)**
- Minerals and salt left behind by evaporation clog and corrode cooling systems, and seawater isn't a suitable substitute for standard evaporative towers: Friends of the Earth. https://foe.org/blog/why-do-data-centers-need-fresh-water/
- Some sites do use seawater with special designs, which is why "most of them can't" is the right wording: Google's Hamina, Finland data center (https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/google-launches-seawater-cooled-finland-data-center/) and Data Center Knowledge, 16 March 2026 (https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/cooling/turning-off-the-tap-the-case-for-seawater-cooling-in-data-centers).

**6. Where the water comes from and goes (Segment 7)**
- Public water systems: EESI (above) on municipal water, and Yuelin Han, Pengfei Li, Adam Wierman and Shaolei Ren, "Small Bottle, Big Pipe: Quantifying and Addressing the Impact of Data Centers on Public Water Systems," arXiv 2603.02705 (2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02705
- Evaporated water counts as "consumed" because it isn't returned to the local basin. The Susquehanna River Basin Commission defines consumptive use as water "used but not returned to the basin" (link above).

**7. Corrections / careful wording**
- **"3 to 5 million gallons a day"** (your first dictation) is "up to 5 million gallons of water a day" for a large data center, credited to EESI. You read it that way in the read-through.
- **"Most of them can't"** use seawater stays, because a few sites use seawater or treated wastewater.
- **"Almost like 50,000 kettles"** is your wording. The maths gives exactly 50,000, and "almost like" is fine as a spoken comparison.
- **"That's kind of why cooling towers need clean, fresh water"** is fine as you said it. Standard evaporative towers need fresh water, and the special seawater setups are covered by "most of them can't."
