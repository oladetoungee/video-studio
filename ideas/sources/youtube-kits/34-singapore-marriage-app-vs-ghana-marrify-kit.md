**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT SINGAPORE'S FIRSTDATE APP IS]**

FirstDate is a dating service run by GovTech, which is Singapore's Government Technology Agency.

GovTech emailed public officers about it on the 29th of September 2026, and it's a pilot.

So as of October 2026, only people who work in Singapore's public service can use it.

Ordinary Singaporeans can't sign up, and GovTech says it hasn't decided whether it will go beyond the pilot.

At the start, you had to be between 21 and 35.

On the 20th of October, they raised the limit to 45 for the second round of sign-ups.

And you have to be single, widowed or divorced.

The idea came out of GovTech's yearly hackathon, which is an event where staff build and test new ideas quickly.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the next part is the faceless, one-match design, which is the reason most people clicked]**

This is how it works.

You sign in with Singpass.

Singpass is Singapore's national digital ID, the login people use for government services.

So every account on FirstDate belongs to a real, verified person.

Then you answer a questionnaire, about 30 questions on your interests, your habits, your values, and what you're looking for.

You can use a nickname instead of your real name.

Then the algorithm gives you one match.

There's no feed of faces to swipe through. You get one person.

You see their profile name, their interests, a compatibility score, and a short note about the match.

You don't see their face.

Both of you get an SMS, and you each have three days to say yes or no.

If you both say yes, FirstDate shares your Telegram contacts with each other.

If either of you says no, that match ends, and you might get another one in a later round.

They also won't match you with someone from your own agency, so you won't be paired with the person who sits next to you at work.

There are optional "date quests," which are suggested things to do on the date, and during some periods there are vouchers for doing them.

And after each match, they send you a survey asking how it went, including whether you actually met in person.

FirstDate's own website says, "Swipe fatigue ends here."

**[SEGMENT 3: WHY SINGAPORE'S GOVERNMENT IS PLAYING MATCHMAKER]**

So why would Singapore's government build a dating app?

Most news reports connected it to Singapore's birth rate, and the numbers explain why.

In 2025, Singapore's resident total fertility rate fell to 0.87.

That's the average number of babies a woman is expected to have in her lifetime, and the year before, it was 0.97.

**[ON SCREEN: Singapore resident total fertility rate / 2024: 0.97 / 2025: 0.87 / Source: Singapore Department of Statistics]**

For a population to replace itself without immigration, that number usually needs to be around 2.1.

Marriages are falling too.

24,688 marriages were registered in Singapore in 2025, which is 6.2% fewer than in 2024.

And in February, Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong warned that if nothing changes, the citizen population could start to shrink by the early 2040s.

To be fair to GovTech, FirstDate's website doesn't mention birth rates at all.

It talks about supporting singles on their dating journey.

But this is also something Singapore's government has done before.

In 1984, it set up the Social Development Unit, because fewer female university graduates were getting married, and the unit helped graduates meet each other.

A year later, another body was set up to do the same for people without degrees, and the two merged in 2009.

So the government matching people is more than 40 years old in Singapore.

FirstDate is the version with Singpass and an algorithm.

And Singapore isn't alone. Tokyo's city government launched its own dating app in 2024, and Seoul runs matchmaking events for young people too.

**[SEGMENT 4: HOW THE GALE-SHAPLEY ALGORITHM WORKS]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the algorithm explanation is the payoff the intro promised, and it's the part nobody else covering this story explains]**

FirstDate matches people with something called the Gale-Shapley algorithm.

It's also called the stable marriage algorithm.

Two mathematicians, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, published it in 1962.

In 2012, Shapley shared the Nobel Prize in economics with Alvin Roth, for work that includes this kind of matching.

And it's used for serious things.

In the US, versions of it are used to match new doctors to hospitals, and some cities use it to assign children to schools.

So how does it work?

Picture two groups of three people, and everyone ranks the people in the other group, from first choice to last.

**[ON SCREEN: preference table]**
```
Ada:    Tunde, Femi, Kola        Tunde: Bisi, Ada, Chioma
Bisi:   Tunde, Kola, Femi        Femi:  Ada, Chioma, Bisi
Chioma: Femi, Tunde, Kola        Kola:  Ada, Bisi, Chioma
```

In the first round, everyone on one side sends an offer to their first choice.

If you receive offers, you don't say yes yet.

You hold on to the best offer you've got so far, like a "maybe," and you reject the rest.

Then everyone who got rejected sends an offer to their next choice.

And if someone holding a "maybe" gets a better offer, they can drop the old one and keep the new one.

It goes round and round like that until nobody gets rejected anymore, and then everyone is matched.

So in my example, Ada and Bisi both send their first offer to Tunde.

Tunde prefers Bisi, so he holds Bisi and rejects Ada.

Ada tries her second choice, Femi.

Femi was holding Chioma's offer, but he prefers Ada, so he swaps.

Chioma tries Tunde next, but Tunde still prefers Bisi, so Chioma tries Kola, and Kola holds her.

Nobody is rejected anymore, so it stops.

Bisi with Tunde, Ada with Femi, and Chioma with Kola.

**[CODE ON SCREEN]**
```js
// Gale-Shapley stable matching, simplified
function stableMatch(proposers, receivers) {
  const free = Object.keys(proposers); // everyone starts unmatched
  const next = {};                     // where each person is on their list
  const holding = {};                  // receiver -> offer they're holding
  while (free.length) {
    const p = free.shift();
    next[p] = (next[p] ?? -1) + 1;
    const r = proposers[p][next[p]];   // their next choice
    const current = holding[r];
    if (!current) {
      holding[r] = p;                  // first offer: "maybe"
    } else if (receivers[r].indexOf(p) < receivers[r].indexOf(current)) {
      holding[r] = p;                  // better offer: swap
      free.push(current);
    } else {
      free.push(p);                    // worse offer: rejected
    }
  }
  return holding;
}
// returns { Tunde: "Bisi", Femi: "Ada", Kola: "Chioma" }
```

The important word here is "stable."

A matching is stable when there are no two people who would both rather be with each other than with the person they got.

Chioma would have preferred Femi or Tunde, but both of them prefer the person they already have.

So nobody has a reason to break away from their match.

There's one more detail that matters.

Gale and Shapley showed that the side sending the offers gets the best partner it could get in any stable matching.

So who sends the offers matters.

GovTech hasn't said how FirstDate turns your questionnaire answers into a ranking, or which side sends the offers, so I can't tell you that part.

And a stable match on paper is still only on paper.

FirstDate's own website says compatibility on paper doesn't guarantee chemistry.

**[SEGMENT 5: WHAT DATA FIRSTDATE COLLECTS AND WHO SEES IT]**

When I made my Marrify video, the question I kept asking was who can see your data, and how much of it.

So I looked at Singapore's FirstDate the same way.

This is what GovTech holds about you if you sign up.

It knows who you really are, because you logged in with Singpass.

It knows you're single and looking.

It has your answers about your interests, your habits, your values and your preferences.

It knows who you were matched with, and from the survey, whether you went on the date.

And this is who sees what.

Your profile is only visible to your current match.

Your contact details are only shared if both of you say yes.

And your match doesn't see your face before deciding.

FirstDate says your data is handled under government data security standards, and that it won't be sold or shared with third parties, unless the law requires or allows it.

You can also deactivate your account and ask for your information to be removed.

In my Marrify video, I talked about data minimisation, which means showing only the minimum data needed to answer the question.

FirstDate is close to that.

Your match only sees what they need to decide on one date.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And this next part is the one I didn't expect when I started reading about it.]**

There's one thing to know about the law, though.

Singapore's main data protection law, the PDPA, doesn't apply to government agencies.

Public agencies follow other rules instead, including the Public Sector (Governance) Act.

Singapore's government told Parliament in 2019 that these rules set standards comparable to the PDPA, if not higher.

And at that time, a public officer who misused data could face a fine of up to 5,000 dollars, or up to two years in jail, or both.

So for me, the bigger question is who's holding the data.

Everyone using FirstDate works for the government.

So your dating answers sit with the government, and the government is also your employer.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the comparison is the video's main promise, so it lands here]**

So how does Singapore's FirstDate compare with Ghana's Marrify?

A quick reminder of Marrify, in case you haven't seen that video.

It launched in Accra at the end of August 2026.

It's run by a private company, Marry Right Ghana Limited, in partnership with Ghana's Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection.

It pulls marriage and divorce records from the Registrar-General's Department, the courts, local assemblies and the Ghana Gazette.

So you can check whether someone already has a marriage on record, and whether a pastor, an imam or a venue is licensed to conduct a marriage.

The founder, Dennis Adjei Dwomoh, said at the launch that you can find that information for free.

Since my video, I've also seen reports that it has an AI assistant that answers questions about Ghanaian marriage law.

And as of October 2026, the database is still being filled, so an empty search doesn't prove that someone has never married.

Now, the differences.

The first difference is the purpose.

FirstDate helps you meet someone, and Marrify helps you check someone.

The second difference is who's behind it.

FirstDate is built by a government agency.

Marrify is a private company working with a ministry, using records that come from government offices and the courts.

The third difference is whose data it is, and to me this is the biggest one.

On FirstDate, every piece of data comes from someone who chose to sign up.

On Marrify, the records are about people who never signed up for anything.

Their marriage or divorce was registered somewhere, and now it can be searched.

The fourth difference is who can see it.

On FirstDate, only your match sees your profile.

Marrify is built for the public to search.

And the fifth difference is how each one builds trust.

FirstDate checks who you are with Singpass.

Marrify pulls from official records, so you don't have to rely on what someone tells you about themselves.

**[SEGMENT 7: WHAT THESE TWO APPS SAY ABOUT SINGAPORE AND GHANA]**

I always say that technology is not neutral.

It works in the system you use it in.

And I think Singapore's FirstDate and Ghana's Marrify show you a lot about the countries they were built in.

In Singapore, I think the problem FirstDate is trying to solve is that fewer people are meeting and marrying, and the government is worried about what that means for the future.

And the solution expects people to trust their government enough to log in with their national ID and tell it what they want in a partner.

Tingting Liu, a researcher at the University of Technology Sydney, described it as "a particularly Singaporean faith in systems."

In Ghana, I think the problem Marrify is trying to solve is different.

The records already exist, but they're spread across different offices, and it's possible for someone to hide a marriage.

Ghana's minister, Dr Agnes Naa Momo Lartey, said the effectiveness of a platform like this "will ultimately depend on public confidence in the information it provides."

So in Singapore, the trust question is about you trusting the government with your data.

In Ghana, it's about trusting the information you find about someone else.

## 5. MAIN SHORT (~90 sec, covers the whole video)

**[HOOK]**

Singapore's government built a dating app, and it gives you one match at a time, with no face.

**[WHAT IT IS]**

It's called FirstDate, it's run by GovTech, and as of October 2026 it's a pilot only for public officers. You log in with Singpass, answer about 30 questions, and get one match with a compatibility score. You both have three days to say yes.

**[THE ALGORITHM]**

The matching uses the Gale-Shapley algorithm from 1962. One side sends offers, the other side holds the best one as a "maybe" and rejects the rest, and it repeats until nobody's rejected. The result is stable, which means no two people would both rather be with each other.

### SHORT 3: The stable marriage algorithm (35-45 sec)

Singapore's dating app uses an algorithm from 1962 called Gale-Shapley.

Everyone ranks the people on the other side.

One side sends offers to their first choice.

If you get offers, you hold the best one as a "maybe" and reject the rest.

Everyone rejected tries their next choice, and if a better offer comes, you can swap.

It repeats until nobody's rejected.

The result is stable, which means there are no two people who would both rather be with each other than with the match they got.

---

A while ago I made a video about Ghana's Marrify, a platform launched in Accra at the end of August 2026 that lets you check whether someone already has a marriage on record. It is run by a private company, Marry Right Ghana Limited, in partnership with Ghana's Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection, and it pulls marriage and divorce records from the Registrar-General's Department, the courts, local assemblies and the Ghana Gazette. A month later, Singapore's Government Technology Agency, GovTech, launched something that sounds similar and turns out to be almost the opposite. It is called FirstDate, and it is a government dating app.

As of October 2026, FirstDate is a pilot for people who work in Singapore's public service. You sign in with Singpass, the national digital ID, so every account belongs to a verified person. You answer about thirty questions on your interests, habits, values and preferences, and you can use a nickname. Then you get one match, with a compatibility score and a short note, but no photo of their face. Both of you get an SMS and have three days to say yes, and only if you both do are your Telegram contacts shared. It will not match you with someone from your own agency. The first round was for people aged 21 to 35, and on 20 October GovTech raised the limit to 45 for the second round. The idea came out of GovTech's yearly hackathon, and its website promises "Swipe fatigue ends here."

Most coverage connected it to Singapore's birth rate. The Department of Statistics reports that the resident total fertility rate fell from 0.97 in 2024 to 0.87 in 2025, and 24,688 marriages were registered in 2025, 6.2% fewer than the year before. FirstDate's own website does not mention birth rates, and it describes its aim as supporting singles on their dating journey. Singapore has done this before. In 1984 the government set up the Social Development Unit because fewer female graduates were marrying, a second body for non-graduates followed in 1985, and the two merged in 2009.

The matching uses the Gale-Shapley algorithm, published by David Gale and Lloyd Shapley in 1962, and Shapley later shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in economics with Alvin Roth. Everyone ranks the people on the other side. One side sends offers to its first choices, and each person receiving offers holds the best one as a "maybe" and rejects the rest. Rejected people move down their list, people holding a "maybe" can swap it for a better offer, and the process repeats until nobody is rejected. The result is stable, which means there are no two people who would both rather be with each other than with the person they got. Gale and Shapley also showed that the side sending offers gets the best partner it could have in any stable matching, and GovTech has not said how FirstDate turns questionnaire answers into rankings or which side sends the offers. Its website admits that compatibility on paper does not guarantee chemistry.

In my Marrify video, my main questions were who can see your data and how much of it, so I looked at FirstDate the same way. GovTech holds your verified identity, the fact that you are single and looking, your answers, who you were matched with, and from its follow-up survey, whether you met. But your profile is visible only to your current match, contacts are shared only by mutual agreement, the data is not sold or shared with third parties unless the law requires or allows it, and you can ask for it to be removed. That is close to data minimisation, the principle of showing only what is needed to answer the question. The part that gives me pause is that Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act does not apply to public agencies, which follow other rules such as the Public Sector (Governance) Act, and every FirstDate user works for the government that holds their answers.

Put the two apps side by side and the biggest difference, to me, is whose data it is. Everyone on FirstDate chose to be there. The people in Marrify's records never signed up for anything, because their marriage or divorce was registered somewhere and is now searchable. FirstDate builds trust by checking who you are. Marrify builds trust by pulling from official records, so you are not relying on what someone tells you about themselves. I think each app also reflects the problem its country is trying to solve. Tingting Liu of the University of Technology Sydney described FirstDate as showing "a particularly Singaporean faith in systems." In Ghana, the records already exist but are scattered, and the minister, Dr Agnes Naa Momo Lartey, said the platform's effectiveness "will ultimately depend on public confidence in the information it provides."

There is a strong case against a government dating app. According to the BBC, some Singaporeans called FirstDate "dystopian," and Pan Wang of the University of New South Wales said the government is "taking the role of big parent." She also doubted it would lift the birth rate while housing costs, living costs and work pressure remain. Teo Kai Xiang told Al Jazeera that cost is a bigger barrier to dating than a lack of matchmaking, and Jean Liu of the Singapore Institute of Technology said questionnaires predict attraction poorly and the process may break down with too few sign-ups, though she sees value in the government publishing what it learns about dating. And on Marrify's side, finding out after a wedding that your partner was already married is a serious harm.

Personally, I wouldn't use FirstDate. I don't want an algorithm to find my love. I have tried dating apps and didn't like them, and I don't think the love of my life is on a dating app. I'm still very interested in it, because I look at things like this as a UX and HCI person who cares about user privacy and about how technology finds its way into our lives. If your government built a dating app, would you sign up?
