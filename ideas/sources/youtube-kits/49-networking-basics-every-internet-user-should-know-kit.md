**[SEGMENT 3: WI-FI VS INTERNET (ROUTER, MODEM AND YOUR ISP)]**

Wi-Fi and the internet are two different things, and that's why your phone can say "connected, no internet."

Think of your home as a house on a street.

Wi-Fi is like the hallways inside the house.

It connects your phone, your laptop and your TV to the router, and it stops around your walls.

The router is like the receptionist at the front desk.

It knows every device in the house, and it passes messages in and out for them.

The modem is the front gate.

It connects your house to the fibre, cable or phone line coming in from outside.

And your internet service provider, the company you pay every month, owns the road from your gate to the rest of the internet.

**[ON SCREEN: phone, laptop, TV / Wi-Fi (hallways) / router (receptionist) / modem (front gate) / ISP (the road) / the internet]**

In a lot of homes, the router and the modem are inside one box from your provider, so it looks like one thing.

And if you use a MiFi, one of those small pocket routers, that little device is the router and the modem together, and it connects to a mobile network instead of a cable.

So when your phone says "connected, no internet," your phone can reach the router, and the hallway is fine.

The problem is further out, at the modem, the line, or your provider.

One quick test helps here.

If every device in the house has lost internet, the problem is probably the router, the modem or your provider, and restarting the router and modem is the first thing to try.

If only one device has the problem, look at that device first.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: 2.4 GHZ VS 5 GHZ WI-FI]**

Most Wi-Fi routers today send Wi-Fi on two radio bands, 2.4 gigahertz and 5 gigahertz, and some newer ones add 6 gigahertz.

You might see them as two network names, like "Home" and "Home-5G."

That 5G means 5 gigahertz, and it has nothing to do with 5G mobile data.

2.4 gigahertz reaches further and gets through walls better, but it's slower.

5 gigahertz is faster, but it has a shorter range, and walls and floors weaken it more.

It's a bit like music from your neighbour's party.

Through the wall, you hear the bass, but you can't make out the singer's words.

Lower frequencies get through walls better, and that's the same reason 2.4 travels further.

**[ON SCREEN: 2.4 GHz, further, through walls, slower / 5 GHz, faster, shorter range]**

2.4 gigahertz also gets crowded.

Your neighbours' Wi-Fi uses it, Bluetooth devices use it, and microwave ovens work in that same part of the airwaves.

Cisco has a guide on Wi-Fi interference that says a microwave running near your router or your device can slow the Wi-Fi down a lot.

So when you're close to the router, 5 gigahertz is usually the better one.

When you're far away, 2.4 might be the one that keeps working.

A lot of routers now put both bands under one name and move your devices between them for you, and Apple recommends doing exactly that, one name for all the bands.

**[SEGMENT 5: WHAT IS AN IP ADDRESS? (PUBLIC VS PRIVATE)]**

An IP address is the address a device uses so data can find it on a network.

Every message on the internet carries the address it's going to and the address it came from, the same way a letter does.

And your devices have two kinds of IP address.

The private one is like a room number inside your house.

Your router gives one to each device, and at home it usually starts with 192.168 or 10.

These addresses only work inside your home network, and the same numbers are used in millions of other homes.

The public one is like your house's street address.

That's the one websites see, and in most homes, every device in the house shares it.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer is about to see their own two addresses, which is a real "try this now" moment]**

You can see both of yours right now.

Search "what is my IP" on Google, and the number you see is your public address.

Then open your phone's Wi-Fi settings, tap the network you're on, and you'll see a private one, something like 192.168.1.23.

**[ON SCREEN: Google "what is my IP" result next to a phone's Wi-Fi details screen showing 192.168.x.x. Take your own screenshots and blur the public one.]**

On a laptop, you can see the private one too.

**[ON SCREEN, optional]**
```
Windows:  ipconfig
Mac:      ipconfig getifaddr en0
```

The private address comes from something called DHCP.

When you join a Wi-Fi network, your router gives your device an address for a while, a bit like a hotel receptionist giving you a room key when you check in.

That's why you never have to type one in yourself.

The reason we have private and public addresses at all is that the original kind of IP address, IPv4, only has about 4.3 billion of them, and that isn't enough for every device on earth.

The central pool of free IPv4 addresses ran out in February 2011.

The newer version, IPv6, has far more addresses, and in March 2026, for the first time, just over half of the people reaching Google did it over IPv6, according to the Internet Society's Pulse.

**[SEGMENT 6: WHAT IS NAT? (HOW YOUR HOME SHARES ONE IP ADDRESS)]**

NAT, which stands for network address translation, is how all the devices in your home share one public IP address.

Picture your router as the receptionist at the front desk of your house again.

Your laptop wants a video from YouTube, so it hands the request to the receptionist.

The receptionist crosses out your laptop's room number, writes the house's street address on it, and sends it out.

Then it writes a line in its notebook, saying the laptop asked YouTube for this.

When YouTube replies to the street address, the receptionist checks the notebook and takes the reply to your laptop's room.

**[ON SCREEN: a notebook page / laptop, room 23, asked YouTube / phone, room 41, asked Instagram / TV, room 12, asked Netflix]**

Your phone and your TV are doing the same thing at the same time, and the notebook keeps all their replies apart.

One side effect is that a stranger on the internet usually can't start a conversation with your laptop directly, because the receptionist has no line in the notebook saying which room it's for.

That helps, but NAT wasn't designed as security, so your router's firewall and its updates still matter.

**[SEGMENT 7: MOBILE DATA VS WI-FI (AND WHY YOUR IP ADDRESS CHANGES)]**

When you switch your phone from Wi-Fi to mobile data, your traffic leaves through a different gate.

On Wi-Fi, your phone talks to your router, and everything goes out through your home internet.

On mobile data, your phone talks to a cell tower, and everything goes out through your mobile network.

That's why your public IP address changes when you switch.

Try it.

Search "what is my IP" on Wi-Fi, then turn Wi-Fi off and search again on mobile data, and you'll get two different addresses.

Many mobile networks also share one public address between lots of their customers.

It's the same NAT idea from the house, on a much bigger scale, and it's called carrier-grade NAT.

So the address a website sees when you're on mobile data could be shared with many strangers on the same network, and it can change from time to time, which is normal.

**[~6:00 BEAT: next is the "Private Wi-Fi Address" setting most people have seen on their phone and never understood, then the safety half of the video]**

**[SEGMENT 8: WHAT IS A MAC ADDRESS?]**

A MAC address is an ID for your device's network chip, and it's used on the local network, like your home Wi-Fi or the café Wi-Fi.

If your IP address is your room number in the house, the MAC address is like the name tag your device wears.

The router uses it to tell the devices on the network apart.

It's written as six pairs of letters and numbers, something like A4:83:E7:2B:91:0C.

**[ON SCREEN: example only, A4:83:E7:2B:91:0C]**

Your MAC address doesn't travel out to the websites you visit.

It stays on the local network you're connected to.

But whoever runs that network can see it, and if your phone used the same MAC address on every network, it could be used to recognise your phone as you move between them.

That's why iPhones, since iOS 14, use something Apple calls a private Wi-Fi address.

Your phone shows a different MAC address to each network, and Apple says it's there to reduce that kind of tracking.

Newer Android phones do something similar by default.

So if you've seen "Private Wi-Fi Address" in your settings, that's what it is, and for most people it's fine to leave it on.

**[SEGMENT 9: WHAT IS DNS? (AND PORTS)]**

DNS, the domain name system, turns website names into IP addresses.

Computers find each other with IP addresses, but people remember names.

So when you type youtube.com, your device first asks a DNS server for the IP address of youtube.com, and then it connects to that address.

It's like the contacts on your phone.

You tap "Mum," your phone dials the number, and you never have to remember the number yourself.

Usually your internet provider runs the DNS server your devices use, unless you've changed it to one like Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 or Google's 8.8.8.8.

**[ON SCREEN: youtube.com, DNS, 142.250.x.x (example)]**

When DNS isn't working, it can look like the whole internet is down, even when your connection is fine, because your device can't turn any name into an address.

I walked through DNS step by step in my video on what happens when you type a URL and press enter, so watch that one if you want the full journey.

Ports get one line here.

If an IP address is like a company's phone number, a port is like the extension, and it tells the computer which program the message is for.

Secure websites use port 443, and your browser adds it for you, so you never type it.

And if you're a developer, my video on what happens when you send an API request follows one request from your app all the way to a server and back.

**[SEGMENT 10: WHAT THE HTTPS PADLOCK MEANS]**

HTTPS is the "https" at the start of a web address, and it means the connection between your browser and the website is encrypted.

Encrypted means scrambled, so anyone in the middle, like the café Wi-Fi owner or your internet provider, can't read the pages you open, the passwords you type, or your card details.

According to the Electronic Frontier Foundation, the EFF, they can usually still see which website you're on and when you visited.

They can't see the specific pages, or what you send.

For years, browsers showed a padlock for this, and a lot of people read it as "this website is safe."

In 2023, Google said that in its own research from 2021, only 11% of study participants correctly understood what the lock icon meant.

Google also said the lock icon doesn't show whether a website is trustworthy, and Chrome replaced it with a different icon from Chrome 117.

**[ON SCREEN: "only 11% of study participants correctly understood the precise meaning of the lock icon" (Chromium Blog, May 2023)]**

The FTC, the US consumer protection agency, warns that scammers create fake websites and encrypt them to make you think they're safe.

So the padlock tells you nobody in the middle can read what you send.

It tells you nothing about who is on the other end, and a fake bank website can have one too.

Before you type a password, check the website's name, letter by letter.

**[SEGMENT 11: IS PUBLIC WI-FI SAFE?]**

Public Wi-Fi, at a café, an airport or a hotel, is a lot safer than it used to be, because most websites and apps now encrypt their traffic.

The FTC's advice now says that connecting through a public Wi-Fi network is usually safe, for that reason.

What's left are a few risks you can avoid.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the fake network trick is the one most people have never heard of, and VPNs and the slow Wi-Fi fixes are still to come]**

The first one is a fake network.

Someone can set up a hotspot with a name like "Airport Free WiFi" and wait for people to join.

People call this an "evil twin," and the Florida Attorney General's office warned people about it in 2024.

So ask the staff for the exact network name before you join.

The second one is a sign-in page that asks for too much.

If a Wi-Fi sign-in page asks for your email password or your card details, close it.

The third one is a website without HTTPS, because what you type there isn't encrypted.

**[ON SCREEN: fake network names / sign-in pages asking for passwords / sites without https]**

A few habits cover most of the rest.

Turn on two-factor authentication for your important accounts, and keep your phone updated, which is also what the FTC recommends.

If you need to do your banking and you don't trust the network, switch to mobile data for those few minutes.

And turn off auto-join for networks you only used once.

---

**[SEGMENT 12: WHAT A VPN DOES AND DOESN'T DO]**

A VPN, a virtual private network, puts your internet traffic in an encrypted tunnel to a VPN company's server, and your traffic goes out to the internet from there.

So the café Wi-Fi owner, or your internet provider, can see that you're connected to a VPN, and not much else.

And websites see the VPN server's IP address and location instead of yours.

That's why people use VPNs to look like they're browsing from another country.

There are also things a VPN doesn't do.

The EFF puts it plainly: "A VPN is not a tool for anonymity."

If you're logged into Google or Instagram, they still know it's you, and cookies can still follow you around.

The VPN company can see the traffic your internet provider used to see, so you're moving your trust from one company to another, and the EFF warns that a VPN provider could sell that information, just like an internet provider could.

A VPN also won't stop you from typing your password into a fake website, or from downloading something harmful.

**[ON SCREEN: a VPN hides your browsing from the Wi-Fi owner and your ISP, and changes your IP / it doesn't make you anonymous, stop fake websites, or hide you from the VPN company]**

With free VPN apps, be extra careful.

The FTC suggests checking an app's permissions, and whether it shares your information with other companies, before you install it.

In my opinion, a VPN is useful on networks you don't trust, or when you want to hide your browsing from your provider.

For everyday browsing at home, HTTPS is already doing the encryption part for most websites.

**[SEGMENT 13: WHY YOUR WI-FI IS SLOW (AND SIMPLE FIXES)]**

When your Wi-Fi is slow, it usually comes down to four things.

The first one is distance, because Wi-Fi gets weaker the further you are from the router.

The second one is what's in the way.

Thick concrete walls, brick, metal and even big mirrors weaken the signal, and a router hidden in a cupboard or behind the TV is in a bad spot.

The third one is interference.

That's your neighbours' Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and microwaves, and Ofcom, the UK's communications regulator, also tells people to keep the router away from cordless phones, halogen lamps, TVs and dimmer switches.

The fourth one is too many devices.

Everything on your Wi-Fi shares the same radio, so they take turns, and Ofcom points out that phones and tablets often use the internet in the background.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: So if your Wi-Fi is slow right now, try these as I go through them.]**

Here are the fixes, starting with the easiest.

First, restart the router and the modem.

Second, move the router somewhere central, high up and out in the open, away from the TV and the microwave.

Third, when you're close to the router, use 5 gigahertz.

Fourth, plug in a cable for things that don't move, like a TV or a desktop computer, because Ofcom says a wired connection usually gives you faster speeds than Wi-Fi.

Fifth, disconnect devices you're not using, and don't start big downloads during an important video call.

And if your home is big or has lots of walls, a mesh Wi-Fi system, which is a few routers working together as one network, can cover the rooms one router can't reach.

**[ON SCREEN: restart / move the router / 5 GHz up close / cable for things that don't move / fewer devices / mesh for big homes]**

To find out whether the problem is your Wi-Fi or your internet, run a speed test standing right next to the router, or on a cable.

If it's slow even there, the problem is your plan or your provider, so call them.

If it's fast next to the router and slow in your room, the problem is your Wi-Fi, and the fixes above are the ones to try.

One last thing that confuses a lot of people.

Internet speeds are measured in megabits per second, but downloads show megabytes, and there are 8 bits in a byte.

So a 100 megabit connection downloads at about 12.5 megabytes a second at best, and nothing is wrong with it.

**[ON SCREEN: 100 Mbps ÷ 8 = about 12.5 MB/s]**

So tell me in the comments, which room in your house has the worst Wi-Fi, and what's between it and your router?

> Your Home Network Is a House With a Receptionist

I'm not a network engineer, and I don't think you need to be one to understand the network you use every day. Most of us only think about networking when something breaks, like a phone that says it's connected to Wi-Fi while nothing loads, or Wi-Fi that's fast in one room and useless in another. Both make sense once you have a simple picture of what's going on in your home, so this is the picture I use.

Think of your home as a house on a street. Wi-Fi is the hallways inside it, connecting your phone, laptop and TV to the router, and it stops around your walls. The router is the receptionist at the front desk, who knows every device in the house and passes messages in and out. The modem is the front gate, connecting the house to the fibre, cable or phone line coming from outside, and your internet provider owns the road beyond the gate. In many homes the router and modem live in one box, and a pocket MiFi device is both at once. So when your phone says "connected, no internet," the hallway is fine and the problem is further out, at the modem, the line or the provider. If every device in the house is affected, restarting the router and modem is the first thing to try.

Every device also needs an address. Inside your home, the router hands each device a private IP address, usually starting with 192.168 or 10, using something called DHCP, a bit like a hotel receptionist handing out room keys. IANA reserves those ranges for private networks, which is why the same numbers appear in millions of homes. The address websites see is your public IP address, the street address the whole house shares. You can see both by searching "what is my IP" and then checking your phone's Wi-Fi details. The reason we share addresses at all is that the original IPv4 system only has about 4.3 billion of them, and the central pool of free ones ran out in February 2011. The newer IPv6 has far more, and in March 2026 just over half of the people reaching Google used it for the first time, according to the Internet Society's Pulse.

Sharing one public address works because of NAT, network address translation. When your laptop asks YouTube for a video, the router swaps the laptop's room number for the street address, sends the request, and writes down who asked for what. When the reply comes back, it checks its notes and delivers it to the right room. Mobile networks do the same thing on a bigger scale, which is why your public IP changes when you switch from Wi-Fi to mobile data and why it may be shared with many strangers. Inside each local network, devices also have a MAC address, a kind of name tag for the network chip, which never reaches the websites you visit. Since iOS 14, iPhones show each network a different one, which Apple says is meant to reduce tracking, and newer Android phones do something similar.

Names come into it through DNS, which turns youtube.com into the IP address your device connects to, much like the contacts on your phone turn "Mum" into a number. When DNS fails, the whole internet can look down even though the connection is fine. After that, HTTPS encrypts what travels between your browser and the website, so the café owner or your provider can usually see which site you're on but not which pages or what you type, as the EFF describes it. The padlock that used to show this was widely misread. Google wrote in 2023 that only 11% of participants in its research understood its precise meaning, said it does not indicate trustworthiness, and replaced it in Chrome 117. The FTC warns that scammers encrypt fake websites too, so the address bar is still worth reading letter by letter.

That widespread encryption is also why the FTC now says connecting through public Wi-Fi is usually safe. The risks that remain are mostly about being tricked, like a fake hotspot named to look like the airport's, a sign-in page asking for passwords it has no reason to want, or a site without HTTPS. Two-factor authentication, an updated phone, and switching to mobile data for banking on a network you don't trust cover most of it. A VPN adds a tunnel to a VPN company's server, hiding your browsing from the Wi-Fi owner and your provider and changing the IP address websites see. The EFF is direct that "a VPN is not a tool for anonymity," because your logins and cookies still identify you, and the VPN provider can now see what your provider used to see.

Slow Wi-Fi usually comes down to distance, things in the way like concrete, metal and mirrors, interference from neighbours' networks, Bluetooth and microwave ovens, and too many devices taking turns on the same radio. 2.4 GHz reaches further and gets through walls better, while 5 GHz is faster at shorter range. Ofcom's advice is to keep the router away from things like cordless phones, halogen lamps and TVs, and to use a cable where you can. Restarting the router, moving it somewhere central and high, and running a speed test right next to it will tell you whether to fix your Wi-Fi or call your provider. And if your 100 megabit plan downloads at around 12.5 megabytes a second, it's working, because there are 8 bits in a byte.

I'd like to know which room in your house has the worst Wi-Fi, and what sits between it and your router.
