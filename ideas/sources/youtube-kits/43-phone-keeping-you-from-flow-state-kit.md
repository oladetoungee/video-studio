**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

I was telling someone the other day that I have achieved flow states so many times in my life, even before I knew what flow state was.

I've had times in my life where I literally got into states that I would describe as flow states, and I really enjoy being in that state.

I haven't been in that state as consistently as I would want, over the past few years at least.

Then I was watching this Arthur Brooks video about how your phone addiction is keeping you from achieving flow state, and he has a test in it that I want you to try with me.

And of course, in this video, we'll talk about what flow state even is, what your phone does to it, and how to get back into it.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT FLOW STATE IS]**

So what is flow state?

Flow is the state where you're so absorbed in what you're doing that everything else drops away, and when you look up, hours have passed.

The idea comes from a psychologist called Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi.

**[ON SCREEN: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (MEE-high CHEEK-sent-me-high), psychologist, author of "Flow" (1990)]**

He spent years studying the moments when people enjoyed what they were doing the most.

A lot of the people he talked to described those moments as being carried along by a current, and that's where the name flow comes from.

Csikszentmihalyi and his colleague Jeanne Nakamura described six things people experience in flow.

**[ON SCREEN: 1. intense focus on the present / 2. action and awareness merge / 3. you stop watching yourself / 4. a sense of control / 5. time feels different / 6. the activity is rewarding in itself]**

Three of them are the ones you'll recognise.

The first one is intense focus on what you're doing right now.

All your attention is in the task.

The second one is that time feels different.

Usually it flies. You sit down after lunch, and the next time you look up, it's dark outside.

The third one is that the activity feels rewarding in itself.

Csikszentmihalyi called this autotelic, which means you'd want to do it even if nobody paid you or praised you for it.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the three conditions come next, and the rest of the video hangs on them]**

Now, flow doesn't show up just because you want it to.

In the research, it tends to happen when three conditions are in place.

**[ON SCREEN: the 3 conditions for flow / 1. a clear goal / 2. immediate feedback / 3. a challenge that matches your skill]**

The first condition is a clear goal. You know what you're trying to do right now.

The second is immediate feedback. You can tell, moment by moment, whether what you're doing is working.

The third is a challenge that matches your skill. The task is hard enough to stretch you, but not so hard that you give up.

Think about a footballer in the middle of a match.

The goal is clear, literally.

Every pass tells them straight away if it worked.

And a match against a good team stretches them without crushing them.

If a task is too easy for you, you get bored. If it's too hard, you get anxious.

Flow happens in between those two.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: ARTHUR BROOKS'S ONE-HOUR PHONE TEST]**

Arthur Brooks, a social scientist who studies happiness, has a simple test for whether you're addicted to your phone.

He shares it in his Big Think video, "Your phone addiction is keeping you from achieving flow state."

Put your phone away for one hour, and count how many times you think about it.

If it's more than once every five minutes, he says you're addicted.

**[ON SCREEN: Arthur Brooks's test / put your phone away for 1 hour / count how many times you think about it / more than once every 5 minutes = addicted]**

Once every five minutes is twelve times in an hour.

So if the thought "let me just check my phone" comes thirteen times in that hour, by his test, you're there.

Brooks also talks about how almost nobody believes they're addicted to their phone.

My screen time is already low compared to the average person.

But I still want it lower, because when I look at what that time gives me, I don't see the benefit.

And this test doesn't ask about your screen time at all.

It asks how often your phone takes your mind, even when it's not in your hand.

"Addicted" is Brooks's word. Phone addiction isn't an official medical diagnosis, so I'm using this test as a mirror to look at myself, and not to diagnose anybody.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer now has something to do after the video, and a reason to come back and comment]**

So try it after this video, and tell me your number in the comments.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: HOW YOUR PHONE COMPETES WITH THE THREE CONDITIONS OF FLOW]**

Flow tends to happen when you have a clear goal, immediate feedback, and a challenge that matches your skill.

I think your phone competes with each one of those conditions.

Start with the clear goal.

When you sit down to work, you have one goal. Finish this chapter. Fix this bug. Write this section.

Your phone comes with a hundred small goals of its own.

Reply to this message. See who liked your post. Watch the video your friend just sent.

None of them is the goal you sat down with, and every one of them asks for a bit of your attention.

Then there's feedback.

Real work often gives you slow feedback. You write a paragraph, and you're not sure yet if it's any good.

Your phone gives you feedback all the time. A like, a reply, a new video that starts playing on its own.

It's quicker and it's easier, and I think that's why your real work can feel like nothing is happening when the phone is next to it.

And then there's the challenge.

Scrolling has almost no challenge, and that's part of why it feels relaxing.

This connects to something Csikszentmihalyi found that surprised me.

In a 1989 study with Judith LeFevre, they followed 78 working adults for a week, using pagers that beeped at random times and asked people what they were doing and how they felt.

Most of the flow experiences people reported happened while they were working, and very few happened in their free time.

**[ON SCREEN: "the great majority of flow experiences are reported when working, not when in leisure" (Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre, 1989)]**

But people were more motivated in their free time than at work.

So the things that give us flow usually take effort, and the thing we want in the moment is usually the easy thing.

And the easiest thing in the room is usually the phone.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And your phone can pull at your attention even when you don't touch it. That's the next study.]**

---

**[SEGMENT 5: A NOTIFICATION CAN DISTRACT YOU EVEN IF YOU DON'T CHECK IT]**

A phone notification can distract you even if you never pick up the phone.

In 2015, Cary Stothart and his colleagues at Florida State University gave 166 students an attention task on a computer.

Numbers flashed on the screen, and they had to press a key for every number except 3.

While the students worked, the researchers called or texted some of the students' own phones.

Nobody answered, and nobody read anything.

The students who got a call or a text made more mistakes on the task than the students who got nothing.

**[ON SCREEN: a call or text you don't answer = more mistakes on an attention task (Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert, 2015)]**

So just getting the notification was enough to pull their attention away.

Then in 2016, Kostadin Kushlev, Jason Proulx and Elizabeth Dunn tested this in everyday life.

221 university students spent one week with their phone alerts on and their phone close by.

They spent another week with alerts off, and the phone out of sight.

In the week with alerts on, they reported more inattention and more hyperactivity.

**[ON SCREEN: alerts on = more inattention and hyperactivity, self-reported (Kushlev, Proulx and Dunn, CHI 2016)]**

Those were the students' own reports, and the researchers were clear that this doesn't mean notifications cause ADHD.

**[OPTIONAL CUT START: brain drain lines, about 55 words. Clip 14 needs them.]**

You might also have heard of a 2017 study that said just having your phone on your desk drains your brainpower, even when it's switched off.

Later studies that tried to repeat it got mixed results, and some found no effect at all.

So I won't build anything on it. The notification studies are the ones I would lean on.

**[OPTIONAL CUT END]**

---

**[SEGMENT 6: THE PHONE INTERRUPTIONS WE GIVE OURSELVES]**

A lot of phone interruptions don't come from the phone. They come from us.

Gloria Mark is a researcher at the University of California, Irvine, who studies attention and interruptions.

She has said, "These kinds of self-interruptions happen almost as frequently as people are interrupted from external sources."

**[ON SCREEN: "These kinds of self-interruptions happen almost as frequently as people are interrupted from external sources." (Gloria Mark)]**

A self-interruption is you, in the middle of a task, picking up your phone with nothing pinging at all.

She has also said that when people get a lot of interruptions from outside in an hour, they keep interrupting themselves even after the outside interruptions slow down.

So a day full of pings can teach you to keep checking, even after the pings stop.

And every check costs you something when you come back to your work.

Sophie Leroy, a researcher who studies how people work, called it attention residue.

When you move from one task to another, part of your attention stays on the first one.

So you check one message, you go back to your work, and part of your mind is still on that message.

I talk about attention residue more in my video on switching between apps, so I'll link it.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the viewer now sees why flow keeps slipping, and the next segment explains why we reach for the phone at all]**

Now put that together with flow.

Flow needs a long stretch of attention on one thing.

If your mind goes to the phone every few minutes, and every check leaves a bit of residue behind, you rarely get that long stretch.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: WHY WE REACH FOR THE PHONE AT THE HARD PART]**

Have you noticed when you usually pick up your phone while you're working?

For a lot of us, it's at the hard part.

It's the sentence that won't come out, or the bug you can't find, or the paragraph you've read three times and still don't understand.

That moment feels uncomfortable, and the phone makes the feeling go away straight away.

Arthur Brooks talks about this in his video too.

Part of it is about how we use technology to numb uncomfortable feelings, like boredom, and he argues that those feelings have a purpose.

And for flow, I think that purpose is very clear.

Flow needs a task that stretches you, and that uncomfortable feeling is often what being stretched feels like.

So if you reach for your phone every time the work gets hard, you leave right around the point where flow could start.

**[OPTIONAL CUT START: monkey mind lines, about 30 words. Clip 19 needs them.]**

Brooks contrasts flow with what he calls the monkey mind.

Monkey mind is an old phrase for a restless mind that keeps jumping from one thing to the next.

**[OPTIONAL CUT END]**

---

**[SEGMENT 8: HOW TO GET BACK INTO FLOW STATE]**

So how do you get back into flow state?

These are the things I would tell you, and I'm working on them myself.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the practical part starts here, so viewers who stay get the payoff]**

The first one is to take Arthur Brooks's one-hour test, so you know where you're starting from, and write your number down.

The second one is to turn your alerts off and put the phone out of sight.

In the Kushlev study, that was the week the students reported less inattention.

Do Not Disturb is a good start, and if you can, put the phone in another room.

The third one is to set up the three conditions for flow before you start.

Give your session one clear goal.

"Work on my project" is too big. "Write the first draft of the introduction" is clear.

Then find a way to see your progress quickly, like a word count going up, tests passing, or small steps you can tick off.

And pick a level of challenge that stretches you a little.

If the task is too easy, give yourself a time limit. If it's too hard, break off a smaller piece.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: The next one is the one I think most of us skip.]**

The fourth one is to give flow enough time.

I don't think flow will show up in the ten minutes between two meetings, so block an hour or more if you can.

The fifth one is to stay with the hard part.

When the work gets hard and you want your phone, write the urge down on a piece of paper, and keep going for a few more minutes. See if it passes.

And the sixth one comes from Arthur Brooks.

In May 2026, he told CNN that his tech-free times are the first hour of the day, mealtimes, and the last hour before bed.

He also suggests turning off notifications, and setting your phone to grayscale, so the screen is less interesting to look at.

**[ON SCREEN: grayscale on iPhone / Settings > Accessibility > Display & Text Size > Color Filters > Grayscale]**

I read while I eat instead of watching something, and there is always a book in my bag.

So tell me in the comments, what was your number on the one-hour test? And when was the last time you were in flow?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> I Used to Reach Flow State All the Time

### Subtitle:
> What flow actually needs, what a phone does to each of those needs, and how I'm trying to get back into it.

### SEO Title:
> Why You Can't Get Into Flow State Anymore (and How to Get It Back)

### SEO Description:
> What flow state is, Arthur Brooks's one-hour phone test, the research on notifications and self-interruptions, and practical ways to get back into flow.

---

I was telling someone the other day that I have reached flow states so many times in my life, even before I knew what flow state was. I really enjoy being in that state. But I haven't reached it as consistently as I would want over the past few years, and around the same time, I watched Arthur Brooks's Big Think video, "Your phone addiction is keeping you from achieving flow state." So I went looking into what flow actually needs, and what a phone does to it.

Flow comes from the psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, who spent years studying the moments people enjoyed what they were doing the most. Many of the people he spoke to described being carried along by a current, which gave him the name. With Jeanne Nakamura, he described what people experience in flow, including intense focus on the present moment, a changed sense of time, and the feeling that the activity is rewarding in itself. Flow tends to show up when three conditions are in place. You have a clear goal, you get immediate feedback on how you're doing, and the challenge matches your skill, so it stretches you without breaking you. Too easy, and you get bored. Too hard, and you get anxious.

Arthur Brooks has a simple test for phone addiction. Put your phone away for one hour and count how many times you think about it. If it's more than once every five minutes, he says you're addicted. Addiction is his word, and phone addiction isn't an official diagnosis, but I like the test because it ignores screen time. My screen time is already low compared to the average person, and I still want it lower, because I don't see the benefit. The test asks how often the phone takes your mind when it isn't even in your hand, and that's the thing flow can't survive, because flow needs a long stretch of attention on one thing.

I think a phone competes with each of the three conditions. It brings a hundred small goals that aren't yours, like replying to a message or seeing who liked a post. It gives you faster and easier feedback than real work ever does. And scrolling has almost no challenge, which is why it feels restful. In 1989, Csikszentmihalyi and Judith LeFevre followed 78 working adults for a week with random beeps, and found that most of their flow happened at work and very little in their free time. Yet people were more motivated in their free time. The things that give us flow tend to take effort, and the phone is the easiest thing in any room.

It doesn't even need to be touched. In 2015, Cary Stothart, Mallorie Mitchum and Courtney Yehnert gave students a boring attention task and called or texted some of their phones while they worked. Nobody answered or read anything, and those students still made more mistakes. In 2016, Kostadin Kushlev, Jason Proulx and Elizabeth Dunn had students spend one week with alerts on and the phone nearby, and one week with alerts off and the phone out of sight. With alerts on, they reported more inattention and hyperactivity. I left out the well-known 2017 study claiming that a phone on the desk drains your brainpower, because later attempts to repeat it got mixed results.

A lot of the interruptions come from us. Gloria Mark has said that "these kinds of self-interruptions happen almost as frequently as people are interrupted from external sources," and that a busy hour of outside interruptions can keep people interrupting themselves after it calms down. Every check also leaves something behind. Sophie Leroy calls it attention residue, the part of your attention that stays with the last thing when you move to the next. And for many of us, the moment we reach for the phone is the hard part of the work, when the sentence won't come out. Brooks argues that the uncomfortable feelings we numb with technology have a purpose, and for flow, I think the purpose is clear, because being stretched often feels uncomfortable right before it starts to feel good.

So these are the things I would tell you, and the things I'm working on myself. Take Brooks's one-hour test and write your number down. Turn your alerts off and put the phone in another room. Give each session one clear goal, a quick way to see progress, and a challenge that stretches you a little. Block an hour or more, because I don't think flow will show up in ten minutes between meetings. When the work gets hard and you want your phone, write the urge down and keep going a few more minutes. And try Brooks's own tech-free times, which he told CNN are the first hour of the day, mealtimes, and the last hour before bed. I already read while I eat instead of watching something, and there's always a book in my bag.

I'd love to know your number on the one-hour test, and when you were last in flow.
