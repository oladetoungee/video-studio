**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

You read a chapter, you close the book, and you feel like you know it.

Then someone asks you one question about it, and nothing comes.

Or you're halfway through explaining something, and a little voice in your head says, wait, do I really understand this?

That little voice has a name. It's called metacognition.

In this video, I'll explain what it is, why your feeling of "I know this" can be wrong, and simple ways to practise it, including when you use AI.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT IS METACOGNITION?]**

Metacognition means thinking about your own thinking.

It's the part of you that notices what's going on in your own head while you learn, decide or solve a problem.

The word comes from an American psychologist called John Flavell.

In 1976, he gave an example I really like.

He wrote, "I am engaging in metacognition if I notice that I am having more trouble learning A than B."

So if you've ever noticed that maths goes in easier for you than history, that noticing is metacognition.

Then in 1979, he wrote a paper that made the idea popular, and he opened it with a study his team had done with children.

They asked preschool and primary school children to study a set of items until they were sure they could remember all of them.

The older children studied for a while, said they were ready, and usually were.

The younger children also studied for a while and said they were ready, and usually were not.

**[ON SCREEN: older children: "I'm ready" and usually were / younger children: "I'm ready" and usually were not (Flavell, 1979)]**

So both groups did the studying. The difference was in knowing when they had learned it.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the viewer has the core idea, and the next line says adults do this too]**

And Flavell didn't think this was only a children's problem.

He wrote that there is "far too little rather than enough or too much" cognitive monitoring in this world, and that this is true for adults as well as for children.

Cognitive monitoring is the checking part. Checking whether you understand, whether you remember, and whether what you're doing is working.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: THE THREE PARTS OF METACOGNITION]**

Researchers split metacognition up in slightly different ways, but a simple way a lot of them use has three parts.

Knowledge, monitoring and control. Some people call that last one regulation.

**[ON SCREEN: metacognition = knowledge + monitoring + control (also called regulation)]**

I'll use one example for all three. Say you're learning a new language. I did Estonian A1 at my university, so this one is close to home.

The first part is metacognitive knowledge.

That's what you know about how you learn, about the task in front of you, and about which strategies work.

For example, knowing that words you only read once don't stay with you.

The second part is monitoring. That's checking yourself while you're learning.

You look at your list of Estonian words and ask, do I really know these, or do they only look familiar because they're right in front of me?

The third part is control. That's changing what you do because of what you noticed.

So you cover the English side, you test yourself, and you spend more time on the words you got wrong.

If you skip monitoring, you never find out there's a problem.

And if you skip control, you know there's a problem and nothing changes.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHY YOUR FEELING OF "I KNOW THIS" CAN BE WRONG]**

Every time you study something, your brain makes a quick guess about how well you've learned it.

Researchers call that guess a judgment of learning.

And that guess decides when you stop studying, and how confident you feel walking into the exam.

The problem is that the guess can be wrong.

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke asked students to learn short passages of text.

One group read the passage four times. Another group read it once, and then practised recalling it three times.

Then the researchers asked them how well they thought they'd remember it a week later.

The students who read it four times were the most confident.

And five minutes later, they did remember more. 83% against 71%.

But one week later, it flipped. The rereaders remembered 40%, and the group that practised recalling remembered 61%.

**[ON SCREEN: read 4 times: most confident, 83% after 5 minutes, 40% after a week / read once, recalled 3 times: least confident, 71% after 5 minutes, 61% after a week (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006)]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the flip is the payoff, and the fix comes next]**

So rereading made the text feel familiar, and familiar felt like knowing.

Researchers like Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork call this an illusion of competence. You feel more competent than you are, because the information keeps being right there in front of you.

The good news is that you can make your guess better.

In 1991, Thomas Nelson and John Dunlosky had people learn pairs of words, and asked them to judge how well they'd remember each pair.

When people judged straight away, they were only moderately accurate.

When they judged a little later, seeing only the first word of the pair, they were much more accurate.

So the simple version is this. Don't ask "do I know this?" while you're looking at it.

Close the book, wait a bit, and try to bring it back. What comes back is a much better answer.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: IS THE DUNNING-KRUGER EFFECT REAL?]**

The Dunning-Kruger effect comes up a lot when people talk about metacognition, so I want to be careful with it.

In 1999, Justin Kruger and David Dunning tested people on humour, grammar and logic.

They reported that people who scored in the bottom quarter were around the 12th percentile, and they guessed they were around the 62nd.

**[ON SCREEN: bottom quarter: scored around the 12th percentile, guessed around the 62nd (Kruger & Dunning, 1999)]**

Kruger and Dunning linked this to metacognition.

Their idea was that the skills you need to do something well are often the same skills you need to notice you're doing it badly.

That idea has been argued about a lot since.

In 2020, Gilles Gignac and Marcin Zajenkowski argued that a big part of the famous pattern is a statistical artefact.

That means the way the data is grouped and drawn can create a pattern like it, even without the effect.

Two ordinary things can do it.

First, most people rate themselves a bit above average.

Second, if you score very low on one test, part of that is usually bad luck on the day, so your guess about yourself doesn't drop as far as your score did.

Put those two together, and the bottom group looks very overconfident, even if nobody is especially blind to their own skill.

When they tested 929 people with a better method, they found the effect may be much smaller than reported before. They called it mostly an artefact, and they didn't say it's entirely one.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: There's another side to this, and it matters.]**

On the other side, in 2021, Rachel Jansen, Anna Rafferty and Tom Griffiths tested thousands of people on grammar and logic, and their results supported the idea that low performers are less able to tell when they're right.

So as of October 2026, researchers are still arguing about this.

And the internet version, the mountain graph with "Mount Stupid" at the top, isn't in the 1999 paper at all.

What I'm comfortable saying is smaller.

How sure you feel is weak evidence of how good you are, whether you're near the bottom or near the top. Checking yourself is what tells you.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the viewer has the honest version of Dunning-Kruger, and the next segment answers "can you get better at this?"]**

---

**[SEGMENT 6: CAN METACOGNITION BE TAUGHT? WHAT SCHOOLS FOUND]**

Can metacognition be taught? In schools, there's a lot of evidence on this.

The Education Endowment Foundation, or EEF, is a charity in England that gathers research on what helps children learn, and puts it in a toolkit for teachers.

As of October 2026, their toolkit lists metacognition and self-regulation at plus eight months.

That means students who got these approaches made about eight extra months of progress, on average, compared with similar students who didn't.

They describe it as high impact for very low cost, based on extensive evidence.

**[ON SCREEN: metacognition and self-regulation: +8 months, very low cost, extensive evidence (EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit, as of October 2026)]**

You might see plus seven quoted elsewhere, from older or partner versions of the toolkit.

It's an average across many studies in schools, with teachers trained to teach it, so it isn't a promise for one person watching a YouTube video.

And EEF's guidance on this, which they updated in November 2025, talks about teaching students to plan, monitor and evaluate their learning.

**[SEGMENT 7: HOW TO PRACTISE METACOGNITION]**

If you want to practise metacognition yourself, the easiest place to start is three sets of questions. One set for before, one for during, and one for after.

Before you start, you plan.

What exactly am I being asked to do? What do I already know about this? How will I learn it, and how will I know I've got it?

While you're working, you monitor.

Can I say this without looking? Which part is confusing me? Is what I'm doing working, or am I only busy?

After, you evaluate.

What worked? What didn't? What will I do differently next time?

**[ON SCREEN: PLAN: what am I asked to do? what do I already know? how will I know I've got it? / MONITOR: can I say it without looking? what's confusing me? is this working? / EVALUATE: what worked? what didn't? what will I change?]**

**[~9:00 BEAT: the most practical part of the video, with a study that shows the "before" questions working]**

In 2017, Patricia Chen and her colleagues at Stanford gave students in a statistics class a short online survey, about a week to ten days before each exam.

It took about fifteen minutes. Students thought about what the exam would cover, picked which class resources they would use, wrote why each one would help, and planned how they'd use it.

Those students finished the class about a third of a letter grade higher, on average, than students who didn't get the survey.

And using more resources didn't help them. Using them more thoughtfully did.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: The next one is my favourite, because you can try it with whatever you're learning today.]**

The next one is self-explanation. That means explaining to yourself why each step makes sense while you're learning it.

In 1989, Michelene Chi and her colleagues watched students study worked examples in physics.

The students who learned the most were the ones who kept explaining the steps to themselves.

So when you read a solution, stop at each line and ask why this step comes next, and how it connects to the one before.

The last one is called an exam wrapper. Marsha Lovett at Carnegie Mellon University has written a lot about these.

After a test comes back, you fill in a short sheet. How did I prepare? What kinds of mistakes did I make? What will I change for the next one?

To be fair, the evidence on wrappers is mixed.

In 2017, Raechel Soicher and Regan Gurung tried them in one course, and found no improvement in exam scores, final grades or metacognition.

So I'd try a wrapper as a habit, and see if it helps you.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: METACOGNITION, APPS AND AI]**

Metacognition is also a design question, and that's the part I care about as an HCI student.

A lot of apps are built to make things feel easy, and easy can feel like knowing.

In 2024, Lev Tankelevitch and his colleagues at Microsoft Research presented a paper at CHI, which is a big human-computer interaction conference.

It's called "The Metacognitive Demands and Opportunities of Generative AI."

Their point is that AI tools ask a lot of your metacognition.

You have to know what you want, break the task into parts, judge whether the answer is any good, and decide when to use AI at all.

One of their suggestions is that these systems "cue users to reflect on their goals and strategies via question prompts."

They also pointed out that a smooth, fluent answer can raise your confidence without making you more accurate.

In 2025, a team from Carnegie Mellon and Microsoft Research, led by Hao-Ping Lee, surveyed 319 knowledge workers about 936 real examples of using AI at work.

Higher confidence in the AI was linked to less critical thinking. Higher confidence in themselves was linked to more.

It's a survey, so it shows a link, and it can't prove that one causes the other.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: This next part is the one I'd build myself.]**

If I were designing a study app, I'd add one small thing.

Before it shows you the answer, it asks, how sure are you?

And over time, it shows you how often your "very sure" was right.

I use AI every day, so this is what I would tell you.

Before you ask AI, write down what you already know and what you think the answer is.

Then compare. The gap between your answer and the AI's answer shows you what you didn't know, and that's usually the part worth learning.

If you watched my video on curiosity, it's the same idea. Let the question sit with you for a moment before you hand it over.

So tell me in the comments, when you study, do you reread or do you test yourself?

### SHORT 4: Is Dunning-Kruger real? (30-40 sec)

The Dunning-Kruger effect comes from a 1999 paper where the lowest scorers guessed they were well above average.

But in 2020, two researchers argued much of that pattern is a statistical artefact.

Most people rate themselves a bit above average, and a very low score is partly bad luck on the day. Together, those two can make the bottom group look very overconfident.

Other researchers still find low performers are worse at telling when they're right.

So it's debated. What's safe is that how sure you feel is weak evidence of how good you are.

### SHORT 5: Plan, monitor, evaluate (25-35 sec)

If you want to practise metacognition, ask three sets of questions.

Before you start. What am I being asked to do, what do I already know, and how will I know I've got it?

While you work. Can I say this without looking, and which part is confusing me?

### SHORT 6: Write your answer before you ask AI (25-35 sec)

Before you ask AI something, write down what you already know and what you think the answer is.

Then ask, and compare.

The gap between your answer and the AI's answer shows you what you didn't know.

There's research behind the worry. In a 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers, people with more confidence in AI reported less critical thinking. It's a link, and it doesn't prove cause.

### SHORT 7: Judge later, not now (20-30 sec)

Don't ask "do I know this?" while the answer is in front of you.

In a 1991 study, people judged how well they'd remember word pairs.

Right after studying, their judgments were only moderately accurate. A little later, seeing only the first word, they were much more accurate.

So close the book, wait, and try to bring it back.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> The Feeling of Knowing Something Is Not Always Right

### Subtitle:
> What metacognition is, why rereading fools us, what the Dunning-Kruger debate is really about, and how to practise thinking about your thinking.

### SEO Title:
> What Is Metacognition? Thinking About Your Thinking, Explained

### SEO Description:
> A plain guide to metacognition, from John Flavell's work to judgments of learning, the Dunning-Kruger debate, the EEF's school evidence, and practical questions for studying and using AI.

---

Most of us know the moment. You read a chapter, close the book and feel ready, and then someone asks one question about it and nothing comes back. There is a name for the part of you that is supposed to catch that before it happens. It's called metacognition, and it means thinking about your own thinking. It matters to me as an HCI student who uses AI every day, because so much of what we build is designed to make things feel easy.

The word comes from the American psychologist John Flavell. In 1976 he wrote, "I am engaging in metacognition if I notice that I am having more trouble learning A than B." In 1979 he opened a well-known paper with a study his team had run with children. Preschool and primary school children were asked to study a set of items until they were sure they could remember them all. The older children said they were ready and usually were. The younger children said they were ready and usually were not. Flavell didn't think this was a children's problem only. He wrote that there is "far too little rather than enough or too much" cognitive monitoring in the world, in adults as well as children.

A simple way to picture it has three parts. Metacognitive knowledge is what you know about how you learn and which strategies work for you. Monitoring is checking yourself while you learn, like asking whether you really know a list of new words or whether they only look familiar because they're in front of you. Control, which some people call regulation, is changing what you do because of what you noticed, like covering one side of the list and testing yourself.

The monitoring part goes wrong more often than we'd like. In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students learn short texts. Some read a text four times, and others read it once and practised recalling it three times. The rereaders were the most confident they'd remember it, and five minutes later they did remember more, 83% against 71%. A week later they remembered 40%, against 61% for the recall group. Rereading made the text familiar, and familiar felt like knowing. Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork have called this kind of thing an illusion of competence. The fix is surprisingly small. In 1991, Thomas Nelson and John Dunlosky found that people judged their own learning of word pairs much more accurately when they did it a little later, seeing only the first word, than when they did it straight away. So close the book, wait, and try to bring it back before you decide you know it.

This is also where the Dunning-Kruger effect usually comes up, and I think it needs care. In 1999, Justin Kruger and David Dunning reported that people in the bottom quarter on tests of humour, grammar and logic scored around the 12th percentile and guessed they were around the 62nd, and they linked this to weaker metacognitive skill. In 2020, Gilles Gignac and Marcin Zajenkowski argued that the effect is mostly a statistical artefact, since most people rate themselves above average and very low scores partly reflect bad luck, which together can produce the famous pattern. In 2021, Rachel Jansen, Anna Rafferty and Tom Griffiths reported results that still support low performers being less able to tell when they are right. As of October 2026 the argument is still open, and the mountain-shaped "Mount Stupid" graph that circulates online isn't in the original paper. What I'm comfortable saying is that confidence is weak evidence of skill, at any level.

The encouraging part is that metacognition can be taught. As of October 2026, the Education Endowment Foundation's Teaching and Learning Toolkit lists metacognition and self-regulation at about eight extra months of progress for pupils, on average, and calls it high impact for very low cost, based on extensive evidence. Some versions, including their Australian partner's, give seven months. Either way, it's an average across many school studies with trained teachers, and it isn't a promise for any one person. The EEF's guidance frames the skill as planning, monitoring and evaluating learning, and that's a good shape for practising it on your own. Before you start, ask what you're being asked to do, what you already know and how you'll know you've got it. While you work, ask whether you can say it without looking and which part is confusing you. Afterwards, ask what worked and what you'll change. In 2017, Patricia Chen and her colleagues at Stanford gave statistics students a fifteen-minute planning survey before each exam, and those students finished about a third of a letter grade higher on average. Michelene Chi's work from 1989 found that the students who learned most from worked physics examples kept explaining each step to themselves. Exam wrappers, the short reflection sheets Marsha Lovett has written about, are worth trying too, although Raechel Soicher and Regan Gurung found no improvement when they tested them in a single course.

All of this matters for the tools we design. In 2024, Lev Tankelevitch and colleagues at Microsoft Research argued at CHI that generative AI places real metacognitive demands on users, who have to know their goals, break tasks down, judge outputs and decide when to use AI at all. They suggested systems that "cue users to reflect on their goals and strategies via question prompts." A 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers led by Hao-Ping Lee found that higher confidence in AI was linked to less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence to more, although a survey can't show cause. And in a 2025 study by Yizhou Fan and colleagues, students helped by ChatGPT improved their essays the most without gaining more knowledge than the others, which the authors connected to "metacognitive laziness." If I were designing a study app, I would ask people how sure they are before showing the answer, and show them over time how often "very sure" was right. For my own use, the habit I'd recommend is to write down what you already know before asking AI, then compare, because the gap between the two answers is usually the part worth learning. I'd like to know whether you reread or test yourself when you study.
