## 1. YOUTUBE VIDEO SCRIPT

**How this script is built:** a short opinion video in seven segments, each opening with its own topic so a viewer who lands there (or a clip cut from it) follows it. About 650 spoken words, so roughly 4 minutes at your pace.

---

**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

In June this year, the Federal Executive Council approved a big set of changes to NYSC.

To me, that's the government agreeing that the old way wasn't working.

But I don't think the changes go far enough.

I think NYSC has outlived its purpose.

It either needs to go, or be replaced with something actually productive, for the young people in it and for the country.

Right now, it's just a redundant inconvenience.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHY NYSC WAS CREATED IN 1973]**

NYSC, the National Youth Service Corps, was created in May 1973, under General Yakubu Gowon, about three years after the civil war.

**[ON SCREEN: NYSC, created 22 May 1973, Decree No. 24, General Yakubu Gowon]**

The idea was to post young graduates far from where they grew up, so a generation that had lived with each other would find it harder to see each other as enemies.

For 1973, I think that made a lot of sense.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHY YOU CAN'T JUST SKIP NYSC]**

**[~1:00 BEAT: the certificate is why this affects every graduate, and the safety point comes next]**

If you're not Nigerian, you might be wondering why graduates don't just skip NYSC.

The reason is the certificate.

The NYSC Act says it's the duty of every prospective employer to demand your NYSC certificate, or your exemption certificate, before they employ you.

**[ON SCREEN: NYSC Act, section 12: "it shall be the duty of every prospective employer to demand and obtain from any person" a copy of the Certificate of National Service, or an exemption certificate]**

So if you're meant to serve and you don't, a lot of doors close, especially government jobs.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: THE SAFETY PROBLEM WITH NYSC]**

The part of NYSC that makes me angry is safety.

After the 2011 elections, Human Rights Watch reported that, quote, "rioters killed ten youth corps members" in Bauchi State.

**[ON SCREEN: Human Rights Watch, May 2011: "In total, rioters killed ten youth corps members in the state." (Bauchi State)]**

In August 2023, eleven prospective corps members were kidnapped in Zamfara State on their way to camp, and some were still being held months later.

**[ON SCREEN: August 2023, 11 prospective corps members kidnapped in Zamfara on their way to camp. Some were still held in February 2024 (NYSC, via The ICIR)]**

So the state compels you to serve.

And then the state can't guarantee your safety while you serve.

Nobody should have to risk their life to get the certificate they need for a job.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: WHAT THE NYSC YEAR ACTUALLY GIVES YOU]**

Even when NYSC is safe, there's still the question of what the year actually gives you.

A lot of corps members are posted to work that has nothing to do with what they studied. You studied engineering, and you end up teaching in a secondary school.

The allowance is 77,000 naira a month. In my opinion, that's still small for a full year of a graduate's life.

**[ON SCREEN: NYSC monthly allowance: ₦33,000 (from 2020), ₦77,000 (paid from March 2025)]**

So for a lot of graduates, NYSC is a pause button on a life they're trying to start.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: THE CASE FOR KEEPING NYSC, AND MY ANSWER]**

Now, there are real arguments for keeping NYSC.

For a lot of people, it's the first time they live outside their region, and some meet their husband or wife during service.

In many rural communities, corps members are the teachers and the health workers.

And for some graduates, the place they serve becomes their first job.

Those are real. But I don't think any of them needs the year to be compulsory.

On unity, we're all on the same internet now. We're in each other's comments and in each other's DMs.

If a village school only has a teacher because a corps member was posted there for one year, I think that's a staffing problem. Those communities deserve teachers who are paid properly and who stay.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: THE 2026 REFORM, AND WHAT I WOULD DO INSTEAD]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the reform status, then your answer. This is what people searching "NYSC reform" came for]**

So what about the 2026 NYSC reform?

It was approved on the 29th of June. It brings a six-week camp, eleven career streams, and postings that are meant to match your qualifications.

**[ON SCREEN: approved 29 June 2026, six-week camp, 11 career streams, skills-based postings, risk-sensitive deployment, graduation ceremony replaces the passing-out parade]**

But it's approved, and that's different from being in effect. The NYSC Act still has to be amended, and in August, the camps were still running for three weeks.

Matching people to their field is a good change. Credit where it's due.

But the year is still compulsory, whether it's useful to you or not, and whether it's safe or not.

So if it were up to me, I'd either scrap the compulsory version and let it retire with dignity, or, and this is the one I prefer, make it opt-in.

A service year you choose, that pays you enough to live on, and places you in your actual field, somewhere safe.

I think people would line up for that.

So tell me in the comments, and be honest. Was your service year worth it?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> NYSC Has Outlived Its Purpose

### Subtitle:
> The 2026 reform changes how the service year is run, but it keeps the year compulsory. Here is why I think that's the part to change.

### SEO Title:
> Should NYSC Be Scrapped? The 2026 Reform and the Case for an Optional Service Year

### SEO Description:
> Why Nigeria's National Youth Service Corps was created in 1973, the safety and certificate problems, the strongest arguments for keeping it, what the June 2026 reform changes, and why I think the service year should be optional.

---

On 29 June 2026, Nigeria's Minister of Youth Development, Ayodele Olawande, announced that the Federal Executive Council had approved a reform of the National Youth Service Corps. The orientation camp is meant to go from three weeks to six, corps members would choose one of eleven career streams, postings are meant to match qualifications, and the passing-out parade would become a graduation ceremony. To me, that's the government agreeing that the old way wasn't working. I don't think it goes far enough, because I think NYSC has outlived its purpose. It either needs to go, or be replaced with something actually productive, for the young people in it and for the country. Right now, it's a redundant inconvenience.

NYSC was created on 22 May 1973 by Decree No. 24, under General Yakubu Gowon, about three years after the civil war. The scheme was meant to help reconstruct, reconcile and rebuild the country, and its stated aim was the promotion of national unity. Graduates were posted far from where they grew up, so that a generation that had lived among each other would find it harder to see each other as enemies. For 1973, I think that made a lot of sense, and I respect what it was trying to do.

What makes NYSC compulsory in practice is the certificate. Section 12 of the NYSC Act says it is the duty of every prospective employer to demand a Certificate of National Service, or an exemption certificate, before employing a graduate. Some people don't have to serve, such as graduates who were over 30 when they graduated, but anyone who is meant to serve and skips the year finds a lot of doors closed. The certificate is taken seriously enough that in 2018 the finance minister, Kemi Adeosun, resigned after a report that her exemption certificate was forged.

The part that makes me angry is safety. Human Rights Watch reported in 2011 that rioters killed ten youth corps members in Bauchi State during the post-election violence. In August 2023, eleven prospective corps members travelling from Akwa Ibom to their camp were kidnapped in Zamfara State, and NYSC confirmed months later that some were still being held. In 2020, Hon. Awaji-Inombek Abiante sponsored a bill to scrap NYSC, and one of his reasons was the "incessant killing of innocent corps members in some parts of the country due to banditry." The bill never became law. The state compels young people to serve, and then it can't guarantee their safety while they serve. The new reform itself talks about "risk-sensitive deployment."

Even when the year is safe, I don't think it gives many people much. Many corps members are posted to work unrelated to their training. Tony Onyima, who teaches at Paul University in Awka, wrote this year that some organisations reject corps members without good reason, underpay them, or give them unrelated work, and he called the scheme "a one-year pause on graduate unemployment rather than a permanent cure." The monthly allowance was ₦33,000 from 2020 until corps members started receiving ₦77,000 in March 2025, after the national minimum wage went up. That's better, but I still think it's small for a full year of a graduate's life.

The arguments for keeping NYSC deserve a fair hearing. For many people it's the first time they live outside their region, and some meet lifelong friends or their partners during service. In rural areas, corps members are often the teachers and health workers. In May 2026, NYSC's Sokoto coordinator said more than 1,200 corps members had been posted to rural schools in the state for the 2025/2026 service year, and 340 to primary health centres, and Onyima cites a field study in which almost 80 percent of rural residents surveyed named corps members as the main support for education. For some graduates, the place they serve becomes their first real job.

My answer is that most of these benefits don't need compulsion. On unity, we are all on the same internet now, and fifty years on, I don't think one year of service has fixed the tension we still have. Abiante also noted that because of insecurity, NYSC was considering posting corps members within their own geopolitical zone, which gives up the unity purpose. On rural schools, I think a village school that depends on a new corps member every year has a staffing problem, and those communities deserve teachers and nurses who are paid properly and who stay. On first jobs and exposure, a good first job should be paid and chosen, and I believe people will choose a year that is really valuable.

That's why the reform doesn't satisfy me, even though parts of it are good. Matching people to their field is far better than sending an engineering graduate to teach something they never studied. But the reform is about how the year is run. It was approved, and the Attorney-General and the Ministry of Youth Development were directed to start amending the NYSC Act, so as of October 2026 most of it still isn't in effect. And when it is, every graduate under 30 will still be expected to give the country a year, whether it is useful to them, safe, or wanted.

If it were up to me, there would be two options. One is to scrap the compulsory version and let it retire with dignity. The other, which I prefer, is to keep a service year that you choose. It would pay enough to live on, place you in your actual field somewhere safe, and build a real skill or fund a real business, so that you finish the year more skilled and more employable than you started. I think people would line up for that. Right now, NYSC is compulsory, and for a lot of people it isn't worth it. If you fix one of those, you might have something. If you fix both, you'd have something young people actually want.
