I use AI every day, at work as a developer and in my master's here in Tallinn.

**[SEGMENT 2: WHY I DON'T LET AI DO MY THINKING]**

Why would a student who uses AI every day be careful with it?

For me, it's about what happens when the AI goes away.

In 2025, a group of researchers published a study in PNAS, which is one of the big science journals.

They worked with nearly a thousand high school maths students in Turkey.

Some students got a normal ChatGPT-style assistant while they practised.

Some got a version that was designed to give hints instead of answers.

And some got no AI at all.

While they had the AI, the students with the normal version did 48% better on the practice problems than the students with nothing.

The hint version did 127% better.

Then the AI was taken away, and everyone sat an exam.

The students who had practised with the normal version did 17% worse than the students who never had AI at all.

The students with the hint version did about the same as the students with no AI.

**[ON SCREEN: Bastani et al., PNAS 2025. Practice with normal AI: +48%. Exam without it: -17%. Hint version: +127% in practice, about the same in the exam]**

So think about it like a friend who does your homework for you every week.

Your homework looks great.

Then exam day comes, and your friend isn't allowed in the exam hall.

That's what I worry about.

It can feel like you're learning when you're mostly watching.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the study result is the first payoff. Optional line to carry them on:]**

And the hint version tells us something useful too, because it shows that how you use the AI changes what you get from it.

So everything I'm about to show you is me trying to be the hint version for myself.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT MY UNIVERSITY SAYS ABOUT USING AI]**

Before you use AI for anything in your studies, check what your school says about it, because the rules are different everywhere.

At my university, Tallinn University, there's a page of recommendations on using AI.

It says students can use AI as a study partner, for generating ideas, for self-check, for correcting texts and translating, for making summaries while you learn, and as a programming aid.

It also says the teaching staff can decide how much AI you can use in their course.

And this is the line I want you to hear.

Using AI where your teacher has limited it, or not properly referencing your use of AI, is considered academic fraud.

The same page says the author is fully responsible for the content of the work, even when AI helped make it.

**[ON SCREEN: Tallinn University: "failure to make proper reference to the use of AI is considered academic fraud." The author "is fully responsible for the content and form of the presented work."]**

The university even gives us access to some AI tools, like ChatGPT Edu and Google Gemini.

So my school is fine with AI, as long as you're open about how you used it and you own what you hand in.

That's my school, as of October 2026.

TalTech, another university in the same city, has its own separate AI regulation from July 2026.

So even two universities in one city don't share one rulebook.

Please read your own school's AI rules, its plagiarism rules, and your course outline, before you open an AI tool for an assignment.

And if anything isn't clear, ask your lecturer in the first week, not the night before the deadline.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: HABIT 1, I MAKE AI QUIZ ME]**

The first way I use AI as a student is to make it quiz me.

Most of us study by reading our notes again and again.

It feels like learning, because the words start to look familiar.

But what you need on exam day is to remember it when the page is closed.

There's an old study on this from 2006, by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke at Washington University in St. Louis.

Students read a short passage.

One group kept reading it again and again.

Another group read it once, and then kept testing themselves by writing down what they could remember.

A week later, the testing group remembered 61% of the passage.

The re-reading group remembered 40%.

And the re-reading group had read the passage about 14 times, while the testing group read it about 3 times.

The re-reading group also felt more sure that they knew it.

**[ON SCREEN: Roediger and Karpicke, 2006. One week later: tested themselves 61%, re-read 40%]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the study sets up the prompt, which is the thing they can use tonight]**

So AI is a quiz partner that never gets tired of you.

This is the kind of prompt I'd use.

**[ON SCREEN]**
```text
I'm studying [topic]. Quiz me on it.
Ask me one question at a time, and wait for my answer.
Don't tell me the answer until I've tried.
At the end, tell me which ones I got wrong and why.
```

The "one question at a time" part matters.

If it lists ten questions with the answers under them, your eyes will read the answers.

If your course allows it, you can paste in your own notes, so the questions match what your lecturer actually covered.

What I would tell you is to close your notes before the quiz starts.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: HABIT 2, I ASK AI TO ARGUE AGAINST MY IDEA]**

The second way I use AI as a student is to make it argue against my idea.

AI tools can lean towards agreeing with you.

In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back an update to ChatGPT after people shared screenshots of it praising questionable ideas.

OpenAI said that version had become "overly supportive but disingenuous."

So if you ask "is my idea good?", there's a good chance you'll hear yes.

And when your lecturer reads it, they might not be that kind.

So I ask the opposite question.

**[ON SCREEN]**
```text
Here is my argument: [your argument].
Argue against it as strongly as you can.
Give me the three strongest objections a lecturer would raise.
Don't soften them.
```

Then the thinking part is mine.

I don't accept its objections just because they sound smart, either.

I go through them one by one, and I decide which ones are real, and I answer those in my own words.

Some of them will be weak.

Some will make you change your argument.

Either way, you've thought about your idea harder than you did before you asked.

Tallinn University's page lists something close to this as a good use, which is using AI for developing critical thinking, for example by analysing the answers it gives you.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: HABIT 3, I TRY FIRST, THEN ASK AI TO EXPLAIN]**

The third way I use AI as a student is to explain a concept, but only after I've tried to explain it myself.

This is an example from my field.

Say I'm trying to understand cognitive load, which is roughly how much your brain is juggling at one time.

Before I ask anything, I write two or three sentences of what I think it means.

Then I paste my sentences in and ask this.

**[ON SCREEN]**
```text
This is my understanding of [concept]: [your sentences].
What's wrong or missing?
Don't rewrite it for me. Just point to the gaps.
```

When you try first, you find the exact spot where you're stuck.

So when the explanation comes, it lands on that spot.

If you ask first, you read a perfect paragraph, you nod, and it feels like you understood it.

Then someone asks you about it in class, and the words are gone.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the "try first" prompt is the most useful thing in the video for most students]**

This is close to the hint version in the maths study.

The students who got hints instead of answers did much better in practice, and they didn't lose out in the exam.

You can ask for that directly.

Tell it, "Don't give me the answer. Give me a hint."

---

**[SEGMENT 7: HABIT 4, AI NEVER WRITES WHAT I'M GRADED ON]**

The fourth habit is the strictest one, and that's that AI never writes anything I'll be graded on.

That's my own rule, and it's stricter than my school's page, which allows things like correcting texts.

I have two reasons.

The first one is that an assignment is how your lecturer sees your thinking.

If AI writes it, they're grading the AI's thinking, and you're still the one responsible for every word.

Remember, at my school, not referencing your AI use is academic fraud, and the author is fully responsible for the work.

The second reason is that, for a lot of people, writing is where you find out if your idea holds together.

You think you understand something, then you try to write the paragraph, and you can't.

That moment is useful, because it shows you what you still need to learn.

If AI writes the paragraph, you skip that moment.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT LINE]**

And the last habit is the one that can save you from embarrassing yourself in front of your lecturer.

So around graded work, AI can quiz me before, argue with my idea, and explain the concept I got stuck on.

But the words I submit are mine.

If you use AI anywhere your course allows, reference it the way your course asks you to.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: HABIT 5, I CHECK EVERY SOURCE AI GIVES ME]**

The fifth way I use AI as a student is with a rule, and it's that I check every source it gives me.

AI tools can make up citations that look completely real.

They come with authors' names, a journal and a year, and they look like any other reference.

In 2023, William Walters and Esther Isabelle Wilder published a study in Scientific Reports.

They used ChatGPT to write 84 short papers, which came with 636 citations.

With GPT-3.5, 55% of the citations were made up.

With GPT-4, it was 18%.

And many of the real ones had mistakes in them.

**[ON SCREEN: Walters and Wilder, Scientific Reports 2023. Made-up citations: GPT-3.5 55%, GPT-4 18%]**

Those were the versions of ChatGPT from that time, and the tools have changed since.

But this still happens, even to big companies.

In October 2025, the Associated Press reported that Deloitte would partially refund the Australian government for a report that cost 440,000 Australian dollars.

The report had apparent AI-generated errors, including references to research papers that don't exist, and a made-up quote from a court judgment.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the Deloitte story shows this happens to professionals too]**

Tallinn University's page says it plainly too.

It says this kind of technology "does not know" which answers are right or wrong.

So this is how I check.

I copy the title of the paper and search for it in Google Scholar or my library's database.

If it exists, I open it, and I find the part that's supposed to say what the AI said it says.

Because a real paper can still be cited for something it never said.

Think of it like a classmate who tells you "I read it somewhere."

You'd still want to see where.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: WHAT I WOULD TELL YOU ABOUT USING AI AS A STUDENT]**

If you're a student and you use AI, this is what I would tell you.

Read your school's AI rules and its plagiarism rules first, and ask your lecturer if anything isn't clear.

Make AI quiz you, instead of reading your notes for the tenth time.

When you have an idea, ask it to argue against you, and then decide for yourself which objections are real.

When you don't understand something, try to explain it first, and ask it for the gaps.

Don't let it write the thing you'll be graded on.

And check every source it gives you with your own eyes.

I think AI is one of the most useful tools I have as a student.

And I want to finish this master's able to think without it too.

So tell me in the comments, what's one way you use AI for school or work that makes you think more?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How I Use AI as a Master's Student Without Letting It Think for Me

### Subtitle:
> I use AI every day in my MSc. These are the five habits I keep, and the study that made me careful.

### SEO Title:
> How to Use AI as a Student Without Cheating or Losing Your Thinking

### SEO Description:
> A master's student at Tallinn University on using AI for studying: what the research says, what university AI rules say about academic fraud, and five habits that keep AI from doing your thinking.

---

I use AI every day, at work as a developer and in my master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University. I have one rule for myself. It can help me think, but it doesn't get to do the thinking for me. I want to explain why I hold that rule, and the five habits that come out of it, because I think a lot of students are working this out on their own.

The reason I'm careful is a study published in PNAS in 2025 by Hamsa Bastani and her colleagues. They worked with nearly a thousand high school maths students in Turkey. Some practised with a normal ChatGPT-style assistant, some with a version built to give hints instead of answers, and some with no AI. During practice, the students with the normal assistant did 48% better than the students with nothing. Then the AI was taken away for the exam, and those same students did 17% worse than the students who never had it. The hint version did 127% better in practice and about the same as the no-AI group in the exam. That result is why I'm careful. It can feel like you're learning when you're mostly watching, and how you use the tool changes what you get from it.

Before any habit, though, there are your school's rules. Tallinn University's recommendations say students can use AI as a study partner, for generating ideas, for self-check, for correcting texts and translating, for summaries while learning, and as a programming aid. They also say teaching staff can decide how much AI is used in their course, and that using AI where it's been limited, or failing to properly reference AI use, is considered academic fraud. The author stays fully responsible for the work. TalTech, in the same city, has its own separate regulation from July 2026. So please read your own school's AI and plagiarism rules, and ask your lecturer early if anything is unclear.

My first habit is making AI quiz me. In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke at Washington University in St. Louis had students either keep re-reading a passage or read it once and then keep testing themselves on it. A week later, the testing group remembered 61% and the re-reading group 40%, even though the re-readers had read it about 14 times and felt more confident. So I ask AI to quiz me on a topic one question at a time and not to give me the answer until I've tried. If it shows the answers, my eyes will read them.

My second habit is asking AI to argue against my idea. AI tools can lean towards agreeing with you. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back a ChatGPT update after people shared it praising questionable ideas, and the company said it had become "overly supportive but disingenuous." So I ask for the strongest objections a lecturer would raise, and then I decide which ones are real and answer those in my own words. Tallinn University's own list of good uses includes using AI to develop critical thinking by analysing its answers, which is close to this.

My third habit is trying first. When I don't understand a concept, I write two or three sentences of what I think it means, and then I ask AI what's wrong or missing, without letting it rewrite my sentences. When you try first, you find the exact spot where you're stuck, and the explanation lands there. You can also tell it to give you a hint instead of the answer, which is what the hint version in the Bastani study did.

My fourth habit is the strictest. AI never writes anything I'll be graded on. That rule is stricter than my school's page, and I keep it for two reasons. An assignment is how a lecturer sees your thinking, and you're responsible for every word whoever wrote it. And for a lot of people, writing is where you find out whether an idea holds together. You think you understand something, you try to write the paragraph, and you can't, and that moment shows you what you still need to learn.

My fifth habit is checking every source. In 2023, William Walters and Esther Isabelle Wilder published a study in Scientific Reports where ChatGPT wrote 84 short papers with 636 citations. With GPT-3.5, 55% of the citations were made up, and with GPT-4 it was 18%, and many of the real ones had errors. The tools have changed since, but in October 2025 the Associated Press reported that Deloitte would partially refund the Australian government for a report with references to research papers that don't exist. So I search every title in Google Scholar or my library's database, open the paper, and find the part that's supposed to say what the AI said it says. I think AI is one of the most useful tools I have as a student, and I want to finish this master's able to think without it too.
