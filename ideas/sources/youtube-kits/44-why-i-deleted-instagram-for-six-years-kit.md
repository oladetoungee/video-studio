**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

I was 18, 19 when I decided that I was going to delete Instagram from my phone.

And I did delete Instagram, and I stopped using it for six good years.

I didn't use it at all for six good years.

And I enjoyed using Instagram.

So why would a 19-year-old girl delete an app she enjoys?

In this video, I'm going to try to put that into words, with some research that helped me understand it.

And I'll give you one question you can ask yourself, that shows you how much of your life social media is deciding for you.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHY I DELETED INSTAGRAM AT 19]**

I've always had a very strong opinion about social media.

When I deleted Instagram at 19, I don't know what it was at the time.

I was so young. I didn't know how to put it in words.

I remember I used to use Instagram a lot.

And I feel like if I had continued using Instagram at that rate, I probably would have turned out to be one big influencer or something.

But there was just something so strong in me about Instagram.

One of the things I say, even though it sounds so serious when I say it, is that it felt like this thing was going to hinder me from actually living the life that I wanted to live.

And this was at a point when it wasn't even as popular as it is now.

I already felt like, nah, this thing is doing things to me that I don't like.

**[~1:00 BEAT: you're about to say why it took you years to understand your own decision. Optional line: "And it took me years to understand why."]**

As I grow older, I'm now learning to put that decision into words, because I think over it again and again.

And now I'm starting to understand why a 19-year-old girl would delete Instagram when she enjoys using it.

So I've always thought about social media as a topic very, very deeply.

I used to really write on Medium, and my most viral article on Medium is still the one I wrote about leaving Instagram for six years.

**[ON SCREEN: your Medium article about leaving Instagram]**

I think social media does a lot of things that people do not pay attention to.

And the biggest one, for me, is that it can start deciding what you do with your life.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: IF YOU COULDN'T POST ABOUT IT, WOULD YOU STILL DO IT?]**

There's one question I want you to ask yourself about social media.

I was telling my friend about it the other day.

I saw someone on Twitter ask it.

If you want to do anything, just about anything you're doing in your life, ask yourself, if I cannot post about this thing, will I still do it?

**[ON SCREEN: "If I couldn't post about this, would I still do it?"]**

You will realise how much stuff you're doing in your life right now that you're doing just because you can post it.

If the ability to post about that thing is removed, you probably wouldn't be doing it.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer gets to test the question on their own week. Optional line: "Do this with me right now."]**

Try it with a few things from your own week.

Think about the last restaurant you picked. Would you have picked that same place if you couldn't take a single picture there?

Think about the last trip you planned. Would you still go to the same places if nobody would ever see the pictures?

Some of your answers will be yes, and that's good. Those things are yours.

But some of them will be no.

And that should make you realise that social media is actually, if you're not careful, literally dictating the way you live your life.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU DO THINGS TO POST THEM]**

So what actually happens when you do something because you want to post it?

There's research on this.

In 2018, three researchers, Alixandra Barasch, Gal Zauberman and Kristin Diehl, published five studies about taking photos during experiences.

Some of them were done at real tourist sites, and some were done in a lab.

They compared people taking photos to keep for themselves with people taking photos because they wanted to share them.

The people taking photos to share enjoyed the experience less.

**[ON SCREEN: Taking photos to share lowered enjoyment. Barasch, Zauberman and Diehl, Journal of Consumer Research, 2018]**

The researchers think it's because of something called self-presentational concern.

In simple words, the moment you plan to share, part of your mind is on how you'll come across to other people.

And that worry takes some of your attention away from the thing you're actually doing.

The effect was weaker for people who care less about what others think of them, and when the photos were going to people they're close to.

A sociologist called Nathan Jurgenson wrote about this feeling back in 2011.

He called it documentary vision, and he described it as "the habit of experiencing more and more of life with the awareness of its document-potential."

**[ON SCREEN: "the habit of experiencing more and more of life with the awareness of its document-potential." Nathan Jurgenson, 2011]**

Document-potential just means, how will this look as a post?

You're at a birthday dinner, and part of you is already looking for the angle.

You're watching the sunset, and part of you is already thinking about the caption.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: LIVING YOUR LIFE ON A STAGE]**

Social media can also turn more of your life into a stage.

In 1959, a sociologist called Erving Goffman wrote a book called The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life.

He described everyday life a bit like a theatre.

There's a front stage, where you know people are watching, so you perform.

And there's a back stage, where nobody is watching, and you can relax and be yourself.

Think of a waiter in a restaurant.

In the dining room, they smile and speak politely to the customers.

Then they push through the kitchen door, and they can complain about the rude customer at table four with the other staff.

**[~6:00 BEAT: this is where the old idea meets your phone. Optional line: "Now bring that into 2026."]**

Goffman was writing long before social media.

But think about what happens when your phone is always with you, and anything can become a post.

More and more of your life starts to feel like front stage.

Your holiday becomes front stage, and so can your dinner and your workout.

And when you're performing so much of the time, it gets harder to tell what you actually like, and what you're doing because it looks good.

I think that's a big part of how social media hijacks the life you want to live.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: COMPARING YOUR LIFE TO EVERYONE ELSE'S]**

Another way social media shapes your life is comparison.

In 1954, a psychologist called Leon Festinger described what he called social comparison.

The idea is that we work out how we're doing by comparing ourselves with other people.

People have always done it. But social media gives you thousands of people to compare yourself with every day, and what you're seeing is their front stage.

In 2014, Erin Vogel and her colleagues found that people who used Facebook more often had lower self-esteem.

And part of that link was upward comparison, which means comparing yourself with people who seem to be doing better than you.

Instagram's own researchers found something similar.

In 2021, the Wall Street Journal reported on Instagram's internal research.

One slide said, "Social comparison is worse on Instagram."

Another said, "Thirty-two percent of teen girls said that when they felt bad about their bodies, Instagram made them feel worse."

**[ON SCREEN: Instagram internal research, reported by the Wall Street Journal, September 2021]**

Facebook pushed back, and said the slides used shorthand and didn't explain every caveat.

But think about what comparison does to your life path, over years.

You start to measure your job against the jobs that look impressive online.

You start to feel late because of where people your age seem to be.

And slowly, other people's front stage starts setting the standard for your own life.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: MASS DRIFT]**

There's a creator called Ruth Guest who made a video called "How social media quickly hijacks your life path."

She uses a phrase I really like, mass drift.

In her description, she writes that many people have "fallen into the mass drift and are not living to their true selves."

She's clear that this is her own speculation, and not science.

But this person's video, it does hijack the life you want to live, if you're not careful.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the turn from "why" to "what happens when people leave" is coming. Optional line: "And researchers have actually tested what happens when people step away."]**

Drift is slow.

You don't decide one day that you're going to live your life for an audience.

It happens one small choice at a time.

You pick this one because it'll look good. You skip that one because nobody would care to see it.

And after some years, you're living a life you didn't really choose.

And when a lot of people are seeing the same things on the same apps, a lot of people drift in the same direction. That's the mass part.

I can't prove that with a study. It's an idea.

But I think it's an idea worth checking against your own life.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN PEOPLE STEP AWAY FROM SOCIAL MEDIA]**

So what happens when people actually step away from social media? Researchers have tested this.

In 2018, the economists Hunt Allcott, Matthew Gentzkow and their colleagues paid a randomly chosen group of American Facebook users to deactivate their accounts for four weeks, before the US midterm elections.

It freed up about an hour a day for the average person.

And they spent more of that time offline.

That included more time with friends and family, and, to be fair, more time watching TV alone.

They reported a small improvement in their wellbeing. Small, but statistically significant.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: "And the part I find most interesting is what happened after."]**

When the four weeks were over and nobody was paying them anymore, the people who had deactivated kept using Facebook a lot less.

One participant wrote, "I've been reading books and playing the piano, which I used to do daily until the phone took over."

**[ON SCREEN: that quote. Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow, American Economic Review, 2020]**

Then, in 2020, a much bigger experiment, again with Allcott and Gentzkow, this time working with researchers at Meta, looked at Instagram too.

About fifteen thousand Instagram users were paid to deactivate. Some stayed off for six weeks, and the comparison group stayed off for just one week.

The people who stayed off Instagram for six weeks reported a small improvement in their emotional state.

And when the researchers looked closer, most of that came from young women, aged 18 to 24.

I couldn't help noticing that's the group I was in when I deleted Instagram.

That closer look was exploratory, and as of October 2026, the paper is a working paper, which means it hasn't been through peer review yet.

So these studies don't say leaving Instagram will change your life. The effects were small.

They do show that once people in the Facebook study stepped away, a lot of them didn't rush back.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: THE OTHER SIDE OF THE SOCIAL MEDIA ARGUMENT]**

Now, I want to be fair to social media, because the other side has a real argument.

In 2019, Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski at the University of Oxford looked at data from over 300,000 teenagers and parents.

They found that technology use explained at most 0.4% of the differences in teenagers' wellbeing.

Their comparison was that regularly eating potatoes had a similar link with wellbeing.

**[ON SCREEN: at most 0.4%. Orben and Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour, 2019]**

Other researchers disagree with them, and that argument is still going on.

And social media gives people a lot. People find community there, they find work there, and they learn things they'd never have come across otherwise.

I post my content on these apps myself, and I want people to watch it.

This sounds a little bit hypocritical, I know.

Deleting Instagram was right for me at 19, and it doesn't have to be right for you.

I'd just like you to be the one deciding.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: MY NEED RULE AND THE QUESTION TO ASK YOURSELF]**

These days I'm back on social media, because I post my content there.

And my rule for social media now is need.

I only open Instagram, or Twitter, or TikTok, when I need something from there, and my need is usually to post my content.

And I break that rule too, sometimes.

If you want to try something, this is what I would tell you.

Before you do something, ask yourself the question. If I couldn't post about this, would I still do it?

If the answer is yes, do it, and post it if you want. It's yours.

If the answer is no, you don't have to stop. But at least you'll know who you're doing it for.

And if you have that strong feeling I had at 19, that an app is getting in the way of the life you want to live, listen to it.

You can always come back. I did, six years later.

But it will do nobody any good if we are all stuck on this thing.

So tell me in the comments, what's one thing you're doing right now that you wouldn't do if you couldn't post it?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Would You Still Do It If You Couldn't Post It?

### Subtitle:
> Why I deleted Instagram at 19 for six years, and the words I've found for it since.

### SEO Title:
> Why I Deleted Instagram for 6 Years (and What Research Says About Living for the Post)

### SEO Description:
> A personal story of quitting Instagram at 19, with research on sharing and enjoyment, performing for an audience, social comparison, and what happens when people step away from social media.

---

I was 18, 19 when I decided I was going to delete Instagram from my phone, and I did. I didn't use it at all for six good years. I enjoyed Instagram, and if I had kept using it at that rate, I probably would have turned out to be one big influencer or something. But there was something so strong in me about it. It felt like this thing was going to hinder me from actually living the life I wanted to live, and I didn't have the words for that at the time. I've been thinking about it again and again since, and I'm only now learning to put it into words. My most viral article on Medium is still the one I wrote about leaving Instagram for those six years, so this is me coming back to it older.

The clearest version I've found came from a question I saw someone ask on Twitter. For just about anything you're doing in your life, ask yourself, if I cannot post about this thing, will I still do it? I was telling a friend about it the other day, because when you try it honestly, you realise how much of what you're doing right now you're doing because you can post it. Think about the last restaurant you picked, or the last trip you planned, and whether you would have done them the same way if nobody would ever see the pictures. Some answers will be yes, and those things are yours. Some will be no, and that should make you realise that social media, if you're not careful, is dictating the way you live your life.

There is research on what this does to the experience itself. In 2018, Alixandra Barasch, Gal Zauberman and Kristin Diehl published five studies in the Journal of Consumer Research comparing people who took photos for themselves with people who took photos to share. The people taking photos to share enjoyed the experience less. The researchers point to self-presentational concern, which in simple words means part of your mind goes to how you'll come across. The effect was weaker when the photos were for close friends and for people who care less about others' opinions. Back in 2011, the sociologist Nathan Jurgenson called this "documentary vision," which he described as "the habit of experiencing more and more of life with the awareness of its document-potential."

In 1959, Erving Goffman described everyday life as having a front stage, where we know we're watched and we perform, and a back stage, where we can relax. A waiter is polite in the dining room and honest in the kitchen. Goffman was writing long before phones, but when anything can become a post, more and more of life starts to feel like front stage, and it gets harder to tell what you like from what looks good. Then there is comparison. Leon Festinger described in 1954 how we judge ourselves by comparing with others, and in 2014 Erin Vogel and her colleagues linked heavier Facebook use with lower self-esteem, partly through comparing upwards with people who seemed to be doing better. In 2021 the Wall Street Journal reported Instagram's own internal research, including a slide that said, "Social comparison is worse on Instagram." Facebook said the slides used shorthand and didn't explain every caveat.

The creator Ruth Guest has a phrase for where this can lead over years, "mass drift." In her words, many people have "fallen into the mass drift and are not living to their true selves," and she is clear that it's her own speculation. I think it describes something real. Nobody decides one day to live for an audience. It happens one small choice at a time, until you're living a life you didn't really choose, in the same direction as everyone seeing the same things.

Researchers have tested what happens when people step away. In 2018, Hunt Allcott, Matthew Gentzkow and their colleagues paid a randomly chosen group of American Facebook users to deactivate for four weeks before the midterm elections. It freed up about an hour a day, people spent more time offline, with friends and family and also watching TV alone, and their wellbeing improved by a small but statistically significant amount. After the experiment, they kept using Facebook a lot less. One participant wrote, "I've been reading books and playing the piano, which I used to do daily until the phone took over." A larger 2020 experiment with about fifteen thousand Instagram users found a small improvement in emotional state from six weeks off, mostly among women aged 18 to 24, which is the group I was in at 19. That closer look was exploratory, and the paper is still a working paper.

The other side deserves a fair hearing. In 2019, Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski at Oxford analysed data from over 300,000 teenagers and parents and found that technology use explained at most 0.4% of the differences in teenagers' wellbeing, a link about the size of regularly eating potatoes. Other researchers disagree, and that argument is still going on. Social media also gives people community, work and things to learn, and I post my own content there. That sounds a little bit hypocritical, I know.

These days my rule is need. I open Instagram, Twitter or TikTok when I need something there, usually to post my content, and I break that rule sometimes. Deleting Instagram was right for me at 19, and it doesn't have to be right for you. But before you do the next thing, ask the question. If you couldn't post about it, would you still do it? It will do nobody any good if we are all stuck on this thing.
