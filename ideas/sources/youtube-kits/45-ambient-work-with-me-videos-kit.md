## 0. WILL THIS COUNT FOR MONETISATION? (YOUTUBE RULES AS OF OCTOBER 2026)

Everything below was checked against YouTube's own help pages on 26 and 27 October 2026. Links are in section 11.

**Do watch hours from these videos count?**
Yes, as long as the videos are public and long-form. YouTube's eligibility page says the long-form route is 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 "qualified watch hours" in the last 12 months, and only hours from public long-form videos count. Private, unlisted and deleted videos don't count, and Shorts don't count toward the 4,000 hours either. So the long ambient videos count. The Shorts you cut from them don't add hours. They only help people find the long ones.

**The bar doubles on 1 February 2027.**
YouTube's help page on the 2027 changes says that from 1 February 2027, new creators need 8,000 qualified watch hours in the last 365 days (or 20 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days), still with 1,000 subscribers. Channels already in the Partner Program keep their status. YouTube's page doesn't say what happens to a channel that becomes eligible in January but is still under review on 1 February, so if you cross 4,000 hours, apply the same day.

**Being honest about why ambient videos work for hours.**
These channels earn watch time because people leave the video running for an hour or two while they work or study. That's real watch time and it counts the same as any other. But most of those viewers come for the mood and the music. I'd expect very few of them to click your talking videos about HTTP status codes or studying in Europe, so these hours help the number without building much of an audience for your main content. That's a trade-off, and it's fine as long as you go in knowing it.

**The "inauthentic content" policy (this used to be called "repetitious content").**
On 15 July 2025 YouTube renamed the "repetitious content" policy to "inauthentic content" and said it covers content that is "repetitive or mass-produced." The examples YouTube gives of what can't earn money include "image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative" and "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates." Recurring intros and outros are fine as long as most of the content is different. Rene Ritchie, YouTube's creator liaison, called it "a minor update to YouTube's long-standing YPP policies," and Search Engine Journal reported that this kind of mass-produced content was already ineligible before the rename.

There's a separate "reused content" policy for content taken from someone else "without adding significant original commentary." That one doesn't touch you, because the footage and the music are both yours.

**What keeps your series safe, in plain terms:**
1. Every second of footage is yours, filmed for that video. No stock clips and nothing downloaded.
2. Every video has its own new tracks. Never upload the same mix twice with a new thumbnail, and never stretch one 3-minute track into an hour by looping it. Third-party guides on AI music channels flag both of those as the things that get channels refused.
3. The five videos are different from each other (different time of day, different activity, different tempo and sound). A channel of near-identical mixes is the pattern the policy describes.
4. You're in the footage doing real things. The policy is aimed at templated videos with no person and no point of view, and real footage of your actual work session is the opposite of that.
5. The opening "lock in" track and the closing "that's enough for today" track can repeat across the series. YouTube says recurring intros and outros are fine when the rest is different.

**AI music and disclosure.**
YouTube's page on altered or synthetic content says you must disclose content that "creates music that's the main focus of the video," and it lists AI-generated music as something that needs disclosure. In these videos the music is the main focus. So if your tool's music is made by a generative AI model (and that includes Claude composing it), tick "Yes" in the altered or synthetic content question when you upload. YouTube says on the same page, "Disclosing AI content alone won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money." Not disclosing is the risky option, because YouTube says creators who keep failing to disclose can get a label added for them, have content removed or be suspended from the Partner Program. On 14 October 2026 TechCrunch reported that YouTube Music is rolling out labels that mark songs as "Fully Gen AI" or "Partly Gen AI", and that the labels are coming to the main YouTube app too, so a label on your music is normal now. I'd also add one honest line to each description (it's in the templates in section 3).

**Music rights if your tool uses anything that isn't yours.**
1. If the tool makes music from scratch (generated audio or code it writes), the main question is the terms of whatever AI service it calls. If it calls a music model through an API, check that your plan allows commercial use on YouTube. Some free tiers of AI music services don't.
2. If the tool uses sample packs, loops or drum kits, check each pack's licence says royalty-free for commercial video. Some "royalty-free" loop libraries are still registered in Content ID, which means your video can get claimed and the ad money goes to them.
3. Never put a recognisable piece of someone else's song in a track, even a few seconds.
4. When you upload, wait for YouTube Studio's "Checks" step (copyright and ad suitability) before you publish. If it flags a track, swap the track instead of disputing.
5. Don't register these tracks with a distributor's Content ID service if you ever put them on Spotify. That can make YouTube claim your own uploads. Also, the US Copyright Office said in its January 2025 report that purely AI-generated material isn't protected by copyright, so you'd be on weak ground claiming it anyway.

**Ads in a 2-hour video.**
Once you're in the Partner Program, mid-roll ads are possible on videos 8 minutes or longer (YouTube's help page on mid-roll ad breaks). For ambient videos, place the breaks yourself at track changes, so an ad never cuts into the middle of a song.

**Main channel or a second channel? (pros and cons, the decision is yours)**

On your main channel (@WithGbemi):
- Pro: every hour counts toward the same 4,000 (or 8,000 after 1 February) that your talking videos are building. A second channel would start from zero subscribers and zero hours.
- Pro: it's still you. Your face, your desk, your city and your work are in it, and it sits naturally next to your productivity and focus videos (brain rot, doomscrolling, flow state, preparing for 2027). A "work with me" playlist on a channel about focus makes sense.
- Pro: one channel to run. Your editing tool and your posting routine stay the same.
- Con: the ambient audience mostly won't watch your talking videos, so your subscriber count may grow faster than the audience for your main videos.
- Con: your channel analytics get mixed. Average view duration and click-through rate on 2-hour ambient videos behave very differently from a 10-minute explainer, so comparing videos gets harder.
- Con: when you apply, YouTube reviews your channel. If a big share of your hours comes from ambient videos, those are the ones a reviewer is likely to open. That's fine if they look like section 0 says, but it means they need to be clean.

On a second channel:
- Pro: a clean audience that only wants this, and your main channel's signals stay about your talking content.
- Pro: you can post ambient videos as often as you like without crowding your main channel.
- Con: it needs its own 1,000 subscribers and its own watch hours, and after 1 February 2027 that's 8,000 hours. I don't think a new ambient channel reaches that before February, and its hours do nothing for your main channel.
- Con: two channels to run, two sets of thumbnails, two posting schedules.

My honest read is that if the goal is monetisation before February, only the main channel helps with that. If the goal is to grow the ambient side as its own thing over 2027, a second channel is cleaner. You decide.

## 1. THE FIVE VIDEOS

**Series rules for all five:**
- No talking. The only sound is music, room tone, typing and real sounds from the scene.
- One scene per track (about 3 minutes). A scene is 3 to 6 long shots of 20 to 60 seconds each. Long holds feel calm, and fast cuts feel like an ad.
- A track name appears small in the bottom-left corner when each track starts, the way lofi mixes do. It helps people find their place and it matches the chapters.
- One caption card every 2 or 3 tracks, never more. The cards are short, lowercase and plain.
- Never show your address, building number, street signs near your home, your work screen with real company code or data, or strangers' faces up close. If a screen is in shot, film it from behind, out of focus, or open something that isn't private (your personal notes, a public docs page, the editor with a blank file).
- Film real sessions. If the video says "75 minutes", you really worked in that space. The time-lapse parts compress real time, so they stay honest.

### VIDEO 1: First work session in the new apartment (about 75 minutes, 22 tracks)

**The idea:** you've just moved into the bigger apartment this week. The video opens on boxes and the empty room, you set up the desk (in time-lapse), make the first tea in the new kitchen, and then sit down and work for real. The boxes stay in the background the whole time, which is the charm of it. Someone else watching is also working in a mess.

**When to film:** this week, while the boxes are still there. You only get one chance at this one.

**Shot list (X4):**
1. Empty room, 360 at 8K, camera on a small tripod in the middle of the floor. In the edit, reframe a slow pan that ends on the window.
2. Desk setup as a time-lapse (Timelapse mode, camera on the floor or on a shelf, 360 so you can pick the angle later). Unboxing the monitor, the lamp, the cables. Compresses 30 to 40 minutes into about 40 seconds.
3. Boxes close up, Single-Lens 4K, slow push in with the camera on the selfie stick held low. Keep any labels with your address out of shot.
4. First tea in the kitchen. Kettle steam, close up, Single-Lens 4K at 60fps so you can slow it to half speed.
5. Walking from the kitchen to the desk with the mug, invisible selfie stick held behind you (Me Mode or a 360 reframe from behind your shoulder). This is the "doesn't look normal" shot. It looks like a drone following you through the flat.
6. Desk hero shot, Single-Lens 4K, camera on a desk tripod to your side at about shoulder height, laptop screen out of focus or angled away. This is the shot you come back to most.
7. Overhead of the desk. Clamp or a tall stick over the desk pointing down, 360 recording, then reframe to a flat top-down view of your hands, keyboard, notebook and mug.
8. Window light moving across the floor, Timelapse over 2 to 3 hours of real working time.
9. Little planet transition (reframe in the Insta360 app). Use it once, at the halfway break, so it stays special.
10. End shot of the lamp turned off and the boxes in the dark.

**Caption cards (pick 8 to 10):**
- i moved this week. the boxes can wait one more hour.
- new apartment, same work. let's start.
- put your phone in another room. it will still be there when you come back.
- start with the smallest thing on your list. that one is enough for now.
- if you're working in a mess right now too, you're in good company.
- drink some water before the next song.
- break time. stand up, stretch, and look out of a window for a minute.
- back to it. same task, if you didn't finish it.
- you're still here, which means you're still working. i'm proud of you.
- that's the session. the boxes are still here, and so is the work you did.

**Sound design:**
- Record 2 minutes of room tone in the new apartment with nobody moving (phone voice memo near the desk). Lay it under the whole video very low, so the silence between tracks never goes dead.
- Real sounds to keep from the X4's own audio: tape tearing off a box, the kettle, the mug touching the desk, the first keyboard clicks.
- Typing: record your own keyboard close up with your phone for 10 minutes of real work. Mix it under the music, low enough that you notice it only when you listen for it.

**Music brief for your tool:** warm and hopeful lofi hip hop. 78 to 84 BPM. Rhodes or electric piano chords, a soft upright or muted bass, a lazy kick and snare with swing, light vinyl crackle. Major 7th and 9th chords. Feels like a Saturday morning in a new place. No vocals.

### VIDEO 3: Code with me, 2 hours of real deep work (about 2 hours, 34 tracks)

**The idea:** a real two-hour coding session at your desk, using 50/10 Pomodoro blocks (50 minutes of work, 10 minutes of break, twice). This is the one closest to your channel, because developers search for "code with me" and "music for coding." The breaks show you stretching, making coffee and looking out the window, and the work blocks stay on the desk.

**When to film:** any real coding session. Pick a personal project or open-source work so the screen can show actual code without any company code in shot. If you work on company code, keep the screen out of focus the whole time.

**Shot list (X4):**
1. Overhead of hands on the keyboard. This is the main shot of the work blocks.
2. Over-the-shoulder shot with the screen soft and the code shapes visible but unreadable.
3. Side profile at the desk, Single-Lens 4K. Use the lamp as the key light.
4. A 360 "orbit" around the desk. Put the X4 on a desk tripod, record 360, and in the Insta360 app keyframe a slow rotation around you. The camera never moves but the shot looks like it circles you. Use it at the start of each work block.
5. Time-lapse of the full two hours from the corner of the room. Use 30 to 40 seconds of it at the very end so viewers see the whole session pass.
6. Break shots: standing up, stretching, coffee, the window, a few seconds of the sky. Me Mode for walking to the kitchen.
7. Sticky note or notebook close up with the task for this block written on it (something like "fix the login bug"). Never write anything about your employer.
8. Laptop closing at the end, overhead.

**On-screen timer:** a simple countdown in the top-right corner during work blocks (50:00 down to 0:00) and a "break" card with a 10:00 countdown during breaks. Viewers who use these videos to work along like knowing where they are. Line the blocks up with track changes.

**Caption cards:**
- 50 minutes of work, then 10 minutes of break. the timer is on.
- write down the one task for this block before you start.
- i barely write code by hand at work anymore. i still have to understand every line of it.
- if a bug has taken more than an hour, write down what you've tried so far.
- close the tabs you're not using. you'll find them again.
- commit what works, then take your break.
- break. look at something far away for a minute.
- second block. same task, or the next one on your list.
- read the error message properly. it usually tells you more than you think.
- two hours done. close the laptop and go outside if you can.

**Sound design:**
- Typing is more present in this one, because it's a coding video. Keep it under the music, but let it come up a bit in the first 10 seconds of each work block.

**Music brief for your tool:** steady chillhop with more groove. 85 to 92 BPM. Jazzy clean guitar or Rhodes, a round bass line, a crisp boom-bap beat with swing, the odd vinyl stop between tracks. ## 1b. X4 SETTINGS AND SHOOTING CHEAT SHEET

The numbers here come from Insta360's published specs, as reported by ProVideo Coalition at launch (links in section 11).

- **360 video for reframing:** shoot 8K at 30fps for shots you'll reframe and show big (wide city shots, room reveals). Reframed 360 footage looks softer than single-lens footage, because you're cropping into a sphere, so the extra resolution helps. 5.7K at 60fps is the backup for movement such as cycling.
- **Single-Lens Mode:** 4K at 60fps. Use this for the desk hero shots, close-ups and anything that has to look sharp. It records like a normal action camera, so you choose the angle when you film.
- **Me Mode:** 4K at 30fps. It frames you automatically and hides the selfie stick. Good for walking through the apartment.
- **Timelapse and TimeShift:** Timelapse for the camera staying still (window, desk), TimeShift for moving hyperlapse (walking through Old Town).
- **Battery:** Insta360 rates it at about 135 minutes at 5.7K30. A two-hour real-time session at 8K may need a second battery or the camera plugged in. Do a short test before the big session.
- **Gesture and voice control:** use them to start and stop recording without walking over to the camera.
- **Lenses:** wipe both lenses before every shot. A smudge on a 360 lens shows up as a soft blur that you can't fix later.
- **Storage:** 8K 360 files are big. Use a fast card that Insta360 lists as compatible with the X4 (check their card list before you buy), and empty it after each session.
- **Editing 360:** reframe in the Insta360 app or the free Insta360 Studio desktop app, export the flat clips, then edit those in CapCut or your tool.

**Four "doesn't look normal" moves that suit this series:**
1. The fake orbit. The camera is still on a tripod, and you keyframe a slow rotation around yourself in the reframe.
2. The invisible follow. Stick held behind you as you walk, reframed so it looks like a drone following you through the flat.
3. The little planet. Use it once per video at most, for a transition.
4. The overhead. The camera on a stick or clamp above the desk, reframed straight down.

## 1c. EDITING RECIPE (CapCut or your Claude Code video tool)

If your tool takes a folder of clips and a track list, give it these steps as the spec.

1. **Build the music bed first.** Put all the tracks in order and crossfade 3 to 5 seconds between them. Normalise the whole bed to about -14 LUFS integrated. That's the loudness most creators aim for, because YouTube turns louder uploads down.
2. **One scene per track.** Lay 3 to 6 shots under each track. Each shot is 20 to 60 seconds long. Use slow cross-dissolves (1 to 2 seconds) and almost no hard cuts.
3. **Real sound layer.** Room tone under the whole video, very low. Then the real sounds of each scene (typing, kettle, pages) under the music, about 15 to 20 dB below it. If you notice the typing before the music, it's too loud.
4. **One look per video.** Pick one colour preset and use it for the whole video. Warm and soft for videos 1 and 5, cooler blue for video 2, neutral for video 3, soft and slightly faded for video 4.
5. **Track names.** At the start of each track, the track name fades in at the bottom-left, small and lowercase, stays for 5 seconds, then fades out.
6. **Caption cards.** Same font and same spot every time (bottom-centre, small, lowercase). On screen for 6 to 8 seconds with a slow fade. One card every 2 or 3 tracks.
7. **Timers.** Videos 3 and 4 only. A countdown in the top-right during work blocks, and a "break" card during breaks.
8. **Opening.** The first 10 seconds are the best-looking shot of the video with the first track already playing. No logo, no title card. The title card is the thumbnail.
9. **Ending.** The last track plays out over the final shot, then 15 to 20 seconds of quiet room tone for the end screen (link to another video in the series).
10. **Export.** 4K if most of the footage is Single-Lens 4K or 8K reframes, otherwise 1440p. Upload the highest quality you have.
11. **Before you publish.** Wait for the copyright check, tick the altered or synthetic content box if the music is AI-made, add the track list as chapters, and add mid-roll breaks at track changes once you're monetised.

## 1d. MUSIC TOOL RULES (for all five videos)

Give your tool these rules once, then the per-video brief from section 1.

- **Length:** each track 2:50 to 3:50. Video 1 needs 22 tracks, video 2 needs 18, video 3 needs 34, video 4 needs 26 and video 5 needs 18. That's all new tracks, apart from the opener and closer you reuse.
- **Loopable structure:** build each track from 8-bar phrases, and start and end it on the same chord, so a track can be extended by repeating a phrase without a bump. Don't fade the track out in the file. Do the crossfade in the edit.
- **Section plan per track:** intro (4 or 8 bars, thinner), A (8 bars), A with a small change (8 bars), B (8 bars, a new chord or the drums drop out), A again, then an outro that matches the intro. The small changes stop it from sounding like one loop on repeat.
- **No vocals,** no spoken samples and no recognisable melodies from existing songs.
- **Keep the key family close within a video** (for example, all tracks in or near D major and B minor for video 1) so the crossfades sound smooth. Change the key family between videos so each video sounds different.
- **Two series tracks you make once:** "lock in" (the opener for videos 1 to 4, 2 to 3 minutes, recognisable intro chord) and "that's enough for today" (the closer for all five). Video 5 opens with its own "log off".
- **Export:** WAV, 48kHz, and keep the project files so you can show how each track was made if anyone ever asks.
- **If the tool uses third-party samples or an AI music service,** follow the music-rights checklist in section 0 before you upload.
