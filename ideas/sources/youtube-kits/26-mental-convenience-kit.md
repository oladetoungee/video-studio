We don't navigate roads manually anymore. We follow our GPS. We follow a voice.

In 2020, Kenneth Arnold and Krzysztof Gajos at Harvard, with Krysta Chauncey, asked people to write captions for pictures, some with predictive text suggestions and some without.

With the suggestions, people wrote shorter captions, and they used fewer of the words the system wasn't predicting.

So the suggestions changed what people actually wrote.

**[ON SCREEN: with predictive text, captions were shorter and used fewer words the system didn't predict (Arnold, Chauncey & Gajos, 2020)]**

**[SEGMENT 4: WHAT GPS DOES TO YOUR BRAIN]**

**[Optional line to carry people in: "And this is the part that shook me."]**

The clearest research on mental convenience is about GPS, and how we find our way around.

And it starts with London taxi drivers.

To drive a black cab in London, drivers have to learn something called the Knowledge.

That's about 25,000 streets, and thousands of landmarks, all in their heads. It usually takes three to four years.

**[ON SCREEN: the Knowledge: about 25,000 streets, thousands of places, usually 3 to 4 years]**

A neuroscientist at University College London, Eleanor Maguire, studied their brains.

She looked at the hippocampus. That's a part of your brain that does a lot of the work for memory, and for finding your way around.

In 2011, she and Katherine Woollett followed 79 trainees through the training.

The 39 trainees who passed had more grey matter in that back part of the hippocampus by the end.

The ones who didn't pass didn't show that change.

So years of practising finding your way changed something in the brain.

Now, what happens when you hand that job to your phone?

In 2017, Hugo Spiers and his team at UCL scanned 24 people while they found their way around a simulation of Soho, in London.

When people chose their own route, their hippocampus got more active every time they entered a new street.

When they followed directions, those areas didn't respond to the street network in the same way.

Hugo Spiers put it like this: "In that sense our brain has switched off its interest in the streets around us."

**[~6:00 BEAT. OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT LINE: "And that's one session in a scanner. Wait till you hear what happened over three years."]**

Then in 2020, Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot at McGill University in Canada tested 50 regular drivers.

The people who had used GPS more in their lives had worse spatial memory when they had to find their way without it, in virtual navigation tests.

About three years later, they retested 13 of those people. The ones who had used GPS more in those three years had a steeper decline.

And before you say maybe people who are bad with directions just use GPS more, they checked that.

The heavy GPS users didn't report a worse sense of direction. So it looks like the GPS use came first.

But that follow-up was only 13 people, and the researchers themselves say it needs a bigger study.

So I take it as a strong hint, and I'd love to see the bigger study.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: THE GOOGLE EFFECT]**

And mental convenience is not even an AI thing. People have been talking about this for over a decade.

Our brains are becoming pretty economical.

For example, my brain will probably not want to put in the effort to remember something that I can access somewhere again.

There's a study from 2011, in the journal Science, by Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner. People call it the Google effect.

What they found is that when people expected to have access to information later, they remembered the information itself less, and they remembered where to find it more.

**[ON SCREEN: expect to look it up later, and you remember WHERE it is more than WHAT it is (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, 2011)]**

But I have to be careful with this one, because when other scientists tried to repeat it, it didn't hold up so well.

A big replication project in 2018 couldn't repeat the first experiment, and neither could a team in Germany in 2020.

And a team in 2021 found the "I'll forget what I saved" effect only when people first saw that the computer really would save their work.

So I hold this one loosely. I think my brain does make that trade sometimes. How strong the effect is, scientists are still arguing about.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: OFFLOADING ISN'T THE PROBLEM]**

Handing mental work to something outside your head, like putting a meeting in your calendar, is what psychologists call cognitive offloading.

But then again, I'm not saying that offloading mental work to tools is bad.

Writing itself, writing, that we think so highly of, is pretty much offloading.

It's offloading something from your brain, and it's been such a good idea for thousands of years.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the turn, offloading isn't the enemy]**

And funny enough, even writing had people worried.

In Plato's Phaedrus, which is about 2,400 years old, Socrates tells a story where an Egyptian king says writing "will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories."

I'm very glad people kept writing anyway.

So I don't think offloading is even the problem here. I'm talking through my thoughts here.

There's a whole philosophical position that your tools become part of your thinking rather than a replacement for it. The philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers called it the extended mind.

And there's this concept in HCI that I learned about, oh my goodness, distributed cognition. Yeah, distributed cognition.

It comes a lot from Edwin Hutchins, who studied how the crew of a navy ship navigated together, with their charts and their instruments.

And basically the whole idea is that your cognition is not limited to your mind. The tools you use are part of your thinking.

They're not a replacement for it.

So it cannot be that offloading is the problem. I feel like something else is going on. Or maybe I'm overthinking.

Like, I am such an evil overthinker.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: WHAT WE OFFLOAD MATTERS]**

So I don't think offloading is the problem with mental convenience, but I also think something else is going on somewhere.

There's some mental convenience that is resulting in a reduction in cognitive ability for us.

And when I was thinking about it, and this is not even finished, I'm still thinking, I'm thinking perhaps it is what we are offloading that is the problem.

Maybe it's because we are offloading the result, or we are offloading our thinking process. And we build the ability by thinking.

So if I read the summary of a book, that gives me information. But it wouldn't do to me what reading the book will actually do to me.

And also, there's this state where you don't know what you're thinking yet.

Have you ever followed through your thinking process, and there's this part, this time, where you haven't really gotten your thoughts yet, but you're approaching it?

I don't know if I'm the only one this happens to.

You can feel yourself approaching your thoughts, but you haven't gotten there quite yet.

I feel like that part is where something is. That part is where we are either missing it, or we are shortening it.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: WHAT HAPPENED WHEN CHATGPT WROTE THE ESSAYS]**

There's one AI study that fits this idea of offloading the thinking, and I want to tell you about it carefully.

It's from the MIT Media Lab, led by Nataliya Kosmyna, and it's called "Your Brain on ChatGPT."

They had 54 people write essays over four months.

One group used ChatGPT. One group used a search engine. And one group used only their brains.

They wore EEG caps while they wrote, which pick up electrical activity in the brain.

The researchers wrote that brain connectivity "systematically scaled down with the amount of external support."

So the brain-only group showed the strongest connections, the search engine group was in the middle, and the ChatGPT group was the weakest.

And the ChatGPT group had trouble quoting from the essays they had written just minutes before. They also didn't feel much ownership of their essays.

**[ON SCREEN: brain only > search engine > ChatGPT (brain connectivity, EEG). Preprint, 54 people, 18 in the final session.]**

Now, I really want to be careful here.

This is a preprint, which means when it came out in June 2025, it hadn't been peer reviewed.

It's small. 54 people, and only 18 of them came back for the last session.

And it's one task, writing essays, in a lab.

The lead author herself told Scientific American that the study showed "no dumbness in the brain, no stupidity, no brain on vacation."

So please don't share it as "ChatGPT makes you dumb." The study doesn't say that.

What it does fit, for me, is the difference between offloading the result and offloading the thinking.

I think struggling to quote your own essay is what you'd expect if the thinking part got skipped.

I made a separate video on how I use AI as a master's student without letting it think for me, so I won't go into my own setup here.

**[~12:30 BEAT: the study lands, and the next segment says why this is so easy to miss]**

---

**[SEGMENT 9: CONVENIENCE FEELS PRODUCTIVE]**

I think I've said this in another video before, that attention is trainable.

But I don't even think it's just attention that is trainable. I think thinking is trainable.

So every convenient route that doesn't require me to use my mind as much is a training that I'm not doing.

And convenience feels good. It feels productive. It feels like being smart with your time.

I mean, if you finish something faster than you expected, you don't sit down and start thinking, oh, what part of this did I skip?

What made me get to the end of it so fast? You just count it as a win, right?

And I think that's what makes it slippery.

Sometimes now I'll do something and it feels like it took me so little time, and in my head it feels like, you know when you have too much money and you're like, what bill did I not pay?

Why do I have so much money? What bill did I forget to pay?

Yeah. That feeling.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: WHERE I'VE LANDED ON MENTAL CONVENIENCE]**

So I think there is so much mental convenience.

And I would rather be inconvenient in the few places where the ability is one that I want to keep.

Because there are some abilities that I would love to keep, and it's not just about the results that they give me.

I want to retain the abilities themselves. And I'm actually starting to be more intentional about those parts.

For me, reading is one of them. I read almost every day, and there is always a book in my bag.

If you want to try this too, this is what I would tell you.

Pick the few abilities you actually want to keep. If you don't care about doing maths in your head, let the calculator have it.

For the ones you care about, try first, before you reach for the tool. If it's finding your way, look at the map before you leave, and only check it again when you're stuck.

If you want to read a book, read the book.

And when you use AI for something that matters to you, write your own rough version first, even if it's messy. That messy part is where your thinking happens.

I like things that work. I love using things to make work easy for myself.

I just want to know that there's a cost to the mental convenience I reach out for.

And I'm so convinced that that cost comes in cognitive ability, that there's a loss of ability somewhere.

Anyway, tell me if you think I'm wrong, because I'm still turning this one over.

And tell me if there's something you used to be able to do that you've noticed you can't do as easily now.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> I Build Convenience for a Living, and I Think It's Costing Us

### Subtitle:
> What the research says about GPS, Google, autocomplete and ChatGPT, and the few places I want to stay inconvenient.

### SEO Title:
> Mental Convenience and Cognitive Offloading: What GPS, Google and ChatGPT Do to Your Brain

### SEO Description:
> A software developer and HCI student on mental convenience, London taxi drivers, GPS and spatial memory, the shaky Google effect, predictive text, and the MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" study.

---

I build convenient things for a living. I'm a software developer, and my actual job is removing steps, making a flow shorter, taking something that would have taken a user longer and making it easier. My master's is in human-computer interaction, a field that is largely about reducing friction and cognitive load. And as a user, I'm the master of looking for convenience. I use AI every day, I use Google Maps, and I'll happily pay for a subscription that makes my life easier. So when I say I think mental convenience is killing our cognitive ability, I'm saying it from inside the thing I'm worried about.

What I mean is all the small things we don't do anymore. We don't navigate, we follow a voice. I don't remember things, I remember where to find things. We don't spell as much or do arithmetic, and we don't even finish our own sentences. That last one has been studied in my field. In 2020, Kenneth Arnold, Krysta Chauncey and Krzysztof Gajos had people write captions for pictures with and without predictive text, and with the suggestions, people wrote shorter captions with fewer of the words the system wasn't predicting. The suggestions changed what people wrote.

The clearest research is about finding your way. To drive a black cab in London, drivers learn about 25,000 streets, which usually takes three to four years. Eleanor Maguire's team at University College London found in 2000 that taxi drivers had a larger back part of the hippocampus than people who don't drive taxis, and in 2011 she and Katherine Woollett followed 79 trainees through the training. The 39 who passed had more grey matter in that area by the end, and the ones who didn't pass showed no such change. Then in 2017, Hugo Spiers's team scanned people finding their way through a simulation of Soho. Their hippocampus responded to each new street when they chose their own route, and didn't respond the same way when they followed directions. In Spiers's words, "In that sense our brain has switched off its interest in the streets around us."

In 2020, Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot at McGill found that among 50 regular drivers, the people who had used GPS more had worse spatial memory when navigating without it. When they retested 13 of them about three years later, more GPS use went with a steeper decline, and the heavy users didn't report a worse sense of direction to begin with. Thirteen people is a small group, and the authors say a bigger study is needed, so I treat it as a strong hint.

The idea itself is older than AI. In 2011, Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner published what people now call the Google effect, the finding that when we expect to look something up later, we remember where to find it more than the thing itself. I used to repeat that one confidently. But a large replication project in 2018 didn't reproduce the first experiment, a German team didn't find it in 2020 either, and a 2021 team only found the memory effect when people trusted the computer to save their work. The McGill Office for Science and Society has a good summary. I still think my brain makes that trade sometimes, but I hold the study loosely now.

And offloading on its own isn't the problem. Writing is offloading, and it has served us well for thousands of years, even though Plato's Phaedrus has an Egyptian king warning that it "will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories." Benjamin Storm and Sean Stone found in 2015 that saving one file helped people remember the next one they studied. In HCI we talk about distributed cognition, an idea Edwin Hutchins developed partly by watching a navy ship's crew navigate together, which says our thinking isn't limited to our heads and the tools we use are part of it.

So I think the question is what we're offloading. Offloading a result, like a phone number or a meeting time, is fine. Offloading the thinking process is different, because we build the ability by thinking. Reading a summary of a book gives me information, but it doesn't do to me what reading the book does. There's also a stage in thinking where you can feel yourself approaching your thoughts but haven't got there yet, and I think that's the stage we're shortening. A small MIT Media Lab preprint from 2025, "Your Brain on ChatGPT," fits this. Of 54 people writing essays, the ChatGPT group showed the weakest brain connectivity on EEG and struggled to quote essays they'd written minutes before. It's small, only 18 people came back for the last session, and the lead author, Nataliya Kosmyna, told Scientific American it showed "no dumbness in the brain, no stupidity, no brain on vacation." To me it's a picture of what can happen when the thinking part gets skipped, and I wouldn't stretch it further than that.

What makes this hard to notice is that convenience feels productive. When I finish something much faster than I expected, I don't stop to ask what I skipped. I count it as a win, and sometimes it gives me the feeling of having too much money at the end of the month and wondering which bill I forgot to pay. I'm not giving up my tools. I'd just rather be inconvenient in the few places where the ability is one I want to keep, and try first before I reach for help on those.

Is there something you used to be able to do that you can't do as easily now?
