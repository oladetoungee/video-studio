**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

**[ON SCREEN: a bookstore app. A book with "€30" crossed out and "€18" next to it]**

You open a bookstore app, and a book you like used to be thirty euros, and now it's eighteen.

You feel like you just saved twelve euros, so you buy it.

But if that book had just said eighteen euros from the start, would you have bought it?

That feeling of a good deal comes from a cognitive bias, and apps are designed around biases like this every day.

So in this video, I'll explain what a cognitive bias is, and seven of them that apps use, and how good designers design around them.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT A COGNITIVE BIAS IS]**

So what is a cognitive bias?

Your brain can't think hard about every small decision you make in a day, so it uses shortcuts.

For example, if you see a long queue in front of one food spot and nobody at the one next to it, you'll probably guess the busy one is better.

Most of the time, that shortcut works.

In 1974, two psychologists, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, published a famous paper about these shortcuts. They called them heuristics.

They said these shortcuts are usually useful, but sometimes they lead to errors that are systematic.

Systematic means the errors aren't random. Lots of people make the same mistake, in the same direction, in the same kind of situation.

That predictable error is what we call a cognitive bias.

**[ON SCREEN: heuristic = a mental shortcut / cognitive bias = the predictable mistake the shortcut makes]**

Years later, Kahneman wrote a book called Thinking, Fast and Slow.

In it, he used two names from the psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West. System 1 is fast and automatic, like reading a sign without trying. System 2 is slow and effortful, like working out seventeen times twenty-four.

They're a way to describe two kinds of thinking, and they aren't two separate parts of your brain.

Kahneman links most of these biases to the fast kind of thinking, when you decide before you've really thought about it.

You've probably heard of confirmation bias, where you look for things that agree with what you already believe. I covered that one in my video on how to think clearly.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the viewer now knows what a bias is, and the next line says why it matters on every screen they use]**

I care about this as a Human-Computer Interaction student because every app makes choices for you, so every design meets these shortcuts, whether the designer planned it or not.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: ANCHORING BIAS]**

The first cognitive bias is anchoring.

Anchoring is when the first number you see pulls your judgement towards it, even when that number has nothing to do with the question.

In that same 1974 paper, Tversky and Kahneman spun a wheel of fortune in front of people. The wheel was set up to stop on either 10 or 65.

Then they asked people what percentage of countries in the United Nations were in Africa.

The people who saw 10 gave a typical answer of about 25 percent, and the people who saw 65 gave about 45 percent.

**[ON SCREEN: wheel lands on 10, typical guess about 25% / wheel lands on 65, typical guess about 45%]**

A random number from a wheel moved their answers by about twenty points.

In apps, the anchor is usually the crossed-out price.

In our bookstore, thirty becomes the anchor, and eighteen feels cheap next to it.

Designing around anchoring starts with honest anchors.

In the EU, when a shop announces a price reduction, the old price it shows has to be the lowest price it charged in the 30 days before the reduction.

And for you as a shopper, the question to ask is the one from the start. Would I pay eighteen euros for this if I had never seen thirty?

---

**[SEGMENT 4: THE DEFAULT EFFECT]**

The second cognitive bias is the default effect.

The default effect is when people stick with whatever option is already chosen for them.

The most famous study on this is about organ donation.

In 2003, Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared European countries where you have to sign up to be a donor, which is opt-in, with countries where you're counted as a donor unless you say no, which is opt-out.

Kahneman describes their numbers in his book as close to 100 percent in Austria, which is opt-out, and only 12 percent in Germany, right next door.

**[ON SCREEN: Germany (opt-in) about 12% / Austria (opt-out) close to 100%]**

They also ran an online experiment, where the only thing that changed was the default.

With opt-in, 42 percent agreed to be donors. With opt-out, 82 percent stayed donors.

One careful note here. Those numbers are about consent, meaning who is counted as a donor. Actual donations went up by much less. In their own analysis, opt-out countries had around 16 percent higher donation rates.

Changing which box is already ticked changed what people ended up with.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the organ donation numbers land here, and the next part shows the same thing on the viewer's own phone]**

In apps, defaults are everywhere.

Notifications, auto-renew and data sharing usually start switched on, in settings most people never open.

Defaults can also help people, like a savings plan that signs you up unless you say no.

So as a designer, the question I'd ask is simple. If most people never change this setting, is the default the one that's best for them?

---

**[SEGMENT 5: THE FRAMING EFFECT]**

The third cognitive bias is the framing effect.

The framing effect is when the same information makes you decide differently, depending on how it's worded.

In 1988, Irwin Levin and Gary Gaeth showed people ground beef.

For some people, it was labelled 75 percent lean. For others, it was labelled 25 percent fat.

That's the same beef. 75 percent lean means 25 percent fat.

But people rated the beef better when it was described as 75 percent lean.

**[ON SCREEN: "75% lean" and "25% fat" side by side, with an equals sign between them]**

Apps use framing all the time.

A yearly plan that says "save 17 percent" sounds better than one that says "pay 100 euros today", even when it's the same plan.

Designing around framing matters most when the decision is important, like health, money or privacy.

So for those, I think a good design shows both sides, like the yearly saving and what you pay today.

That way, the wording isn't making the decision for the person.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: THE AVAILABILITY HEURISTIC]**

The fourth one is the availability heuristic.

The availability heuristic is when you judge how common something is by how easily examples come to your mind.

Tversky and Kahneman tested it with letters.

They asked people whether the letter K shows up more often as the first letter of English words, or as the third letter.

Most people said the first letter, because words like kitchen and king come to mind easily, and words like ask and make don't.

But in normal English text, K shows up more often in the third position.

**[ON SCREEN: K first: kitchen, king, kind / K third: ask, make, like, take]**

So the thing that's easier to remember feels more common, even when it isn't.

In apps, one long, scary review with photos sticks in your mind much more than four hundred short good ones.

Designing around availability means showing the whole picture next to the vivid story.

For example, a good review section shows how all the ratings are spread out, from one star to five, right above the reviews.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: SOCIAL PROOF]**

The fifth one is social proof.

Social proof is when we decide what to do by looking at what other people are doing.

It's the queue at the food spot again, and the psychologist Robert Cialdini made the term famous.

In 2008, Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius tested it in a hotel.

They put cards in the rooms asking guests to reuse their towels. With a card about saving the environment, about 35 percent did.

Another card said that almost 75 percent of guests reuse their towels. With that card, about 44 percent reused them.

**[ON SCREEN: "help save the environment" about 35% / "most guests reuse their towels" about 44% / "guests who stayed in this room" about 49%]**

And in a second experiment, the card that said "the guests who stayed in this room" did best of all, at about 49 percent.

The closer the other people were to you, the stronger it worked.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the "this room" result is the payoff of the towel study, and the next line turns it into star ratings the viewer sees daily]**

In apps, social proof is the star rating, the "bestseller" badge, "most popular" on a pricing plan, and "2,000 people bought this".

Designing around it means only using real numbers.

Fake reviews and made-up "twelve people are looking at this" messages are dark patterns, and in the EU and the UK, regulators have gone after them.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: LOSS AVERSION AND SCARCITY]**

The sixth one is loss aversion.

Loss aversion is when losing something feels worse than gaining the same thing feels good.

Losing ten euros hurts more than finding ten euros makes you happy.

It's part of Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory from 1979. They later estimated that losses weigh about twice as much as gains, although newer research shows that number changes a lot between people and situations.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: If you've ever opened Duolingo at 11:58 pm just to save your streak, this one is about you.]**

Duolingo is the best example I know. Their own blog says the streak works partly through loss aversion. Once you have a long streak, you don't want to lose it.

They also wrote that learners who reach a 7-day streak are 3.6 times more likely to complete their course.

And they added a streak freeze, which lets you skip a day. I think that's good design, because the streak helps you learn, and the freeze stops it from punishing you for one bad day.

**[ON SCREEN: a streak counter at 47 days, with a small streak freeze icon]**

Scarcity is close to loss aversion. When something looks rare, we want it more.

In 1975, Stephen Worchel and his colleagues gave people cookies from a jar. When the jar had only two cookies, people rated the cookies as more desirable than when the jar had ten.

In apps, scarcity is "only 2 rooms left" and the countdown timer on a deal.

When it's true, it's useful information.

When it isn't, it's pressure selling. In 2019, the UK's competition regulator got Booking.com, Expedia, Agoda and Trivago to commit to stopping it.

I made a whole video on dark patterns, including how to check if a countdown timer is fake, so I'll link it here.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: THE DECOY EFFECT]**

The seventh cognitive bias is the decoy effect.

The decoy effect is when adding a third option that nobody really wants changes which of the other two options people pick.

It was first shown in 1982, by Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto.

The most famous example comes from Dan Ariely's book, Predictably Irrational.

He saw an offer from The Economist. Online only was 59 dollars, print only was 125, and print and online together was also 125.

Nobody wants print only, because for the same price, you get print and online.

When he gave these three options to his students, 84 percent chose print and online.

When he took the print only option away, only 32 percent chose print and online.

**[ON SCREEN: three cards. Online $59 (16%) / Print $125 (0%) / Print + online $125 (84%). Then two cards: Online $59 (68%) / Print + online $125 (32%)]**

The print only option did its job without anyone choosing it. It made print and online look like a great deal.

In our bookstore, it would be an ebook for ten euros, a paperback for twenty, and both together for twenty.

To be fair to the research, the original authors have said the decoy effect only shows up under certain conditions, for example when people are already torn between the two main options.

**[~9:00 BEAT: all seven biases are done, and the last segment answers the question a lot of viewers are now asking, which is whether this is manipulation]**

Designing around it means every option on the page should be one that someone might really want.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: HOW TO USE COGNITIVE BIASES IN DESIGN FAIRLY]**

So is designing with cognitive biases manipulation?

I think you can't design without them, because every screen has a default and every price sits next to something.

In 2008, Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein called this choice architecture, in their book Nudge. A nudge is a design that makes one choice easier, without taking any options away.

Later, Thaler gave a name to the bad version. He called it sludge.

Sludge is design that makes it harder for you to do what's good for you, like a subscription that takes one click to start and twenty minutes to cancel.

Thaler's test for a good nudge is close to this. It's open about what it's doing, it's easy to say no to, and it leaves the person better off.

**[ON SCREEN: a good nudge is open / easy to say no to / good for the person]**

That's the test I want to use on my own designs. If I'd be embarrassed to explain to the user why the screen looks the way it does, it's probably sludge.

This is the first video in a series where I explain UX concepts in simple words, so tell me which concept you want next.

And tell me in the comments, which of these seven did you notice in an app this week?

### SHORT 3: The box that's already ticked (25-35 sec)

In an online experiment, Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein asked people about being organ donors.

When people had to opt in, 42 percent agreed.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> The Crossed-Out Price and Other Shortcuts Your Brain Takes

### Subtitle:
> What a cognitive bias is, seven of them you meet in apps every day, and how designers can use them without working against the people they design for.

### SEO Title:
> What Is Cognitive Bias? 7 Examples From the Apps You Use

### SEO Description:
> A plain explanation of cognitive bias, with seven common biases (anchoring, defaults, framing, availability, social proof, loss aversion and the decoy effect), the studies behind them, and how they show up in app design.

---

When a book in an app shows thirty euros crossed out and eighteen euros beside it, most of us feel that we've saved twelve euros. I study Human-Computer Interaction, and the more I learn about how people make decisions, the more I notice small moments like that one in every app I open. That feeling of a good deal comes from a cognitive bias, and I want to explain what that means in simple words.

Our brains make so many decisions every day that they can't think hard about each one, so they use shortcuts. In 1974, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a paper in Science describing three of these shortcuts, which they called heuristics, and they showed that while the shortcuts are usually useful, they sometimes produce systematic errors. Systematic means that many people make the same mistake in the same direction, and that predictable mistake is what we call a cognitive bias. Kahneman later described two kinds of thinking in his book Thinking, Fast and Slow, using the names System 1 and System 2 that the psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West had proposed. System 1 is fast and automatic, System 2 is slow and effortful, and most biases appear when we decide quickly.

Anchoring is the first one I notice everywhere. In that 1974 paper, people watched a wheel stop on either 10 or 65 and were then asked what percentage of United Nations countries were in Africa. Those who saw 10 gave a typical answer of about 25 percent, and those who saw 65 gave about 45 percent. Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale later found that even real estate agents were pulled by a house's listing price while saying they weren't. The crossed-out price in a shop works the same way, which is why the EU now requires that the old price shown next to a reduction is the lowest price from the previous 30 days.

The default effect may be the strongest. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein found in 2003 that countries where people are donors unless they opt out had far higher consent rates than countries where people must opt in, and in their online experiment consent went from 42 percent to 82 percent when only the default changed. Actual donations rose by much less than that, about 16 percent in their own analysis, but the lesson for design holds. Most people never change a setting, so whoever picks the default is making the choice for most users. Defaults can help too, as Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea showed when automatic enrolment raised participation in a company's retirement plan.

Framing changes decisions through wording alone. Irwin Levin and Gary Gaeth found in 1988 that people rated beef labelled "75% lean" better than the same beef labelled "25% fat." The availability heuristic makes things that are easy to remember feel common, which Tversky and Kahneman showed with the letter K, since people think more English words start with K even though in ordinary text it appears more often in the third position. In apps, it's why one vivid bad review can outweigh hundreds of good ones, and why I like review sections that show how all the ratings are spread out before showing any single review.

Social proof is the shortcut of copying other people. Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius found that hotel guests reused their towels more often when a card said most guests did, and even more when it said guests who had stayed in that same room did. Loss aversion, from Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, is the feeling that a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases, and Duolingo's own blog says its streaks work partly because of it. Scarcity sits close by, as Stephen Worchel and his colleagues showed when people rated cookies from a nearly empty jar as more desirable. When scarcity claims aren't true, they become pressure selling, and the UK's Competition and Markets Authority got several big booking sites to commit to stopping it in 2019.

The decoy effect, first shown by Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto in 1982, is when an option almost nobody wants changes what people choose between the others. Dan Ariely describes it in Predictably Irrational using an offer from The Economist, where a print-only subscription at the same price as print and online pushed most of his students towards the bundle. The original researchers have since said the effect appears only under certain conditions, but you can still see it on a lot of pricing pages.

Because every screen has a default, every price sits next to something, and every button has words, I don't think a designer can avoid these biases. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein called this choice architecture in their book Nudge, and Thaler later named the harmful version sludge, the kind of design that makes it harder to do what's good for you. The test I want to use on my own work is whether a design is open about what it's doing, easy to say no to, and good for the person using it. I'd like to know which of these biases you've noticed in an app this week.
