**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

Something happens in the world, and within one hour, everybody has a take.

You see it on X and TikTok, and in the family WhatsApp group.

And everyone is very, very sure.

If you're not careful, you pick up an opinion before you even know what happened.

My boyfriend always jokes that I'm an overthinker. A deep thinker.

I even have think-out-loud sessions with Claude, where I talk through my ideas.

So today, the overthinker is going through what I do to think clearly when everyone around me is already sure.

It starts with slowing down, and it ends with changing your mind without feeling ashamed.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: SLOW DOWN BEFORE YOU REACT]**

The first habit is slowing down before you react to anything online.

When I see a hot take, the first thing I try to do is nothing.

I don't reply, and I don't share it or quote it with my own opinion on top.

I wait.

And I wait because the things that spread fastest online are often the things that are not true.

In 2018, researchers at MIT published a study of about 126,000 news stories that were shared on Twitter between 2006 and 2017.

They found that false news was 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than true news.

**[ON SCREEN: false news was 70% more likely to be retweeted than the truth (Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, Science, 2018)]**

And the researchers found that people, more than bots, were the ones spreading it.

People like you and me, pressing share.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the 70% number is the first payoff. The question that follows, "why do we share things that aren't true?", carries people into the next part.]**

So why do we share things that aren't true?

Two researchers, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand, have studied this for years.

Their work suggests that a lot of the time, people share false things because they never stopped to ask if it was true. Their attention was on something else, like whether the post was funny, or whether it proved their point.

In one experiment, they sent a message to more than 5,000 Twitter users.

The message asked them to rate how accurate one headline was. That's all.

And afterwards, the news those users shared came from more reliable sources, on average.

The change was small. But one question did that.

**[ON SCREEN: "How accurate is this headline?" One question improved the quality of news people shared afterwards (Pennycook et al., Nature, 2021)]**

When a post makes me feel something strong, angry, or scared, or very proud, I try to take that as my cue to wait.

Before I ask "should I share this?", I ask "is this true?"

I read past the headline. I open the actual article.

And if you're on WhatsApp, look out for the label that says "Forwarded many times," with two arrows.

WhatsApp says that label goes on messages that have been forwarded many times.

So it probably didn't start with the person who sent it to you. It came through a long chain of people.

That's a good moment to check before you forward it again.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: HOW TO TELL A FACT FROM AN OPINION]**

The second habit is separating the facts from the opinions, because most posts mix the two together.

A fact is something you can check. You can prove it or disprove it with evidence.

An opinion is what someone believes, or what they value.

Both are allowed. I have plenty of opinions. You've watched my videos.

The trouble starts when a fact and an opinion come in one sentence, and you swallow both at once.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the Pew test is a "would I pass this?" moment. Let the question land.]**

And telling them apart is harder than it sounds.

In 2018, Pew Research Center tested a little over 5,000 adults in America.

They showed people ten statements from the news. Five were factual, five were opinions, and people had to say which was which.

Only 26 percent got all five factual statements right.

And only 35 percent got all five opinions right.

**[ON SCREEN: 26% correctly labelled all five factual statements, 35% all five opinions (Pew Research Center, 2018)]**

Would you get all ten? I'm not sure I would.

Pew also found that people were more likely to call a statement factual when it favoured their own political side.

That study was in America. I haven't seen the same test done in Nigeria, but I don't think we'd do very differently.

So this is how I practise it. Take a made-up post like this one.

**[ON SCREEN (label it MADE-UP EXAMPLE): "Estonia has made master's degrees free for every African student. Best country in Europe. Apply before it's too late!"]**

"Estonia has made master's degrees free for every African student."

That's a factual claim. It could be true or it could be false, but you can check it, on the university's own website.

"Best country in Europe."

That's an opinion. Someone is allowed to feel that way, but you can't check it.

"Apply before it's too late."

That one is pressure. It's there to make you move fast.

So in that whole post, the part you can check is one sentence.

And that's the sentence to go and check.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: HOW TO CHECK A SOURCE LIKE A FACT CHECKER]**

The third habit is checking the source, and the best trick I know for this comes from professional fact checkers.

At Stanford, two researchers, Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, watched three groups of people judge websites.

Ten professional fact checkers. Ten historians with PhDs. And twenty-five Stanford students.

One task was to compare two websites about children's health.

One was the American Academy of Pediatrics, a big professional body for children's doctors.

The other was the American College of Pediatricians, a much smaller group with a very similar name, which started as a protest against one of the Academy's positions.

Both websites look professional.

Every fact checker said the Academy's site was the more reliable one.

Most of the students picked the other one.

**[~6:00 BEAT: what the fact checkers did differently is the payoff people wait for. This line is also the re-engagement point inside this segment.]**

The difference was what the fact checkers did first.

On average, they left the College's page in about half a minute.

They opened new tabs, searched for the organisation itself, and read what other people said about it.

The researchers call this reading laterally. You read sideways, across tabs, instead of only going down the page.

**[ON SCREEN: fact checkers "read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan" (Wineburg and McGrew, Stanford)]**

The historians and the students mostly stayed on the page and read it carefully, from top to bottom.

And a well-designed page can fool a careful reader.

So when you see a claim from a source you don't know, open a new tab before you read it closely, and search who is behind it.

The other half of checking a source is going back to the original.

If it's a screenshot of a headline, find the actual article.

If the article says "a study found," find the study, and at least read the summary at the top.

A lot of the time, the original says something smaller and more careful than the post that went viral.

**[ON SCREEN: link to my video on how I read research papers, if it's live]**

If research papers feel scary to read, I have a video on how I read them.

**[SEGMENT 5: FIND THE BEST VERSION OF THE OTHER SIDE]**

The fourth habit is finding the best version of the side you disagree with.

The opposite of this is called a straw man.

A straw man is when you take the weakest, silliest version of someone's argument and knock it down, because it's easy.

A steelman goes the other way. You build the strongest version of their argument, the best case they could make, and you deal with that one.

In 1859, the philosopher John Stuart Mill wrote, "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that."

**[ON SCREEN: "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that." John Stuart Mill, On Liberty (1859)]**

And the philosopher Daniel Dennett, in his book Intuition Pumps, wrote down a rule I really like. He credits it to a game theorist called Anatol Rapoport.

Before you criticise someone, Dennett says, "You should attempt to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.'"

**[ON SCREEN: the Dennett quote, with "Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking (2013)"]**

So that's my test.

Could I explain your side so well that you'd say, yes, that's exactly what I mean?

If I can't, I'm not ready to argue with you yet.

In practice, skip the angriest person on the other side, and find the calmest, smartest person who disagrees with you, and read them in their own words, instead of in a screenshot posted by someone who can't stand them.

Let me give you an example that a lot of us will recognise.

Say you're convinced that every young Nigerian should japa, and go and study abroad.

The straw man of the other side is "they're just jealous," or "they don't want you to succeed."

The steelman is someone who says the money for one year abroad could start a business at home.

And that the first years abroad can be lonely and hard, so some people would build a better life by staying.

You might still disagree with them at the end. That's fine.

But now you disagree with the real argument, and your own opinion is stronger for it.

**[SEGMENT 6: HOW TO NOTICE YOUR OWN BIASES]**

The fifth habit is noticing your own biases, and I think this is the hardest one.

A bias is a shortcut your brain takes that pushes your thinking in one direction, usually without you noticing.

There are a lot of them, so I'll name five quickly, and then explain the three I think matter most for opinions.

**[ON SCREEN: list of the five as she names them]**

Confirmation bias is when you look for, and remember, the things that agree with what you already believe.

The bandwagon effect is when you believe something more because a lot of people around you believe it.

Motivated reasoning is when you reason your way to the answer you wanted from the start.

The bias blind spot is when you see bias in other people much more easily than in yourself.

And the illusion of explanatory depth is when you think you understand something better than you do.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the skin cream and guns study is a story with a twist. Optional line to carry people in:]**

Let's start with motivated reasoning, because there's a study that shows it really clearly.

Dan Kahan and his colleagues at Yale gave about 1,100 American adults a small table of numbers from a made-up study, and asked them what the results showed.

For some people, the table was about a cream for skin rashes.

For others, the exact same numbers were about a ban on carrying hidden guns, and whether crime went up or down.

When the topic was skin cream, people's maths skills predicted whether they got it right.

When the topic was guns, people were much more likely to get it right when the correct answer matched their politics, and to get it wrong when it didn't.

So when I find something that agrees with me perfectly, I ask myself one question.

If this same evidence pointed the other way, would I accept it this easily?

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT LINE]**

The next one is about all of us.

In 2002, Emily Pronin and her colleagues at Stanford asked people how much common biases affected them, compared with other people, like the average American or their own classmates.

People rated themselves as less biased than everybody else.

And even after the researchers explained how the bias could have affected them, people still insisted their own ratings were accurate and objective.

That's the bias blind spot.

Knowing about biases doesn't make you free of them. That includes me, making a whole video about it.

And the last one is a test you can do on yourself right now.

In 2002, Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil at Yale asked people how well they understood everyday things, like a zip, a flush toilet, or a lock.

People rated themselves quite high.

Then they were asked to write out, step by step, how the thing works.

And after trying, they rated their understanding lower.

So try it now. How does a zip work? Explain it, step by step.

**[ON SCREEN: How does a zip work? Explain it step by step.]**

**[PAUSE]**

If that was harder than you expected, you're in good company.

So what I would tell you is, before you argue about how something works, try to explain it, step by step, to yourself first.

A lot of the time, you'll find you only know the headline.

**[SEGMENT 7: HOW TO CHANGE YOUR MIND WITHOUT SHAME]**

The last habit is changing your mind, without feeling ashamed of it.

I think a lot of us grew up feeling that being wrong is embarrassing. That if you change your position, you've lost.

And I think we overestimate how bad it looks.

In 2015, two psychologists, Adam Fetterman and Kai Sassenberg, surveyed 281 scientists, and asked them to imagine one of their findings failing when other researchers tried to repeat it.

The scientists expected that to hurt their reputation more when the finding was their own than when it was a colleague's.

But when they judged other scientists, they rated the ones who admitted they were wrong more favourably than the ones who defended themselves.

It was a survey, so it can't tell us exactly what happens in real life.

But it suggests we fear admitting we're wrong more than we need to.

And I want to show you what changing your mind well looks like, with a real example.

In 2017, William Brady and his colleagues looked at more than 500,000 tweets about topics like gun control and climate change.

They found that each extra word carrying both a moral judgment and a feeling, words like abuse or spite, made a tweet spread about 20 percent more.

That number got quoted a lot.

Then in 2025, Brady and his colleagues pooled 27 studies, with millions of posts.

The effect was still there, but smaller. Closer to 12 percent for each word.

**[ON SCREEN: 2017: about 20% more spread per moral-emotional word. 2025, 27 studies pooled: about 12% (Brady et al., PNAS and PNAS Nexus)]**

The same researcher went back, looked at more data, and published a smaller number than his famous one.

I trust that research more because of it.

So this is what I would tell you.

Before you pick a side on something, ask yourself, what would change my mind?

If you can't think of anything at all, ask yourself whether you're still thinking about it.

Practise saying out loud, "I used to think this, and now I think that."

And as an overthinker, I'll tell you this. Let yourself say, "I don't know yet."

I've said this before and I still believe it. Curiosity is the state of having an open question.

"I don't know yet" keeps the question open.

So tell me in the comments, what's something you changed your mind about recently?

And what made you change it?

### SHORT 3: What fact checkers do differently (30-40 sec)

Stanford researchers watched professional fact checkers, historians with PhDs, and Stanford students judge websites.

One task was two websites about children's health, with very similar names.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How to Think Clearly When Everyone Has an Opinion

### Subtitle:
> Six habits for the hour after something happens and everybody is already sure.

### SEO Title:
> How to Think Clearly: Facts vs Opinions, Checking Sources, Biases and Changing Your Mind

### SEO Description:
> A practical guide to thinking for yourself online, with the research behind each habit: slowing down, telling facts from opinions, lateral reading, steelmanning, noticing your own biases, and changing your mind without shame.

---

Something happens in the world, and within one hour everybody has a take. It's on X, on TikTok, and in the family WhatsApp group, and everyone sounds very sure. If you're not careful, you pick up an opinion before you even know what happened. These are the habits I try to use when that happens, in the order I'd use them on a real post.

The first one is slowing down. In 2018, Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral at MIT looked at about 126,000 news stories shared on Twitter between 2006 and 2017, and found that false news was 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than true news. People, more than bots, were the ones spreading it. Gordon Pennycook and David Rand have argued that a lot of this happens because people never stop to ask whether a post is true. In one of their experiments, they sent more than 5,000 Twitter users a message asking them to rate how accurate one headline was, and the news those users shared afterwards came from more reliable sources on average. In another, with Bence Bago, people who got time to think believed fewer false headlines, while their belief in true headlines stayed about the same. So when a post makes me feel something strong, I take that as my cue to wait, and I ask "is this true?" before I ask "should I share this?"

The second habit is separating the facts from the opinions. A fact can be checked with evidence, even if it turns out to be wrong. An opinion is what someone believes or values. In 2018, Pew Research Center gave just over 5,000 American adults ten news statements, and only 26 percent labelled all five factual ones correctly, while 35 percent labelled all five opinions correctly. People were also more likely to call a statement factual when it favoured their own side. When I read a heated post now, I look for the one sentence that can actually be checked, because that's usually all there is, and that's the sentence to go and check.

The third habit is checking the source the way professional fact checkers do. Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew at Stanford watched ten fact checkers, ten historians and twenty-five Stanford students judge websites. Given two children's health sites with almost the same name, every fact checker picked the more reliable one, and most students picked the other. The fact checkers left the page within about half a minute, opened new tabs, and searched for who was behind it. The researchers call it reading laterally. The other half of checking is going back to the original. If a post says "a study found," I go and find the study, because the original is often smaller and more careful than the post.

The fourth habit is finding the best version of the side I disagree with. John Stuart Mill wrote in 1859 that "he who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that." Daniel Dennett, in Intuition Pumps, passed on a rule he credits to Anatol Rapoport: "You should attempt to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.'" If I can't explain your side that well, I'm not ready to argue with you. And if you're sure every young Nigerian should japa, the strongest version of the other side is someone who says the money could build a business at home, and that the first years abroad can be lonely and hard. You might still disagree at the end, but now you're disagreeing with the real argument.

The fifth habit is noticing my own biases, which I think is the hardest. Dan Kahan and his colleagues gave about 1,100 American adults the same table of numbers, framed for some as a skin cream study and for others as a gun law study. On skin cream, people's maths skills predicted whether they got it right. On guns, people were much more likely to get it right when the answer matched their politics. The original paper also claimed that people better at maths were even more biased, but a later study in Europe didn't find that part, so I leave it out. Emily Pronin and her colleagues found that people rate themselves as less biased than the average person, and keep insisting on it even after the bias is explained to them. And Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil found that people who rated how well they understood a zip or a lock rated themselves lower after trying to explain it step by step. So when something agrees with me perfectly, I check it harder, and before I argue about how something works, I try to explain it to myself first.

The last habit is changing my mind without shame. In 2015, Adam Fetterman and Kai Sassenberg surveyed 281 scientists and found they expected admitting a failed finding to hurt their reputation, while they rated other scientists who admitted being wrong more favourably than those who defended themselves. My favourite real example is moral contagion. In 2017, William Brady and his colleagues found that each extra moral-emotional word made a tweet spread about 20 percent more. In 2025, Brady and his colleagues pooled 27 studies and published a smaller estimate, about 12 percent. I trust that research more because of it.

So before I pick a side, I try to ask what would change my mind, and I practise saying "I used to think this, and now I think that." I also let myself say "I don't know yet." I still believe curiosity is the state of having an open question, and "I don't know yet" keeps the question open. What's something you changed your mind about recently, and what made you change it?
