## 1. YOUTUBE VIDEO SCRIPT

**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

You finish a book, and you enjoyed it. You even underlined things.

Then somebody asks you, "What is it about?"

And all you can say is, "It's really good, you should read it."

In an older video, I described this as being left with a feeling about the book rather than the book itself.

So in this video, I'll show you why that happens, with the research behind it, and what you can do after you close a book so you can actually explain it.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHY YOU FEEL LIKE YOU UNDERSTOOD A BOOK]**

That feeling of understanding a book you can't explain has a name. It's called the illusion of explanatory depth.

It comes from a 2002 study by two researchers at Yale, Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil.

They asked people how well they understood everyday things, like a zip, a flush toilet or a lock, on a scale from one to seven.

Then they asked them to write out, step by step, how the thing actually works.

Then they asked them a harder, more specific question about it.

After each step, people rated their understanding again, and the ratings kept dropping.

Frank Keil later wrote that the drop often came with "shock and surprise."

**[ON SCREEN: rate it, explain it, rate it again / the rating drops]**

So before you try to explain something, it feels like you know it, and when you try, the gaps show up.

The finding that matters most for reading is this one.

The researchers found that the illusion was much stronger for explanations, meaning how and why something works, than for other kinds of knowledge, like facts, procedures or stories.

So if you just finished a novel, you can usually tell someone what happened.

But a lot of non-fiction books are explanations.

A book about habits explains why you do what you do. A book about money explains how interest grows.

And explanations are exactly the kind of knowledge where your brain tells you that you understand more than you do.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the next segment shows why rereading makes the feeling stronger, which is what most people do to fix it]**

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHY REREADING FEELS LIKE LEARNING]**

When we can't explain a book, a lot of us try to fix it by reading it again, or by going back over our highlights.

In 2009, Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger asked 177 university students how they study.

About 84% said they reread their notes or textbooks, and about 55% said rereading was their number one strategy.

Only about one in ten mentioned practising recall, which means testing yourself.

**[ON SCREEN: ~84% reread / ~55% said it's their #1 strategy / ~1 in 10 practise recall]**

Rereading feels good, because the second time, the words come easier. You know what's coming next.

Psychologists call that ease fluency.

And your brain takes that ease as a sign that you've learned the thing, even when you haven't.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the sea otter study is the proof, and the result flips what people expect]**

There's a study that shows how far this feeling can mislead you.

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke gave students a short science passage, about the sun or about sea otters.

One group read the passage four times.

Another group read it once, and then wrote down everything they could remember, three times.

Before they left, students predicted how well they'd remember it a week later, and the group that read it four times was the most confident.

If you tested them five minutes later, the rereaders were actually ahead.

But a week later, the group that read it four times remembered about 40% of the passage, and the group that read it once and recalled it three times remembered about 61%.

**[ON SCREEN: read it 4 times, 40% after a week / read once + recalled 3 times, 61% after a week]**

So the group that felt the most ready remembered the least.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: RECOGNITION VS RECALL]**

Your memory can do two different things with a book. It can recognise, and it can recall.

Recognising is when you see something and know you've seen it before.

Recalling is pulling it out of your head with nothing in front of you.

You know when you see someone at church or at the market, you know their face, but you can't remember their name?

Knowing the face is recognition. Remembering the name is recall.

In 2015, Adam Blake, Meenely Nazarian and Alan Castel at UCLA asked 85 students to draw the Apple logo from memory.

Most of them used Apple products.

Only one person drew it correctly.

Then they showed the students the real logo mixed in with versions that had small changes, and fewer than half picked the real one.

**[ON SCREEN: 85 students / 1 drew it correctly / fewer than half picked the real one]**

Every device I own is Apple, and I'm not sure I would have got it right either.

Alan Castel said, "People's memory, even for extremely common objects, is much poorer than they believe it to be."

So think about what happens when you reread a book.

The book is right there in front of you, so you only need recognition. Every sentence makes you go, "Yes, I know this."

When someone asks you to explain the book, there's no book. That's recall.

And if all you ever did was reread, you never practised recall.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: CLOSE THE BOOK AND WRITE WHAT YOU REMEMBER]**

The first thing you can do so you can explain what you read is to close the book and write down what you remember.

This is called retrieval practice, which means pulling things out of your memory on purpose, like the group in the sea otter study did.

After a chapter, close the book, open a blank page or your notes app, and write what the chapter said in your own words. No peeking.

Then open the book again and check.

What did you miss? What did you get wrong?

Those gaps are exactly the parts you would have stumbled on when someone asked you about the book.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the "low utility" rating for summaries is a small surprise, and the Adler rule gives viewers something to try tonight]**

It will feel harder than rereading, and that's normal.

You might be thinking, I already write summaries.

In 2013, John Dunlosky and his colleagues reviewed ten common study techniques. They rated summarising, rereading and highlighting as low usefulness, and testing yourself as high.

So I would write my summary with the book closed, because then the summary becomes a way of testing yourself.

And when you finish the whole book, try one more thing.

Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren, in How to Read a Book, have a rule for this. "State the unity of the whole book in a single sentence, or at most a few sentences."

So in one or two sentences, what is the whole book about?

If you can't do that yet, you know where to go back to.

By the way, I have an older video on reading the structure of a book before you start, which is for before you read. This part is for after you close the book.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: EXPLAIN IT TO SOMEONE WITHOUT THE BOOK]**

The second thing you can do so you can explain what you read is to explain it to another person.

A lot of people call this the Feynman technique, after the physicist Richard Feynman, who was famous for explaining hard physics in simple words.

As far as I can find, Feynman never wrote it down as a technique himself. Other people wrote up the steps later.

The steps usually go like this. Pick the idea, explain it in simple words like you're talking to a beginner, notice where you get stuck, go back to the book, and try again.

The idea comes from stories like this one.

David Goodstein, a physicist at Caltech, wrote that he once asked Feynman to explain a hard idea in physics.

Feynman said he would prepare a lecture on it for first-year university students.

A few days later, he came back and said, "I couldn't do it. I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we don't really understand it."

**[ON SCREEN: "I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we don't really understand it." / Feynman, as told by David Goodstein]**

I used to run an after-school tutorial centre, and I loved teaching.

When you teach children, they keep asking why, and every why takes you one step deeper, until you reach a step you can't explain.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And there's a study that shows the one thing that makes explaining work.]**

In 2018, Aloysius Koh, Sze Chi Lee and Stephen Lim, researchers in Singapore, gave 124 students a text about the Doppler effect to study for ten minutes.

Then one group taught it to a camera with no notes.

Another group taught it by reading out a prepared script.

Another group wrote down everything they could remember, and the last group did maths problems.

A week later, they all took a surprise test.

The group that taught without notes and the group that wrote from memory did about the same, and both did better than the script group and the maths group.

The researchers concluded that the benefit of teaching came from the recall.

That's why I'd say, explain it without the book in your hand.

There's also something researchers at Stanford called the protégé effect. In their 2009 study, school children put more effort into learning when they were teaching a computer character than when they were learning for themselves.

And if you don't have anyone to explain it to, record a voice note to yourself and listen back.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: WHEN YOU UNDERSTAND IT BUT STILL CAN'T EXPLAIN IT]**

Sometimes you do understand a book, and you still explain it badly. That has a name too. It's called the curse of knowledge.

The curse of knowledge is when you know something so well that you can't imagine what it's like to not know it.

I first saw the next example in a Closed Stacks video called "Why You Can't Explain What You Read," and Chip and Dan Heath made it famous in their book Made to Stick.

In 1990, a Stanford student called Elizabeth Newton ran an experiment for her PhD.

One person, the tapper, tapped out a well-known song like "Happy Birthday" on a table.

Another person, the listener, had to guess the song.

The tappers predicted that listeners would get it about half the time.

Across 120 songs, listeners got three.

**[ON SCREEN: tappers predicted ~50% / listeners got 3 out of 120 (2.5%)]**

When you tap, you hear the song in your head. The listener only hears knocking.

When you finish a book, you're the tapper.

You skip steps because they feel obvious to you, and the person listening only hears knocking.

So explain the book to someone who hasn't read it, and watch their face.

If your friend looks lost, that shows you which step you skipped.

Making videos is the same test for me. Before I can explain something to you on camera, I have to be able to explain it.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the last segment is the fair counterpoint, so viewers who've been blaming themselves get a reason to stay]**

---

**[SEGMENT 8: SOMETIMES THE PROBLEM IS THE BOOK]**

Sometimes you can't explain a book because the writer didn't explain it well.

Closed Stacks goes into this in more detail, and I want to give you one study on it.

In 2006, Daniel Oppenheimer, a psychologist at Princeton, made versions of the same pieces of writing with more complicated words and with simpler ones.

People rated the writers of the complicated versions as less intelligent.

The paper won an Ig Nobel Prize, and its title is "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilized Irrespective of Necessity," which I think is the joke.

**[ON SCREEN: "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilized Irrespective of Necessity" / meaning: using long words when you don't need to]**

To be fair, some ideas are hard, and a book that takes effort isn't automatically badly written.

So this is what I would do.

If you've written down what you remember, explained it to someone, gone back to the page, and the same paragraph still makes no sense, I think it might be the writing.

Find another book or article on the same topic, because someone else might explain it better.

So tell me in the comments, what's a book you loved but couldn't explain when someone asked? Try explaining it to me in two sentences.

You finish a book, someone asks what it's about, and all you can say is, "It's really good, you should read it." There's research on why that happens.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Why You Can't Explain the Book You Just Finished

### Subtitle:
> What the research says about the feeling of understanding, why rereading makes it stronger, and what to do after you close the book.

### SEO Title:
> Why You Can't Explain What You Read (and How to Fix It)

### SEO Description:
> A plain look at the illusion of explanatory depth, rereading, recognition and recall, retrieval practice, learning by teaching and the curse of knowledge, with practical things to do after you finish a book.

---

You finish a book that you enjoyed, and you even underlined things. Then someone asks you what it was about, and all you can say is that it's really good and they should read it. In an older video, I described this as being left with a feeling about the book rather than the book itself. I wanted to understand why that happens, and the research on it is clearer than I expected.

In 2002, Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil at Yale asked people how well they understood everyday things like a zip, a flush toilet or a lock, on a scale from one to seven. Then they asked them to explain, step by step, how each one works, and then asked them a harder question about it. After each step, people rated themselves again, and the ratings kept falling. Keil later wrote that the drop often came with "shock and surprise." They called it the illusion of explanatory depth. The part I find most useful for reading is that the illusion was far stronger for explanations than for facts, procedures or stories. After a novel, most of us can say what happened. A non-fiction book that explains how or why something works is where we overrate what we know.

Most of us respond to that by rereading. In 2009, Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger surveyed 177 university students, and about 84% said they reread their notes or textbooks, with about 55% calling it their number one strategy. Only about one in ten mentioned practising recall. Rereading feels productive because the second time the words come easier, and we take that ease, which psychologists call fluency, as a sign of learning. In 2006, Roediger and Karpicke gave students a short science passage. Students who read it four times were the most confident about remembering it a week later. Five minutes after studying they were indeed ahead, but a week later they remembered about 40% of it, while students who read it once and then wrote down what they remembered three times kept about 61%.

The difference comes down to recognition and recall. Recognising a face and remembering the name that goes with it are different tasks. In 2015, Adam Blake, Meenely Nazarian and Alan Castel at UCLA asked 85 students to draw the Apple logo from memory, and only one got it right, though most of them used Apple products. When you reread, the book is in front of you and you only need recognition. When someone asks you to explain it, the book is gone and you need recall, which rereading never practised.

So the first thing I would do is close the book after each chapter and write what it said in my own words, then open it again and check what I missed. Those gaps are the parts you would have stumbled on. Robert Bjork calls this kind of effort a desirable difficulty, because it feels harder in the moment and helps you remember for longer. In their 2013 review of study techniques, John Dunlosky and his colleagues rated summarising, rereading and highlighting as low usefulness and practice testing as high, so I'd write any summary with the book closed. When I finish the whole book, I'd try the rule from Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren's How to Read a Book, which is to "state the unity of the whole book in a single sentence, or at most a few sentences."

The second thing is to explain the book to someone, without the book in your hand. People call this the Feynman technique, although as far as I can find, Feynman never wrote it down as a technique himself. David Goodstein tells a story of asking Feynman to prepare a first-year lecture on a hard physics idea, and Feynman coming back to say, "I couldn't do it. I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we don't really understand it." In 2018, Aloysius Koh, Sze Chi Lee and Stephen Lim found that students who taught a text to a camera without notes did about as well a week later as students who wrote down what they remembered, and better than students who taught from a script. Their conclusion was that the benefit of teaching came from the recall. Other studies point the same way. Catherine Chase and her colleagues found children worked harder to learn for a computer character they were teaching than for themselves, and John Nestojko and his colleagues found that simply expecting to teach a passage helped students recall more of it.

Sometimes you do understand, and you still explain it badly. That is the curse of knowledge. I first saw the clearest example of it in a Closed Stacks video, and Chip and Dan Heath made it famous in Made to Stick. In 1990, Elizabeth Newton at Stanford had people tap out well-known songs on a table while a listener guessed. Tappers expected listeners to get about half. Across 120 songs, listeners got three. The tapper hears the song and the listener hears knocking. When you have finished a book, you are the tapper, so the best thing you can do is explain it to someone who hasn't read it and watch where they get lost. In healthcare this is called teach-back, and the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality reminds clinicians that "you are checking how well you explained something, not testing the patient."

And sometimes the problem is the book. Daniel Oppenheimer found in 2006 that people rated writers who used needlessly complicated words as less intelligent. Some ideas really are hard, and a book that takes effort isn't automatically badly written. But if you've written down what you remember, explained it to someone and gone back to the page, and the same paragraph still makes no sense, I think it's fair to try another book on the same topic. I'd love to know which book you loved but couldn't explain when someone asked.

**8. Corrections / careful wording**
- **"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough"** is often credited to Einstein, with no good source. It's left out. The Feynman line used is from Goodstein's account.
- **The illusion is weaker for stories, it doesn't disappear.** The script says "much stronger for explanations" and "you can usually tell someone what happened."
- **Rereading wasn't worse at every point.** The five-minute result (rereaders ahead) is in the script, so the 40% against 61% claim is only about one week later.
- **Summaries aren't "useless".** Dunlosky rated them low usefulness, partly because of training. The script recommends closed-book summaries, which is your opinion ("I would").
- **The protégé effect** is from a study with children and a computer agent. The script says that, and doesn't claim it for adults reading books.
- **The Koh study had no live audience**, so the script says "taught it to a camera" and doesn't claim anything about teaching real people.
- **Your teaching background**: the script only says you ran an after-school tutorial centre and loved teaching (your stated facts). The children-asking-why line is written as a general point, not a story.
- **Avoided:** "we forget 70% of what we read in 24 hours" (a misread of Ebbinghaus's nonsense-syllable curve), the "learning pyramid" (people remember 90% of what they teach), which has no research behind its numbers, and learning styles.
