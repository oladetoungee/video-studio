**[SEGMENT 2: SEARCHING VS RESEARCHING]**

The difference between searching and researching is the first thing to get right.

I learned to see it this way from Joy Ogide, a creator I really like.

Her video "How to research like a professional" was the first video of hers I ever watched, it's the reason I found her channel, and it's linked in the description.

She explains that searching is when you look something up, read the first few results, and stop.

Researching is when you also ask where the information came from, who first said it, what people agree on, what they disagree on, and what's still missing.

She breaks research into four levels.

**[ON SCREEN: Joy Ogide's four levels of research. 1. Surface 2. Context 3. Strategic 4. Original intent]**

The surface level is your first look, with Google or AI.

The context level is where you track down your sources and where the idea came from.

The strategic level is where you compare your sources and look for the common ground and the gaps.

And the original intent level is where you add something of your own, even if it's only a question.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the promise of going further than the four levels is the reason to stay]**

In this video, I want to go further, into what researchers actually do at each of those levels.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: START WITH A QUESTION YOU CAN ANSWER]**

Proper research starts with a question that's specific enough to answer.

"How much does a master's in Estonia cost?" is too wide.

Cost for who? At which university? Only tuition, or rent and food as well? And starting in which year?

In research, this is called your research question, and when you write a thesis, it's one of the first things you have to pin down.

So I'd rewrite it like this.

**[ON SCREEN: "How much tuition would a non-EU student pay for a master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University, starting in 2027?"]**

Now the question tells you exactly what to look for.

And it shows you what you're not answering yet.

Rent and food are a second question, and you can research that one after.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: GO TO THE ORIGINAL SOURCE]**

When you research anything, find out where the information started.

Sources come in three types.

Primary sources are the original. The university's own fee page, the actual study, the law itself, or the person who was there.

Secondary sources talk about the original. A news article about a study, a blog post about tuition, or a YouTube video, including this one.

Tertiary sources sum up lots of other sources, like Wikipedia or an encyclopedia.

When you need a number or a rule, try to get to the primary source.

For our question, the primary source is Tallinn University's own tuition page.

A quick way to find a page like that is to search inside one website.

On Google, you type site:, then the website's address, then your search words.

**[ON SCREEN: site:tlu.ee tuition fees]**

You can also type site:.ee to see only Estonian websites, or site:.gov.ng to see only Nigerian government websites.

When I checked Tallinn University's page in October 2026, it listed the Human-Computer Interaction master's at 7,000 euros a year for non-EU students starting in 2027.

**[ON SCREEN: Tallinn University tuition page, checked October 2026. HCI master's, non-EU students, starting 2027: €7,000 a year]**

Then check the date.

Fees and rules change, and an old blog post can stay near the top of Google for years.

For example, the University of Tartu says that from 2026/2027, it doesn't offer tuition waivers to new students from outside the EU anymore.

So a blog from a few years ago telling you about Tartu's tuition waivers would send you the wrong way.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer now has their first practical habit, and the next segment promises how to tell if a source can be trusted]**

---

**[SEGMENT 5: CHECK WHO IS BEHIND A SOURCE]**

Before you trust a source, find out who is behind it.

Who wrote it, and how do they know what they're saying?

The fastest way to check is to leave the page and search for the person or the organisation in a new tab.

Two researchers at Stanford, Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, found that professional fact checkers do exactly this, and I go through that study in my video on how to think clearly.

The next thing to ask is whether anyone gains if you believe it.

With study abroad costs, some of the pages you'll find are written by agencies that sell application help.

It doesn't make them wrong, but check their numbers against the school's own page.

Money can sit behind science too.

In 2016, Cristin Kearns and her colleagues at UCSF published old industry documents showing that in the 1960s, a sugar industry group paid Harvard researchers to write a review on diet and heart disease.

**[ON SCREEN: the review: New England Journal of Medicine, 1967 / the documents: Kearns, Schmidt and Glantz, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2016]**

According to the UCSF team, the industry set the review's goal and saw drafts, and the review put the blame on fat and played down sugar.

And the sugar money wasn't disclosed in the paper.

So when you read a study, look for the funding section, which is usually near the end.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: PEER REVIEW, PREPRINTS AND CITATION CHASING]**

If your topic has research papers in it, the first thing to know is the difference between a peer-reviewed paper and a preprint.

Peer review means other experts in the field read and checked the paper before a journal or a conference published it.

A preprint is a paper the authors put online before that check, on websites like arXiv.

Preprints are useful because science moves fast, but treat them as "not checked yet".

In July 2023, a team in South Korea posted two preprints on arXiv saying they had made LK-99, a superconductor that worked at room temperature and normal pressure, and it went viral.

By August, Nature reported that an impurity called copper sulfide explained what the team had seen.

And when a lab in Germany grew pure crystals of LK-99, they were an insulator, which is the opposite of a superconductor.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And the second thing is the one I use the most when I'm reading for my thesis.]**

The second thing is citation chasing.

When you find one good paper, look at its reference list.

That's backward chasing. You go back to the older work it was built on.

Then look at who cited it afterwards.

On Google Scholar, there's a "Cited by" link under each result, and that's forward chasing. It shows you the newer work, including anyone who later disagreed.

**[ON SCREEN: backward = the papers it cites / forward = the papers that cite it ("Cited by" on Google Scholar)]**

I show you how I find and read papers in my video on how to read research papers.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: COMPARE YOUR SOURCES]**

Once you have a few good sources, compare them, and Joy Ogide calls this the strategic level.

If several independent sources say the same thing, you can trust it more.

But the word independent matters a lot.

Sometimes ten websites agree because they all copied from the same one place, and that's called circular reporting.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the toaster story is the payoff here, and it changes how people read "everyone says so"]**

My favourite example is the toaster.

In 2012, some students in the UK edited Wikipedia to say that a Scottish man called Alan MacMasters invented the electric toaster in 1893.

It wasn't true.

But newspapers repeated it, books repeated it, and a primary school in Scotland even held a day in his honour.

It lasted about ten years, until 2022, when a teenager got suspicious of the photo on the Wikipedia page.

**[ON SCREEN: the Alan MacMasters toaster hoax, 2012 to 2022 (BBC News, November 2022)]**

So when your sources agree, check if each one found it themselves, or if they all point back to the same place.

And when your sources disagree, ask why before you pick a side.

With our tuition question, you might see one site saying Estonia is free, another saying 4,000 euros, and another saying 7,000.

Tallinn University's own page lists 4,000 euros a year for EU students and 7,000 for non-EU students in the HCI programme. Both numbers are correct, for different people.

Comparing sources also shows you the gaps, like the Estonian police's student guide saying you need 880 euros a month in income for a study permit, which the tuition page never mentions.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: HOW TO USE AI FOR RESEARCH WITHOUT GETTING FOOLED]**

AI is really good at the surface level of research, but AI tools often get their sources wrong.

In March 2025, Klaudia Jaźwińska and Aisvarya Chandrasekar at Columbia's Tow Center for Digital Journalism tested eight AI search tools.

They gave each tool a quote from a news article and asked it to name the article, the publisher, the date and the link.

Across 1,600 tries, the tools got it wrong more than 60% of the time, and they rarely said they weren't sure.

Then in October 2025, a study led by the BBC and the European Broadcasting Union checked more than 3,000 answers about the news from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity.

45% of the answers had at least one significant issue, and 31% had serious problems with their sources.

**[ON SCREEN: Tow Center, March 2025: wrong in over 60% of 1,600 tests / EBU and BBC, October 2025: 45% had a significant issue, 31% had sourcing problems]**

**[~9:00 BEAT: the four AI habits are the most practical part of the video]**

Use AI to get the big picture, and to learn the words people use for your topic, so you can search better.

Ask it for its sources, and open every single one.

Check that the page says what the AI said it says.

And for a number or a rule, the primary source has the final word.

There are tools built for research, like Claude Science and Elicit, and I reviewed Claude Science in another video, but you still open the paper.

And AI can also make up citations completely, which I talk about in my video on how I use AI as a master's student.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: KEEP NOTES WHILE YOU RESEARCH]**

Keeping notes while you research saves you from finding the same thing twice.

Before I moved to Estonia, I had docs and spreadsheets everywhere, and I still got surprised by a lot when I arrived.

So for every fact you find, I'd write down four things.

What it says, the link, the date you checked it, and whether it's a primary or a secondary source.

**[ON SCREEN: "HCI tuition, non-EU, from 2027: €7,000 a year / tlu.ee/en/tuition-fees / checked Oct 2026 / primary"]**

The date matters because pages change.

Next year, that fee page might say something different, and your note tells you when it was true.

For research papers, a reference manager like Zotero keeps them all in one place, and I talk about it in my video on reading research papers.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: ADD YOUR OWN QUESTION]**

The last step of researching any topic is adding your own question, and Joy Ogide calls this the original intent level.

After all your sources, ask what's still missing.

With our example, maybe you still want to know if the fee could change in your second year, so ask the people who know and email the programme manager.

The school's website and the programme managers answer these questions, and you don't need to pay anyone for that.

In academic research, that missing question is where new research starts.

A thesis is basically a question the sources haven't fully answered yet, and mine is on the cognitive cost of switching between productivity tools.

So tell me in the comments, what's one thing you believed for years and later found out wasn't true?

### SHORT 4: Who paid for the study? (30-40 sec)

### SHORT 5: Preprint vs peer reviewed (25-35 sec)

Peer review means other experts checked a paper before a journal or a conference published it.

A preprint is a paper that's online before that check.

Within weeks, labs found an impurity explained the results, and pure crystals turned out to be an insulator.

Preprints are useful. Just treat them as not checked yet.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How to Research Any Topic Properly

### Subtitle:
> One everyday question, checked from the first Google result to the official page, and the habits researchers use along the way.

### SEO Title:
> How to Research Any Topic Properly: A Step-by-Step Guide

### SEO Description:
> A plain guide to researching anything, from writing a specific question and finding primary sources to spotting circular reporting and checking what AI tools tell you.

---

Most of us research the same way. We type a question into Google, read the first result, take a screenshot of the number, and move on. Joy Ogide, a creator whose video "How to research like a professional" was the first of hers I ever watched, calls that searching. Researching, in her words, is when you keep going and ask where the information came from, what people agree and disagree on, and what's missing. She describes four levels: a surface level, a context level where you track down sources, a strategic level where you compare them, and an original intent level where you add something of your own. I'm a master's student in Human-Computer Interaction writing a thesis, and I research every video I make, so I want to add what those levels look like in practice.

It starts with the question. "How much does a master's in Estonia cost?" sounds clear, but it has no single answer. It depends on who's asking, which university, whether you mean only tuition, and which year you'd start. A question like "How much tuition would a non-EU student pay for a master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University, starting in 2027?" can be answered, and it tells you when you've found the answer. In a thesis, that's the research question, and it's one of the first things you pin down.

Then you go to the original. A primary source is the original thing itself, like the university's fee page, the study, or the law. A secondary source talks about it, like a blog post or a news article, and a tertiary source sums up many sources, like Wikipedia. For a number or a rule, the primary source wins. On Google, typing site:tlu.ee tuition fees shows only results from Tallinn University's website, and when I checked it in October 2026, the HCI master's was listed at €7,000 a year for non-EU students starting in 2027. Dates matter as much as sources. The University of Tartu says it no longer offers tuition waivers to new non-EU students from 2026/2027, so an older blog about Tartu's waivers would mislead you.

Next, ask who is behind a source and whether anyone gains if you believe it. Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew at Stanford found that professional fact checkers leave a page quickly and search for who runs it, which they call reading laterally. Funding matters in science too. In 2016, Cristin Kearns, Laura Schmidt and Stanton Glantz published industry documents in JAMA Internal Medicine showing that a sugar industry group paid Harvard researchers for a 1967 review in the New England Journal of Medicine, which, according to their analysis, blamed fat and played down sugar. The funding wasn't disclosed in the paper.

If your topic involves research papers, learn the difference between peer-reviewed work and preprints. In July 2023, a team in South Korea posted preprints claiming LK-99 was a superconductor at room temperature and normal pressure. Within weeks, as Nature reported, other labs traced the results to a copper sulfide impurity, and pure crystals turned out to be an insulator. Citation chasing helps here. A paper's reference list takes you back to older work, and the "Cited by" link on Google Scholar takes you forward to newer work, including anyone who later disagreed.

When you compare sources, the word independent matters. In 2012, students edited Wikipedia to say a Scottish man named Alan MacMasters invented the electric toaster. Newspapers, books and even a primary school repeated it, and it lasted about ten years before a teenager grew suspicious of the photo in 2022. Many sources agreed, and they all came from one edit. When sources disagree, they are often answering different questions. Tallinn University lists €4,000 for EU students and €7,000 for non-EU students in the same programme, and both are correct.

AI is useful for the first look, but it gets sources wrong often. In March 2025, Klaudia Jaźwińska and Aisvarya Chandrasekar at Columbia's Tow Center found that eight AI search tools misidentified the source of news quotes more than 60% of the time across 1,600 tests. In October 2025, a study led by the BBC and the European Broadcasting Union found that 45% of AI answers about the news had at least one significant issue. So I ask AI for its sources, open every one, and check that the page says what the AI claimed.

Keep notes as you go. For every fact, I'd write what it says, the link, the date you checked it, and whether it's primary or secondary. Before I moved to Estonia, I had docs and spreadsheets everywhere, and I still got surprised by a lot. And at the end, ask your own question. If no source answers it, ask the people who know. A programme manager will answer an email about fees, and you don't need to pay anyone for that. In academic research, that unanswered question is where new work begins. I'd like to know one thing you believed for years before finding out it wasn't true.
