**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

You're in the middle of a ticket in Jira, and a Slack message comes in.

You answer it, then you see an email and reply to that too.

Then you go back to the ticket, and you have to read it again to remember where you were.

For a lot of us, that's a normal workday, with a few more apps and a lot of browser tabs in between.

That switching between apps costs a lot more than it looks like, and it's very close to what my master's thesis is about.

So in this video, I'll show you what the research says that cost is, why nobody sees it, and what you and your company can do about it.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: HOW OFTEN WE SWITCH BETWEEN APPS AT WORK]**

So how often do people actually switch between apps at work?

In 2022, Harvard Business Review published a study where the researchers tracked 137 people, in 20 teams, across three Fortune 500 companies, for up to five weeks.

Most of them worked in back-office teams, like finance, HR and supply chain.

On average, each person toggled between apps and websites nearly 1,200 times a day.

If you've watched my video on task switching, you've heard that number before.

So I want to show you two other numbers from the same study, because they say a lot about the tools.

The first one is about one supply chain task at a big consumer goods company.

To get that single task done, each person involved switched about 350 times, between 22 different apps and websites.

**[ON SCREEN: one task / 22 apps and websites / about 350 switches]**

And over a full day, one employee there toggled more than 3,600 times.

The second number is this one.

After 65% of switches, people moved to yet another app in less than 11 seconds.

So they'd open an app, spend a few seconds there, and leave again.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the "glue" quote is the reason to stay, because it names what the viewer does all day]**

The researchers wrote something about these workers that I keep thinking about.

They said, "A sizable part of their jobs is to act as the glue between disparate applications."

Disparate here means apps that don't connect to each other.

So the person becomes the connection.

You copy an order number from one system, paste it into another one, check a third one to confirm it went through, and send a message about it in a fourth.

One small note, because I'm a researcher and I care about this.

This was published as an article in a business magazine, so it wasn't peer-reviewed like an academic paper.

But academic research on switching at work points the same way, and I'll show you some of it in this video.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT YOU RELOAD WHEN YOU SWITCH APPS]**

Every time you switch apps at work, you have to reload your context.

Your context is everything you were holding in your head for the task you were doing.

Take a developer's day as an example.

You're fixing a bug from a Jira ticket.

In your head, you're holding which file you were in, which test was failing, what you already tried, and what you were going to try next.

Then Slack pings, and someone needs a quick answer about a different feature.

To answer them, you load a whole different context. Which feature they mean, what that code does, and what you told them about it last week.

Then you go back to the bug, and the first context has to come back.

You read the ticket again. You find the right tab. You scroll back to where you were in the file. And you try to remember what you were going to try next.

**[ON SCREEN: what you reload / the task / where you stopped / what you already tried / what comes next]**

In the Harvard Business Review study, the cost of each switch was a little over two seconds.

That's the extra time people needed to get going on the next step after they switched.

Two seconds sounds like nothing.

But across nearly 1,200 switches a day, they worked out that it came to just under four hours a week.

That's about five working weeks a year, or 9% of their time at work.

**[ON SCREEN: ~2 seconds a switch / just under 4 hours a week / ~9% of the working year]**

And two seconds is only the part that shows up on the screen.

The part in your head takes longer, and it has a name. It's called attention residue.

Sophie Leroy, a researcher who studies how people work, showed in 2009 that when you move from one task to another, part of your attention stays on the first task.

And in her studies, it was stronger when people left the first task unfinished.

That's most app switches at work, because a Slack message doesn't wait for you to finish your ticket.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHY YOUR WORK LOOKS FINE BUT YOU FEEL EXHAUSTED]**

There's a study I think explains why switching and interruptions can leave you exhausted, while your work still looks fine.

It's from 2008, by Gloria Mark and her colleagues, and the title is "The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress."

They gave people an email task to do, and some of them kept getting interrupted while they worked.

The people who got interrupted finished in less time, and the quality of their work didn't drop.

So if you only looked at the work, the interruptions did no harm.

But when the researchers asked people how they felt, the ones who got interrupted reported more stress, more frustration, more time pressure and more effort.

**[ON SCREEN: interrupted people / finished faster, same quality / more stress, frustration, time pressure, effort]**

The researchers think people make up for interruptions by working faster, and they pay for it in stress.

So the output stays the same, and the cost moves into the person.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer now has the reason they're tired on "productive" days, and the next segment promises why nobody at work can see it]**

That study was about interruptions, which isn't exactly the same as switching apps.

But at work, a lot of app switching starts with an interruption. A ping, a notification, a little red badge on your email.

And Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index looked at activity across Microsoft 365 and found that during core work hours, the average person got interrupted about every two minutes, by a meeting, an email or a chat message.

That's around 275 interruptions a day.

So if your day feels like it's made of other people's pings, the numbers back you up.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: WHY THE COST OF APP SWITCHING IS INVISIBLE]**

So why does nobody see the cost of switching between apps at work?

I think there are four reasons.

The first reason is that each switch is tiny.

Two seconds here, one quick reply there. Nobody writes any of that down.

The second reason is that every app only sees itself.

Slack can tell your company how many messages you sent. Jira can tell them how many tickets you closed.

But none of them can see the time you spent between them, getting from one app to another and finding your place again.

**[ON SCREEN: Slack sees messages / Jira sees tickets / nobody sees the gaps in between]**

The third reason has a name in my field. It's called articulation work.

Two researchers, Susan Leigh Star and Anselm Strauss, wrote about it in 1999.

Articulation work is all the coordinating you do so that the main work can happen.

Keeping track of who's doing what, lining things up, and making sure the pieces fit together.

And one thing they pointed out is that this kind of work is often invisible.

It doesn't show up as a result, and people usually only notice it when it's missing, when something falls through the cracks.

Being the glue between your apps is that kind of work.

The fourth reason comes from Gloria Mark's interruption study from 2008.

People can keep their output the same by working faster, so the cost shows up as stress, inside the person, where no report looks.

**[~6:00 BEAT: thesis moment, then the turn to what can be done]**

This is the part that's closest to my thesis.

I'm doing my master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University, and my thesis is on the invisible cognitive cost of switching between productivity tools.

I'm in my second year, and my plan is to finish my other courses this autumn, so spring 2027 is just the thesis.

So to be clear, everything in this video comes from published research. None of it is from my own thesis results.

What I can tell you is why I care about it.

I think most workplace tools get designed and measured one at a time, while people work across all of them at once.

So the cost lands in the gaps between the tools, and it's nobody's job to look there.

And that's where the fixes come in, some on your company's side and some on yours.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: WHAT COMPANIES CAN DO ABOUT APP SWITCHING]**

A lot of app switching at work comes from how the work is set up, so a big part of the fix sits with the company. The team lead, the manager, and whoever picks the tools.

The researchers behind the Harvard Business Review study gave some suggestions, so I'll start with theirs.

First, count the cost of every new app.

Their words were, "Rationalize the cost of introducing new applications."

Every new tool is one more place to check, and one more place where things can get lost.

So before adding one, I would ask what it replaces.

Second, they suggest approving new software "with actual hands-on users."

The person who buys a tool is often not the person who'll be switching into it all day.

Third, rebalance the work.

They noted that people whose jobs involve constantly switching between apps are more likely to get bored and distracted, and more likely to leave.

So they suggest spreading that kind of work across the team, so it doesn't all land on the same few people.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And the next two are my own, and they're the ones I'd push for first.]**

Next, decide where each kind of thing lives.

For example, decisions go in the ticket. Quick questions go in Slack. Anything that needs a proper reply goes in email.

So if a decision gets made in a Slack thread, someone writes it in the Jira ticket, and the next person doesn't have to search three apps to find it.

When the whole team uses each tool the same way, people can stop checking everything just in case.

Then, agree on how fast people need to reply.

If everyone believes a Slack message needs an answer in two minutes, everyone keeps Slack open and keeps switching to it.

If the team agrees that a couple of hours is fine for anything that isn't urgent, people can close it for a while and get on with their work.

There's a small study from 2012, by Gloria Mark and her colleagues, where they cut a group of office workers off from email for five working days.

Without email, people switched between windows less often, they stayed on each task longer, and heart rate monitors showed they were less stressed.

It was a small study, so I wouldn't tell your company to ban email.

But it shows that how your tools are set up changes how much you switch.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: HOW TO REDUCE APP SWITCHING AT WORK YOURSELF]**

If you can't change the tools at your job, there are still things you can do to reduce app switching yourself.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the most practical part of the video starts here, so it's a good place for the second half of the audience to stay]**

The first one is to get to a stopping point before you switch.

Attention residue is stronger when you leave a task unfinished.

So if you can take one more minute to finish the sentence you're writing or the test you're running, do that, and then go and answer the message.

The second one is to leave yourself a note before you switch.

In my video on task switching, I said I leave myself a one-line note about where I stopped and what comes next.

It turns out there's research on exactly this.

In 2018, Sophie Leroy and Theresa Glomb tested what they called a "ready-to-resume plan."

Before switching, people took a moment to write down where they were in the task and what they'd do when they came back.

And that plan reduced attention residue, and people did better on the task they switched to.

**[ON SCREEN: a ready-to-resume note / "BOOK-142: order total is wrong when a discount is applied. Tried the rounding fix, didn't work. Next: check the tax step."]**

So your note helps the thing you're coming back to, and it also helps the thing you're switching to.

The third one is to check Slack and email at set times.

I batch my replies, so I pay the switching cost once, instead of every time a message lands.

You can do it every hour, or whatever your job allows, and keep notifications off in between.

The fourth one is to keep open only what the task needs.

For example, one browser window for the task you're on, and close the tabs from the last one.

Every app that's open and blinking at you is an invitation to switch.

And the fifth one is to notice when you interrupt yourself.

Gloria Mark has also studied self-interruptions, which are the times we switch with nothing pinging us at all.

So when you feel the urge to check Slack, ask yourself if anything actually came in.

And if your team has no agreement on where things live, you can be the one who asks for it.

So tell me in the comments, which two apps do you switch between the most at work?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> The Work of Being the Glue Between Your Apps

### Subtitle:
> What switching between Slack, email, Jira and your browser tabs costs you, why nobody sees it, and what you and your team can do about it.

### SEO Title:
> The Hidden Cost of Switching Between Apps at Work

### SEO Description:
> A plain look at the research on app switching at work, including the HBR toggling study, attention residue, Gloria Mark's work on interruptions, and practical fixes for teams and individuals.

---

You're in the middle of a ticket in Jira when a Slack message comes in. You answer it, notice an email that needs a reply, deal with that too, and when you go back to the ticket you have to read it again to remember where you were. Most office days are full of small loops like that one, and I've become very interested in what they cost, partly because my master's thesis in Human-Computer Interaction is about the invisible cognitive cost of switching between productivity tools. Nothing in this essay comes from my own results. It all comes from published research.

In 2022, Rohan Narayana Murty, Sandeep Dadlani and Rajath B. Das wrote in Harvard Business Review about tracking 137 people in 20 teams across three Fortune 500 companies for up to five weeks. On average, each person toggled between apps and websites nearly 1,200 times a day. At one consumer goods company, finishing a single supply chain task took each person involved about 350 switches across 22 applications and websites, and after 65% of switches, people moved to yet another app in less than 11 seconds. The authors wrote that "a sizable part of their jobs is to act as the glue between disparate applications." I should say that this was a magazine article and not a peer-reviewed paper, and the lead author founded a company that builds workplace software, but the academic work on switching points in the same direction.

Every switch means reloading context. If you're fixing a bug, you're holding which file you were in, which test was failing, what you already tried and what you meant to try next, and a quick question from a colleague replaces all of that with a different feature and a different set of details. The HBR authors measured the visible part of this at a little over two seconds a switch, which came to just under four hours a week, or about 9% of the working year. The part in your head lasts longer. Sophie Leroy's 2009 research on attention residue showed that when people move from one task to another, part of their attention stays on the first, and that this is stronger when the first task is left unfinished. Most switches at work happen before anything is finished.

The cost also tends to stay out of the work itself. In 2008, Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke published "The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress." People who were interrupted while doing an email task finished faster, with no drop in quality, and reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. The researchers' explanation is that people make up for interruptions by speeding up. Anyone looking only at the output would see nothing wrong.

I think that is the main reason the cost of switching stays invisible. Each switch is tiny. Every tool measures what happens inside it, so Slack can count messages and Jira can count tickets, and nothing counts the time between them. And the coordinating work that holds everything together is what Susan Leigh Star and Anselm Strauss called articulation work in 1999, the kind of work that tends to be noticed only when it's missing and something falls through the cracks. Being the glue between apps is that kind of work.

A lot of the fix sits with whoever designs the work. The HBR authors suggest that leaders "rationalize the cost of introducing new applications," approve new software "with actual hands-on users," and spread switch-heavy work across a team. I would add two things of my own. Decide where each kind of thing lives, so decisions go in the ticket and quick questions go in chat, and agree on how fast replies are expected, so nobody has to keep a chat app open all day in case something lands. A small 2012 study by Gloria Mark, Stephen Voida and Armand Cardello, which took a group of office workers off email for five working days, found they switched windows less, stayed on tasks longer, and showed less stress on heart rate monitors. It was a small study, but it shows that how tools are set up changes how much people switch.

There are also things you can do on your own. Get to a stopping point before you switch, since unfinished tasks leave more residue. Before you switch, write one line about where you are and what you'll do next. Sophie Leroy and Theresa Glomb tested exactly that in 2018 and called it a "ready-to-resume plan," and it reduced attention residue and helped people do better on the task they switched to. Check messages at set times instead of the moment they arrive, keep open only what the current task needs, and notice the times you switch with nothing pinging you at all, which Gloria Mark has also studied.

None of this makes the gaps between tools go away, but it makes them smaller and easier to see. I'd like to know which two apps you switch between the most at work.

- Supports "part of your attention stays on the first task" (Segment 3) and "it was stronger when people left the first task unfinished" (Segments 3 and 7). Her first study compared people who finished the first task with people who didn't. The second found that finishing under time pressure reduced residue, which the script doesn't use.

**3. The ready-to-resume plan (Leroy and Glomb, 2018)**
Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). "Tasks Interrupted: How Anticipating Time Pressure on Resumption of an Interrupted Task Causes Attention Residue and Low Performance on Interrupting Tasks and How a 'Ready-to-Resume' Plan Mitigates the Effects." Organization Science, 29(3), 380-397. https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184
- Supports Segment 7. The plan is a short note made before switching about where you are in the interrupted task and what you'll do when you come back. It reduced attention residue and improved performance on the interrupting task (the one people switched to). The script says exactly that and doesn't claim it speeds up getting back to the first task.

**4. Interrupted work: more speed and stress (Mark, Gudith and Klocke, 2008)**
Mark, G., Gudith, D., & Klocke, U. (2008). "The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress." Proceedings of CHI 2008, 107-110. https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072
- Supports Segment 4. People in the interrupted conditions completed an email task in less time with no difference in quality, and reported significantly higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort (on the NASA-TLX workload scale). The authors' interpretation is that people compensate for interruptions by working faster. The script says "the researchers think," because that part is their interpretation.

**5. Articulation work (Star and Strauss, 1999)**
Star, S. L., & Strauss, A. (1999). "Layers of Silence, Arenas of Voice: The Ecology of Visible and Invisible Work." Computer Supported Cooperative Work, 8(1-2), 9-30. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008651105359
- Supports Segment 5. The script explains articulation work in plain words (the coordinating that lets the main work happen) and says it's "often invisible" and noticed when it's missing. It doesn't quote the paper. "The work of making the work work" is a common teaching paraphrase, so it's been left out to avoid presenting it as their words.

**6. Self-interruptions (Gloria Mark)**
Dabbish, L., Mark, G., & González, V. M. (2011). "Why do I keep interrupting myself? Environment, habit and self-interruption." Proceedings of CHI 2011. https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979405
