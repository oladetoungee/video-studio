**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

I read almost every day.

I have a full-time job, and I'm doing a full master's degree.

And I still read books that I'm not mandated to read.

By books that I'm not mandated to read, I mean non-academic books.

Of course, I'm doing a master's degree, so I have to read the books that pertain to my program.

But I also read books outside of that. Books that won't hurt me if I don't read them, but I still read them anyway.

I think I read a lot, so I have a lot of opinions about reading.

So in this video, I'll tell you how I read every day, what I've been reading this year, and what the research says about reading fiction.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: I USE READING TO REPLACE DOOMSCROLLING]**

So how do you read every day when you have a full-time job and a master's?

How do you read like that?

I think for me, you have to use it to replace something.

Most of us don't have an empty hour sitting around, waiting for a book.

For me, one thing I always do is use books to replace doomscrolling. That's like my one thing.

And I'll be honest, I'm not a heavy doomscroller. My screen time is already low for an average person.

But I want it lower, because I don't see the benefit.

So the time I would have spent scrolling, I spend reading.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the first "how" has landed. The research on swapping one habit for another is the reason to stay.]**

And there's research on swapping one thing for another like this.

The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer calls it an implementation intention. In plain words, it's an if-then plan.

You decide ahead of time, "if this happens, then I'll do that."

For example, "if I reach for my phone to scroll, then I'll open my book."

In 2006, Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined 94 tests of these plans.

On average, making an if-then plan had a medium-to-large effect on whether people reached their goals.

**[ON SCREEN: if-then plans, 94 tests combined: a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006]**

Then in 2011, Marieke Adriaanse, Gollwitzer and their colleagues tested plans that replace a habit with something else.

The plan took away the old habit's head start. When the moment came, the old habit no longer came to mind faster than the new thing.

But the new thing didn't become automatic straight away. It only won when people really wanted it.

So the book you swap in has to be one you actually want to read.

If you want more ideas for what to do instead of doomscrolling, I made a whole video on that, and I'll link it.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: I READ WHILE I EAT]**

Another way I read every day is while I'm eating.

If I'm eating, instead of watching a movie or doing something else, I read.

Think about how many times you eat in a day.

That's two or three small windows, every single day, and they add up.

If you read 20 pages a day, that's 7,300 pages in a year.

If a book is about 300 pages, that's about 24 books.

**[ON SCREEN: 20 pages a day x 365 days = 7,300 pages = about 24 books of 300 pages]**

You don't need a free afternoon to be a reader.

The time you already spend eating is enough to start.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: THERE IS ALWAYS A BOOK IN MY BAG]**

Another thing that helps me read every day is that I take books out.

I love to take books out with me.

If you jam me anywhere, there's nowhere you jam me and you will not see a book in my bag.

There is always a book in my bag.

So when I'm waiting on something, I pick up my book, instead of my phone.

I think if your book is at home and your phone is in your pocket, the phone is going to win most of the time.

So put the book where your hand already goes.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the next segment is about what she's reading now, and the writer she loves]**

---

**[SEGMENT 5: READ THE BOOKS YOU ENJOY]**

If you want to read every day, read the books that you enjoy.

And what I enjoy has changed this year.

I love reading the kind of books that people would call self-help books.

But since I started my master's program, I've noticed that I've been reading a lot less of the quote-unquote serious books.

I've been doing that less over the past year, I'm not gonna lie, because I'm in school and I'm doing a lot of school reading.

So most of my reading this year has really been fiction.

And I have also started to enjoy fiction, and started to really like fiction.

One of the writers I've found recently is Khaled Hosseini.

Oh my goodness, I love Khaled Hosseini so much.

If you don't know him, he's an Afghan-American novelist.

And I think enjoying it is what makes the reading happen every day.

If you only pick books because you feel you should, it's hard to keep picking them up.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: IS READING FICTION PRODUCTIVE?]**

When people ask how to read every day, I would say, of course you want to read something that is productive for you.

A lot of people think of fiction as something that is not so productive.

I don't think so.

I've always liked fiction, even though there was a season of my life where I read way less fiction.

And there's research on this, so I want to be careful here.

In 2013, David Kidd and Emanuele Castano published a study in the journal Science.

People read a short piece of literary fiction, or nonfiction, or popular fiction, or nothing at all.

Then they took tests of something called theory of mind.

Theory of mind is your ability to understand what other people are thinking and feeling.

One of the tests shows you photos of just the eyes of different people, and you pick the emotion you think they're feeling.

The people who read literary fiction did better, and the study got a lot of attention.

**[ON SCREEN: a short piece of literary fiction, then a theory of mind test. Kidd and Castano, Science, 2013]**

**[~6:00 BEAT: the twist. Other labs tried to repeat it.]**

But when other researchers tried to repeat it, it didn't hold up well.

In 2016, Maria Eugenia Panero and her colleagues ran it again across three labs, with 792 people, and reading one short passage made no difference.

And in 2018, a big project led by Colin Camerer repeated 21 social science experiments from Nature and Science. This was one of the ones that didn't replicate.

So I won't tell you that one chapter of a novel makes you more empathetic.

But in that 2016 study, there was one thing that did show up. People who had read more fiction over their lives did better on the eyes test.

And when researchers put many studies together, they find a small effect.

In 2017, Micah Mumper and Richard Gerrig combined studies on people's reading over their lives. People who read more fiction tended to score a bit higher on empathy and theory of mind, and the link was small.

In 2018, David Dodell-Feder and Diana Tamir combined 14 experiments, and reading fiction had a small positive effect on understanding other people, compared with reading nonfiction or nothing.

**[ON SCREEN: one short passage, no clear effect / years of fiction reading, a small link. Panero et al. 2016; Mumper and Gerrig 2017; Dodell-Feder and Tamir 2018]**

So the honest answer is that the effect is small, and scientists are still working out how it works.

I read Khaled Hosseini's books because I love them, and if they help me understand people a little better, that's a bonus.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: "And the last thing I want to say is the opinion I hold most strongly about reading."]**

---

**[SEGMENT 7: JUST READ]**

Apart from the kind of reading, how do you even read at all, any day?

Because one of the opinions I hold now is that you should just read, honestly.

You don't even have to be like, oh, I'm reading fiction, or I want to read self-help.

Just freaking read, literally.

It's just like, how do you not read every day?

And reading books has some interesting research behind it.

In 2016, Avni Bavishi, Martin Slade and Becca Levy at Yale looked at 3,635 people in the US, all aged 50 or older, for up to 12 years.

After they accounted for things like age, education, health and wealth, the people who read books had about a 20% lower risk of dying during those years than people who didn't read.

**[ON SCREEN: book readers, about 20% lower risk of dying over up to 12 years. Bavishi, Slade and Levy, Social Science and Medicine, 2016]**

It's an observational study, so it can't prove that reading caused it.

But I'll take it.

And I say almost every day, because some days it doesn't happen.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the "missing a day is fine" finding is the payoff for anyone who has given up on a reading streak]**

In 2010, Phillippa Lally and her team at University College London followed people trying to build a new daily habit.

For the people whose data they could track well, it took 66 days in the middle, and anywhere from 18 to 254 days, before the habit felt automatic.

And missing one day didn't really change how the habit formed.

**[ON SCREEN: a new habit took 18 to 254 days, 66 in the middle. Missing one day didn't really matter. Lally et al., 2010]**

So if you miss a day, pick the book up again the next day.

What are you reading right now? Tell me in the comments. I'd love to know.

### SHORT 7: Missed a day of reading? (20-30 sec)

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How I Read Every Day With a Full-Time Job and a Master's

### Subtitle:
> I still read books nobody asks me to read. This is how I make room for them, and what the research says about reading fiction.

### SEO Title:
> How to Read Every Day With a Full-Time Job and a Master's Degree

### SEO Description:
> A master's student with a full-time job on reading almost every day: replacing scrolling with books, reading while eating, carrying a book, reading fiction, and what the research on fiction and empathy really shows.

---

I read almost every day. I have a full-time job, I'm doing a full master's degree, and I still read books I'm not mandated to read. By that I mean non-academic books. Of course I have to read the books that pertain to my program, but I also read books outside of that, books that won't hurt me if I don't read them. I read them anyway, and people ask me how.

For me, you have to use reading to replace something. The one thing I always do is use books to replace doomscrolling. I'm not a heavy doomscroller, and my screen time is already low, but I want it lower because I don't see the benefit. There's research behind this kind of swap. The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer calls it an implementation intention, which is an if-then plan you make ahead of time, like "if I reach for my phone to scroll, then I'll open my book." When Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined 94 tests of these plans in 2006, making one had a medium-to-large effect on whether people reached their goals. A 2011 study by Marieke Adriaanse, Gollwitzer and their colleagues found that a plan to replace a habit took away the old habit's head start in people's minds, though the new behaviour only won when people really wanted it. So the book you swap in has to be one you want to read.

I also read while I eat. If I'm eating, instead of watching a movie or doing something else, I read. That's two or three small windows every day, and they add up. If you read 20 pages a day, that's 7,300 pages in a year, which is about 24 books of 300 pages. You don't need a free afternoon to be a reader.

And I take books out with me. There is always a book in my bag, so when I'm waiting on something, I pick up my book instead of my phone. I think if your book is at home and your phone is in your pocket, the phone is going to win most of the time.

What I read has changed. I love the kind of books people would call self-help, but since I started my master's, I've been reading a lot less of the quote-unquote serious books, because I'm doing a lot of school reading. So most of my reading this year has been fiction, and I've started to really like it. One of the writers I've found recently is Khaled Hosseini, and I love his books so much. I think enjoying what you read is what makes the reading happen every day.

A lot of people think fiction isn't productive. I don't think so, and the research is interesting, though it needs careful reading. In 2013, David Kidd and Emanuele Castano published a study in Science where people who read a short piece of literary fiction did better on tests of theory of mind, which is your ability to understand what other people think and feel. It got a lot of attention. But when Maria Eugenia Panero and her colleagues repeated it across three labs with 792 people in 2016, reading one short passage made no difference, and a large 2018 project led by Colin Camerer couldn't replicate it either. What does show up is smaller and slower. In Panero's study, people who had read more fiction over their lives did better on the test. Micah Mumper and Richard Gerrig's 2017 meta-analysis found that people who read more fiction tend to score a bit higher on empathy and theory of mind, and David Dodell-Feder and Diana Tamir's 2018 meta-analysis found a small positive effect of reading fiction on social understanding. The effect is small, and scientists are still working out how it works. I read Khaled Hosseini's books because I love them, and if they help me understand people a little better, that's a bonus.

One of the opinions I hold now is that you should just read. You don't have to decide first whether you're reading fiction or self-help. Books in general have some interesting research behind them too. In 2016, Avni Bavishi, Martin Slade and Becca Levy at Yale followed 3,635 people aged 50 and over for up to 12 years, and after accounting for things like age, education, health and wealth, the people who read books had about a 20% lower risk of dying in that time. It's an observational study, so it can't prove reading caused it, but I'll take it.

I say almost every day because some days it doesn't happen, and that's fine. In 2010, Phillippa Lally and her team at University College London followed people building a new daily habit. It took anywhere from 18 to 254 days to feel automatic, with 66 days in the middle, and missing a single day didn't really change how the habit formed. So if you miss a day, pick the book up again the next day. And tell me, what are you reading right now?

Supports "combined 94 tests" and "medium-to-large effect." https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 (summary checked on the University of Konstanz repository: https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)
- **Adriaanse, Gollwitzer, De Ridder, De Wit and Kroese (2011), "Breaking habits with implementation intentions: A test of underlying processes," Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 37, 502-512.** Three experiments. Plans that replace a habitual response removed the habit's cognitive head start (the habit was no longer more accessible than the alternative), but the alternative did not immediately become automatic, and people acted against the habit only to the extent they held strong goals for the alternative. Supports "took away the old habit's head start" and "only won when people really wanted it." https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211399102 (abstract checked at https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/breaking-habits-implementation-intentions-test-underlying-processes)

- **Panero et al. (2016), "Does reading a single passage of literary fiction really improve theory of mind? An attempt at replication," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.** Three independent groups, 792 participants; none of the original effects replicated; greater lifetime exposure to fiction correlated with better performance on the eyes test. - **Camerer et al. - **Mumper and Gerrig (2017), "Leisure reading and social cognition: A meta-analysis," Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 11(1), 109-120.** Lifetime fiction reading was associated with empathy and theory of mind; the effects were small. https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000089 (summary checked at https://onfiction.ca/2017/10/effects-of-leisure-reading.html)
- **Dodell-Feder and Tamir (2018), "Fiction reading has a small positive impact on social cognition: A meta-analysis," Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 147(11), 1713-1727.** 53 effect sizes from 14 studies; reading fiction compared with nonfiction or no reading gave a small, statistically significant improvement (g about .15 to .16). Supports "combined 14 experiments" and "small positive effect." https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000395

**3. Book reading and longevity (Segment 7)**
- **Bavishi, Slade and Levy (2016), "A chapter a day: Association of book reading with longevity," Social Science and Medicine, 164, 44-48.** 3,635 Health and Retirement Study participants aged 50 and over, followed up to 12 years; after adjusting for covariates including age, sex, education, health, wealth, marital status and depression, book readers had about a 20% lower mortality risk (HR 0.80).
