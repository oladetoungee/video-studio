**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

In October 2026, the US government ordered every civilian federal agency to remove DeepSeek, the Chinese AI chatbot, from its systems.

The reason they give is that your data could end up with the Chinese government.

AI is so technical. But it's also such a political thing.

I find this whole thing very interesting, and I feel like there's a lot of hypocrisy in it.

For the record, all my devices are Apple, and I haven't used DeepSeek myself.

So I'll show you what was banned and why, the best argument for it, where I think the hypocrisy is, and what it means for us in Nigeria.

**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT THE US ACTUALLY BANNED]**

So what exactly has the US banned when it comes to Chinese AI?

Mostly one company, DeepSeek.

DeepSeek is an AI company from Hangzhou in China, and in January 2025, its chatbot app went to number one on Apple's App Store in the US.

On the 24th of January 2025, the US Navy sent an email telling its people not to use DeepSeek "in any capacity."

The House of Representatives told congressional offices that DeepSeek was "under review" and "currently unauthorized for official House use."

NASA barred it from agency devices and networks, and the Pentagon's IT agency blocked the website.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the states and the October order are coming, and the viewer wants to know how far this has gone]**

Then the states joined in.

Texas banned DeepSeek on state devices on the 31st of January 2025, along with RedNote and Lemon8.

New York followed on the 10th of February, and Virginia on the 11th.

In Congress, two lawmakers, Josh Gottheimer and Darin LaHood, introduced a bill called the No DeepSeek on Government Devices Act. As of October 2026, it still hasn't made it out of committee.

And then came the biggest one.

On the 19th of October 2026, the Department of Homeland Security made public an order that was signed back in July.

It tells every civilian federal agency to remove DeepSeek products from their systems and stop buying them.

It even covers other companies' products that are built on DeepSeek.

**[ON SCREEN: Jan 2025 Navy, House, NASA, Pentagon / Jan to Feb 2025 Texas, New York, Virginia / Feb 2025 No DeepSeek on Government Devices Act (still in committee) / Oct 2026 DHS order for all civilian federal agencies]**

One thing I want to be clear about.

All of these bans are about government devices, government systems and government contracts.

If you're an ordinary person in the US, or in Nigeria, nothing here stops you from downloading DeepSeek and using it.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHY THE US SAYS IT BANNED DEEPSEEK]**

So why does the US say it banned DeepSeek?

There are four main reasons, and other countries gave some of the same ones.

The first reason is where your data goes.

DeepSeek's own privacy policy said the information it collects is "stored in secure servers located in the People's Republic of China."

**[ON SCREEN: DeepSeek privacy policy: "stored in secure servers located in the People's Republic of China"]**

And the new DHS order says using DeepSeek "may result in the transfer of sensitive data to the PRC if used by U.S. government employees or vendors."

The second reason is a Chinese law.

China's National Intelligence Law, from 2017, says in Article 7 that "All organizations and citizens shall support, assist, and cooperate with national intelligence efforts in accordance with law."

The same article says they have to keep that intelligence work secret.

**[ON SCREEN: National Intelligence Law, Article 7 (China Law Translate)]**

Think of it like this.

You keep your diary at a friend's house, and your friend's country has a law that says if intelligence officers ask for anything in the house, your friend has to help them and keep quiet about it.

Your friend might be a very nice person. The law still applies to them.

That's the worry with a Chinese company holding your data.

The third reason is security.

In January 2025, a security company called Wiz found one of DeepSeek's databases open on the internet, with chat history inside it.

And in September 2025, a US government AI centre called CAISI tested DeepSeek's models.

With a common jailbreak trick, DeepSeek's R1-0528 model went along with 94% of clearly harmful requests. The American models they compared it to went along with 8%.

The fourth reason is censorship.

When New York banned it, Governor Kathy Hochul talked about protecting people from "state-sponsored censorship."

And that same CAISI report found that DeepSeek's models "echoed four times as many inaccurate and misleading CCP narratives as U.S. reference models did." CCP means the Chinese Communist Party.

**[ON SCREEN: CAISI, Sept 2025 / jailbreak: 94% vs 8% / CCP narratives: 4x]**

And it wasn't only the US.

Italy's data protection authority blocked DeepSeek on the 30th of January 2025. DeepSeek had told them European privacy law didn't apply to it, and the authority disagreed.

South Korea paused new downloads for about two months over how the app handled personal data.

And Australia and Taiwan banned it on government devices.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer has all the official reasons, and the next segment promises the best case for the bans before the twist]**

---

**[SEGMENT 4: THE STRONGEST CASE FOR BANNING DEEPSEEK]**

Before I tell you where I think the hypocrisy is, I want to give the best argument for banning DeepSeek on government devices, because a lot of it is fair.

First, governments lock down their devices for American AI too.

In 2023, the US House of Representatives limited staff to the paid version of ChatGPT, for certain tasks only.

In 2024, it told staff they couldn't use Microsoft's Copilot.

And Italy, the same country that blocked DeepSeek, blocked ChatGPT for about a month in 2023, and in December 2024 it fined OpenAI 15 million euros.

Second, the law around a Chinese company is different.

In the US, companies can fight the government in court.

For example, Microsoft refused to hand over a customer's emails that were stored in Ireland, and that fight went all the way to the US Supreme Court in 2018.

China's Article 7 says companies "shall" cooperate with intelligence work and keep it secret, so a public fight like that is hard to picture.

Third, China does the same thing.

OpenAI doesn't offer ChatGPT in China, and in 2024 it also blocked API access from China. Google and Facebook have been blocked there for years.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And stay for this last one, because it changes what the bans actually cover.]**

Fourth, the bans are narrower than they sound.

DeepSeek released its model as open weights. That means anyone can download the model and run it on their own computers.

If you run it on your own server, your data doesn't go to China at all.

Even inside the Pentagon, staff could still use DeepSeek's model through an approved platform called Ask Sage, which doesn't connect to Chinese servers.

The one thing to watch is that the new October order also covers other companies' products "developed using DeepSeek implementations," and it doesn't define exactly what that means.

So if I was arguing for the bans, I'd say this. The US isn't stopping anyone from using the model. It's stopping government data from going to a company that has to cooperate with Chinese intelligence. And I think that's a reasonable thing for a government to want.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: THE HYPOCRISY: AMERICA'S OWN SURVEILLANCE LAWS]**

So where do I think the hypocrisy is in the US banning Chinese AI?

The main argument against DeepSeek is that a Chinese company can be forced to give your data to the Chinese government.

Europe has been making that exact argument about American tech companies for years.

Remember the diary at your friend's house.

Now put the diary at an American friend's house.

The US has a law called FISA Section 702, from 2008.

It lets US intelligence agencies, with approval from a special court, make American companies hand over the communications of people who aren't American and are outside the US.

That's most of us watching from Nigeria.

**[ON SCREEN: FISA Section 702 / targets non-Americans outside the US / data held by US companies]**

In 2020, the European Union's top court looked at that law and struck down the deal that let companies move Europeans' data to America.

The court said US surveillance under laws like Section 702 wasn't limited to what's strictly necessary, and that Europeans had no proper way to challenge it in court.

Then there's the CLOUD Act, from 2018.

It says that American companies have to hand over data when they get a valid US legal order, even if that data is stored in another country.

And Microsoft has been asked about this directly.

In June 2025, Microsoft France's director of public and legal affairs, Anton Carniaux, sat in front of the French Senate.

They asked him if he could guarantee, under oath, that French citizens' data would never be sent to the US government without France agreeing.

He said, "No, I cannot guarantee that," and then added, "but, again, it has never happened before."

**[ON SCREEN: Anton Carniaux, Microsoft France, French Senate, June 2025: "No, I cannot guarantee that"]**

To be fair to the US, after the 2020 ruling it changed some rules, and in 2023 Europe and the US agreed a new data deal with a new review court.

This is how I see it.

When the US says it can't trust an app because a foreign government can reach the data, it's describing exactly what other countries say about American apps.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the viewer has the core of the hypocrisy argument, and the next segment promises TikTok and the free-flow-of-data story]**

I believe the honest version of the US argument is "we don't trust China."

And that's a reasonable position for a government to take.

My problem is when it gets presented as a principle about protecting people's data, because the US doesn't hold its own companies to that principle when the data belongs to the rest of us.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: FREE FLOW OF DATA, AND THE TIKTOK DEAL]**

The second place I see hypocrisy in the US banning Chinese tech is in how it talks about data crossing borders.

For years, US trade policy pushed for data to flow freely between countries, and against rules that make companies store data at home. It wrote that into deals like the one with Mexico and Canada in 2019.

Then in October 2023, the US pulled its support for those same proposals at the World Trade Organization, saying it needed "policy space" to work out its own rules.

And since April 2025, a US Justice Department rule has restricted sending large amounts of Americans' sensitive data to China and five other countries.

Now, protecting your citizens' data is reasonable. Nigeria has a data protection law that does the same thing.

My problem is that the US spent years telling other countries that keeping data at home was a trade barrier, and now it does a version of it too.

Then there's TikTok.

In April 2024, the US passed a law saying TikTok had to be sold by its Chinese owner, ByteDance, or be banned in the US.

In January 2025, the Supreme Court said the law was allowed.

Then the President delayed enforcing it several times.

And in January 2026, it ended in a deal.

A new US company now runs TikTok in America, with Oracle, Silver Lake and MGX each holding 15%, and Oracle running the algorithm in its US cloud.

ByteDance kept nearly 20%.

**[ON SCREEN: TikTok law April 2024 / Supreme Court Jan 2025 / deal Jan 2026 / ByteDance keeps nearly 20%]**

People who support the deal say that was the point. Control of the app and the algorithm moved to the US.

But I think if TikTok was as dangerous as the law said, it's strange that it ended in a business deal where the company it was meant to remove still owns a share.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: WHO GETS TO USE AI: CHIPS AND EXPORT CONTROLS]**

The third place I see hypocrisy is in who the US lets use AI at all.

Since October 2022, the US has restricted selling advanced AI chips to China.

The US says it's about national security, mainly about China's military using American chips.

In January 2025, the US went further and published a rule that split the whole world into three groups for AI chips.

The US and 18 close partners had almost no limits.

China and countries under a US arms embargo were mostly blocked.

And everybody else, which included Nigeria, sat in the middle, with limits on how much computing power they could buy.

**[ON SCREEN: AI Diffusion Rule, Jan 2025 / group 1: US + 18 partners / group 3: China + arms-embargoed countries / group 2: everyone else, including Nigeria]**

That rule was cancelled in May 2025, before it took effect.

Then in December 2025, President Trump said Nvidia could sell its H200 chips to approved customers in China, as long as 25% went to the United States.

So chips that were a national security risk could be sold to China, if the US got a cut.

I think that tells you national security can be a bargaining position as well as a principle.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the most personal part for the audience starts here, because it shows American AI can be switched off too]**

**[OPTIONAL CUT START: if you'd rather not include the Anthropic example, cut from here to OPTIONAL CUT END]**

And it isn't only chips.

In June 2026, the US Commerce Department sent Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, a directive that required a licence before any foreign person could use two of its newest models.

Anthropic said it couldn't block only foreign users at such short notice, so it switched those models off for everyone, everywhere.

The directive was lifted at the end of June.

I use Claude every day for work, so that one caught my attention.

**[OPTIONAL CUT END]**

So American AI can be cut off by the American government, for reasons that have nothing to do with you.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: WHAT THE US BAN ON CHINESE AI MEANS FOR NIGERIA]**

So what does the US banning Chinese AI mean for countries like Nigeria?

We use American and Chinese tech side by side.

Huawei, a Chinese company, has been in Nigeria since 1999 and has become one of the biggest suppliers of our telecom networks.

In 2018, China's Export-Import Bank lent Nigeria 328 million dollars for a national internet project run by Galaxy Backbone, the government's own tech company, and Huawei worked on it.

And at the same time, most of us run our lives on Gmail, WhatsApp, Microsoft and ChatGPT.

And Chinese AI is already here.

In January 2026, a Microsoft report found that in several African countries, including Ethiopia, Zimbabwe, Uganda and Niger, DeepSeek had roughly 11 to 14 percent of the AI chatbot market.

Microsoft said being free had "lowered the barrier for millions of users."

So for us, the choice isn't a clean one.

Whichever app you use, your data probably leaves Nigeria, and it sits under another country's law. America and China both have laws that let their governments reach it.

Nigeria's Data Protection Act from 2023 already says personal data should only go to countries with adequate protection, and the regulator's guidance lists government access to the data as one of the things to check.

That's the same question, and I think it should be asked about American companies and Chinese companies the same way.

So this is what I would tell you.

If you work in government, or you handle client data, don't paste anything sensitive into any free chatbot, American or Chinese.

At my workplace, there's no rule about AI tools. We just use the ones the company uses. So if your workplace doesn't have a rule either, you have to be careful yourself.

Before you trust a tool with something important, ask whose law can reach that data, and ask it the same way for both countries.

If you build products with AI, remember that open models like DeepSeek's can run on servers you choose, and keep a backup provider, so one decision in Washington or Beijing doesn't switch off your product.

And I'd like Nigeria's own rules to hold American and Chinese companies to the same standard.

So tell me in the comments. Is the US right to ban DeepSeek on government devices, or is it hypocrisy?

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> The US Banned DeepSeek for a Reason Europe Has Used Against America for Years

### Subtitle:
> What the US actually banned, the strongest case for it, where I think the hypocrisy is, and what it means for Nigerians who use American and Chinese tech every day.

### SEO Title:
> US DeepSeek Ban Explained: Security or Hypocrisy?

### SEO Description:
> A plain look at the US bans on DeepSeek as of October 2026, the reasons given, the fair case for them, US surveillance law and the TikTok deal, and what it all means for Nigeria.

---

AI is so technical, but it's also such a political thing, and this story shows it. In October 2026, the US Department of Homeland Security made public an order, signed in July, telling every civilian federal agency to remove DeepSeek products from its systems and stop buying them, including other companies' products built on DeepSeek. It came after almost two years of smaller bans. In January 2025 the US Navy told its people not to use DeepSeek "in any capacity," the House of Representatives called it "currently unauthorized for official House use," and NASA and the Pentagon's IT agency blocked it. Texas banned it on state devices on 31 January 2025, and New York and Virginia followed in February. All of these are about government devices, systems and contracts. An ordinary person in the US, or in Nigeria, can still download DeepSeek and use it.

The reasons given are serious. DeepSeek's privacy policy said the information it collects is "stored in secure servers located in the People's Republic of China," and Article 7 of China's 2017 National Intelligence Law says that "All organizations and citizens shall support, assist, and cooperate with national intelligence efforts in accordance with law," and keep that work secret. In January 2025, the security company Wiz found a DeepSeek database open on the internet with chat history in it. In September 2025, the US government's Center for AI Standards and Innovation tested DeepSeek's models and found that, with a common jailbreak, its R1-0528 model complied with 94% of clearly harmful requests, against 8% for the American models it was compared with, and that DeepSeek's models "echoed four times as many inaccurate and misleading CCP narratives as U.S. reference models did." Other governments acted too. Italy's data protection authority blocked DeepSeek in January 2025 after the company said European privacy law didn't apply to it, South Korea paused new downloads for about two months, and Australia and Taiwan banned it on government devices.

The fair case for the bans is stronger than people on my side of this argument usually admit. Governments restrict American AI on their own devices as well. The US House limited staff to the paid version of ChatGPT in 2023 and told them not to use Microsoft's Copilot in 2024, and Italy blocked ChatGPT for about a month in 2023 before fining OpenAI 15 million euros in December 2024. American companies can fight their government in court, as Microsoft did over emails stored in Ireland, a case that reached the US Supreme Court in 2018. China blocks Google, Facebook and ChatGPT, and in 2025 its internet regulator reportedly told big Chinese companies to stop buying a Nvidia chip. And DeepSeek's model is open weights, so anyone can run it on their own servers without sending data to China. Pentagon staff were able to use it that way through an approved platform called Ask Sage.

Where I think it becomes hypocritical is that the main argument against DeepSeek, that a company can be forced to hand your data to its government, is the argument Europe has made about American companies for years. Section 702 of FISA lets US intelligence agencies, with approval from a special court, require American companies to hand over the communications of non-Americans outside the US. In 2020, in the Schrems II case, the EU's top court struck down the EU-US Privacy Shield because US surveillance under laws like Section 702 wasn't limited to what is strictly necessary and Europeans had no effective way to challenge it. The 2018 CLOUD Act requires American companies to hand over data under a valid US order even when it is stored abroad. In June 2025, Anton Carniaux of Microsoft France was asked by the French Senate whether he could guarantee, under oath, that French citizens' data would never be sent to the US government without France's agreement. He said, "No, I cannot guarantee that," and added that it had never happened. The US did change some rules after 2020, and Europe and the US agreed a new data framework in 2023. I still think the honest version of the US position is "we don't trust China," which is a reasonable thing for a government to say. My problem is when it's presented as a principle about protecting people's data that the US doesn't apply to its own companies.

The same pattern shows up in trade and in TikTok. For years, US trade policy pushed for free data flows and against rules that make companies store data at home, and it wrote that into deals like the 2019 agreement with Mexico and Canada. In October 2023, the US withdrew support for those proposals at the World Trade Organization, and since April 2025 a Justice Department rule has restricted sending bulk sensitive data about Americans to China and five other countries. Protecting citizens' data is reasonable, and Nigeria does it too, but the US spent years calling that kind of rule a trade barrier. The 2024 law that said TikTok must be sold or banned was upheld by the Supreme Court in January 2025, enforcement was delayed several times, and in January 2026 it ended in a joint venture with Oracle, Silver Lake and MGX each holding 15%, Oracle running the algorithm in its US cloud, and ByteDance keeping nearly 20%. Supporters say control moved to the US, which was the point. I find it strange that a security threat ended as a business deal where the company it was meant to remove still owns a share.

Access to AI itself follows the same logic. Since 2022, the US has restricted advanced AI chip sales to China. In January 2025, it published a rule that split the world into three groups for AI chips, with Nigeria in the middle group with limits, though that rule was cancelled in May 2025 before it took effect. In December 2025, President Trump said Nvidia could sell H200 chips to approved customers in China if 25% went to the United States. And in June 2026, a Commerce Department directive required a licence before foreign persons could use two of Anthropic's newest models. Anthropic said it couldn't block only foreign users at short notice and switched the models off for everyone until the directive was lifted at the end of June. I use Claude every day for work, so that one caught my attention. American AI can be switched off by the American government too.

For Nigeria, none of this is somebody else's fight. Huawei has been in Nigeria since 1999 and has become one of the biggest suppliers of our telecom networks, and in 2018 China's Export-Import Bank lent 328 million dollars for a national internet project run by Galaxy Backbone. At the same time, most of us run our lives on Gmail, WhatsApp, Microsoft and ChatGPT. A Microsoft report in January 2026 found DeepSeek had roughly 11 to 14 percent of the AI chatbot market in several African countries, including Ethiopia, Zimbabwe, Uganda and Niger, partly because it's free. Whichever app we use, our data probably leaves Nigeria and sits under another country's law. The Nigeria Data Protection Act of 2023 already says personal data should only go where it is adequately protected, and the regulator's guidance lists government access to that data as one of the things to check. I'd like that question asked of American and Chinese companies the same way. In the meantime, I wouldn't paste anything sensitive into any free chatbot, I'd ask whose law can reach the data before trusting a tool with something important, and if I was building a product on AI, I'd keep a second provider ready.

I'd like to know where you land. Is the US right to ban DeepSeek on government devices, or is it hypocrisy?
