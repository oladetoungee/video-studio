**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT DESIGNING FOR DEATH MEANS]**

Designing for death starts with one question. What happens when your user dies?

One of the things that led to accessibility in design is when people started asking questions like, what if my user is blind? What if my user needs this, or needs that?

And asking those questions has established a standard for accessibility in UX, UI, and design. Things like the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines that a lot of websites follow today came out of that kind of thinking.

But one of the parts that I've started to think about is designing for death.

What happens when your users die? How do you design for the death of your user?

**[~1:00 BEAT: the name for this idea and the Oxford numbers are the reason to stay, because they show it's a real field and a real scale]**

It turns out researchers in my field have been asking this for more than fifteen years.

In 2009, Michael Massimi and Andrea Charise wrote a short paper for CHI, which is the biggest conference in human-computer interaction.

And they gave the idea a name. Thanatosensitivity.

Thanatos is the Greek word for death.

They described it as an approach that "actively integrates the facts of mortality, dying, and death into HCI research and design."

In plain words, you design with the fact that the people using your product will die one day, and that other people will be left with everything they leave behind.

Massimi went on to work with bereaved parents, and in a 2012 interview with the University of Toronto, he said something about Facebook that I keep thinking about.

"Seeing a friend complain about a mediocre latte while you are mourning the loss of your child just doesn't work."

**[ON SCREEN: Massimi & Charise, CHI 2009 / "thanatosensitivity" / thanatos = death]**

And this isn't a small problem.

In 2019, Carl Öhman and David Watson at the Oxford Internet Institute estimated that if Facebook got no new users after 2018, the dead could outnumber the living on Facebook by 2070.

In their other scenario, where Facebook keeps growing, Nigeria alone made up more than 6% of the dead profiles by the year 2100.

So if you're building for African users, this is your problem too.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT GOES WRONG WHEN AN APP DOESN'T KNOW A USER HAS DIED]**

So what does it look like when an app doesn't know that one of its users has died?

The first thing is reminders.

In July 2024, Amanda Silberling, a writer at TechCrunch, wrote that Facebook reminded her, twice in one week, to wish two friends who had died a happy birthday.

The title of her article was "Facebook keeps asking me to say happy birthday to dead people."

Facebook does try to prevent this. In 2019, Sheryl Sandberg, who was Facebook's chief operating officer at the time, wrote that they use AI to keep people who have died out of things like birthday reminders, even before the account is memorialized.

But neither of Silberling's friends' accounts had been memorialized, and the reminders still came.

The second thing is ads.

In December 2018, Gillian Brockell, a video editor at the Washington Post, wrote an open letter to tech companies after her baby was stillborn.

She had searched and posted about her pregnancy, and the baby ads kept coming after her loss.

A Facebook ad executive, Rob Goldman, replied that there was a setting to block ads about painful topics like parenting, and that it "still needs improvement."

The system knew so much about her life, and it had no idea about her loss.

And if you build apps, think about every automated email you send.

If you run a bookstore app, for example, is it still sending "we miss you, come back for 20% off" to someone who has died?

**[~3:00 BEAT: the viewer has seen the painful side. The next segment asks the harder question of who should get into the account, and it doesn't have an easy answer]**

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHO SHOULD GET INTO A DEAD PERSON'S ACCOUNT?]**

The next problem with designing for death is access. Who should get into an account after the person dies?

Think about how we secure accounts today.

A lot of us use two-factor authentication, so a code goes to your phone every time you log in somewhere new.

Now imagine the family needs the photos in that person's cloud account.

The code goes to a phone that might be locked, or switched off, or the number might be cancelled.

So the security that protected that person can lock out the people they left behind.

The other side of this is privacy, and I think it's the strongest argument against giving families everything.

A dead person's messages are also other people's messages.

When Facebook explained in 2017 why it doesn't let families read a dead person's messages, Monika Bickert from Facebook's policy team said, "In a private conversation between two people, we assume that both people intended the messages to remain private."

**[ON SCREEN: "In a private conversation between two people, we assume that both people intended the messages to remain private." Monika Bickert, Facebook, 2017]**

And courts don't all agree.

In 2018, Germany's highest court for civil cases ruled that parents could inherit the Facebook account of their 15-year-old daughter, who died after being hit by a train.

The parents wanted to read her messages to understand what happened to her.

The court said the account could be inherited, in a similar way to letters and diaries.

So on one side, a family is locked out of photos and memories. On the other side, a person might have had conversations they never wanted anyone to read.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: So which one is right? I don't think there's one answer for everybody, and the next part shows how the big apps chose.]**

I believe the fairest answer is to let the person decide while they're alive.

And that's what the better tools try to do.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: WHAT FACEBOOK, INSTAGRAM, GOOGLE AND APPLE OFFER WHEN YOU DIE]**

So what do the big apps offer when a user dies? This is as of October 2026.

Let's start with Facebook.

When Facebook learns that someone has died, the account can be memorialized. The word "Remembering" shows next to the person's name, nobody can log in, and the profile is kept out of things like birthday reminders and friend suggestions.

Then in February 2015, Facebook launched the legacy contact.

A big part of the research behind it came from Jed Brubaker, an HCI researcher who's now at the University of Colorado Boulder. He worked on it with Vanessa Callison-Burch at Facebook, and they wrote about it in a 2016 paper.

A legacy contact is someone you choose while you're alive. After you die, they can pin a post on your profile, change your profile and cover photos, accept friend requests, and if you allowed it, download a copy of what you shared.

They can't log in as you, and they can't read your messages.

So the privacy question from earlier is built into the design.

Brubaker said something in 2018 that I love. "I just hope that as a result of my work, death is a little bit kinder to people."

Next is Instagram, and this is what that TikTok was about.

For years, Instagram has had memorialized accounts, with "Remembering" next to the name and nobody able to log in. Immediate family can also ask Instagram to remove the account, with documents like a death certificate.

And depending on where you live, you might also see options in your Instagram settings to choose a legacy contact, or to have your account deleted after you die.

I couldn't confirm it's in every country yet, so check your own settings.

**[~6:00 BEAT: Google and Apple are next, and they're the two most people use for photos, so this is where the video becomes about the viewer's own phone]**

Then Google. Google has something called Inactive Account Manager.

You choose how long Google should wait, 3, 6, 12 or 18 months without you using the account.

You can pick up to 10 people, and choose what each of them gets, like your Gmail, Drive or Photos.

When the time is up, they get a link to download it, and they have three months to save it.

And if you set nothing, since December 2023 Google can delete personal accounts that haven't been used for two years, including what's inside them.

And then Apple. Apple calls it Digital Legacy, and it came with iOS 15.2 in December 2021.

The legacy contact you add gets an access key.

After you die, they need that access key and your death certificate to ask Apple for access.

They can get your photos, messages, notes, files, device backups and more.

They can't get films, music or books you bought, or anything in your iCloud Keychain, like passwords, passkeys and payment information.

And the access lasts three years from when Apple approves it. After that, the account is deleted for good.

**[ON SCREEN: Facebook keeps messages private / Apple includes messages]**

And this is the interesting part for me as a UX person.

Facebook keeps your messages private, even from your legacy contact. Apple gives your legacy contact your messages.

Two big companies looked at the same question and made opposite choices.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: WHAT THE LAW SAYS ABOUT YOUR DATA AFTER YOU DIE]**

So what does the law say about your data after you die? Less than you'd think.

In the EU, GDPR is the big data protection law, and it doesn't cover people who have died.

Recital 27 says, "This Regulation does not apply to the personal data of deceased persons."

It leaves each country to make its own rules.

Estonia, where I live, did. Under section 9 of Estonia's Personal Data Protection Act, a person's data stays protected for ten years after they die, or twenty years if they died as a child.

And during that time, using their data usually needs the consent of close family.

Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023 is different. As far as I could find, it doesn't mention people who have died at all.

**[ON SCREEN: GDPR Recital 27 / Estonia: protected 10 years after death (20 for a child) / Nigeria NDPA 2023: nothing on the dead]**

In the US, most states have passed a version of a law called RUFADAA, the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act.

One thing it says is that if you used a company's own online tool to choose who gets access to your account, that choice generally comes before what your will says.

So in the US at least, the setting inside the app can matter more than your will.

And in a country where the law says nothing about it, the app's own policy decides most of what happens.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: IF YOU BUILD APPS, DESIGN FOR WHEN YOUR USERS DIE]**

If you build apps, designing for death is something you can start on in your next sprint.

I have ranted about Uber leaving Nigeria and what happened to people's data. In that video I said everybody is always about the onboarding, and nobody asks you to design the exit of users.

But what about these situations? Death is one more exit, and it will come for every single user you have.

So these are the questions I'd ask about any app I work on.

One. How does our app find out that a user has died, and who are we going to believe?

Two. Can a user choose someone to look after their account, and what can that person do and not do?

Three. Can the family get photos and files out, without logging in as the person who died?

Four. What should stop? Birthday reminders, memories, marketing emails, ads, and "we miss you" messages.

Five. What happens to payments? Is a subscription still charging the card every month?

Six. What stays private, even from family?

And seven. What words are we using? There's a big difference between a profile that says "Remembering" and an email that says "Account of deceased user."

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: And some of this is a very small change in code.]**

If you're using Laravel, for example, it can be one line in a query.

**[CODE ON SCREEN]**
```php
// Skip birthday reminders for memorialized accounts
$birthdays = $user->friends()
    ->whereNull('memorialized_at')
    ->whereMonth('birthday', now()->month)
    ->whereDay('birthday', now()->day)
    ->get();
```

The hard part is deciding who is allowed to set that column, and how you check.

For you as a user, the one thing I'd do today is set a legacy contact.

**[~9:00 BEAT: this is the practical payoff of the whole video, so the second half of the audience stays for the steps]**

If you have an iPhone like me, go to Settings, tap your name, then Sign-In and Security, then Legacy Contact.

**[ON SCREEN: Settings / your name / Sign-In & Security / Legacy Contact]**

For Google, go into your Google Account settings, and under Data and privacy, look for the option to make a plan for your digital legacy. That's Inactive Account Manager.

For Facebook and Instagram, search your settings for "memorialization" or "legacy contact."

Then tell the person you picked. With Apple, they'll need the access key, so make sure they know where it is.

And if you lost someone recently and this video brought it back, I'm sorry. Take your time with any of this.

So tell me in the comments, has an app ever reminded you of someone who died? And have you set your legacy contact?

### SHORT 3: What if my user dies? (25-35 sec)

One of the things that led to accessibility in design is when people asked, what if my user is blind? What if my user needs this or that?

In 2009, two HCI researchers, Michael Massimi and Andrea Charise, gave this a name. Thanatosensitivity. Thanatos is the Greek word for death.

It means designing with the fact that your users will die one day, and people will be left with what they leave behind.

### SHORT 6: GDPR doesn't cover the dead (25-35 sec)

GDPR is the EU's big data protection law, and it doesn't apply to people who have died.

Recital 27 says it in plain words. "This Regulation does not apply to the personal data of deceased persons."

It leaves each country to decide. Estonia, where I live, protects your data for ten years after you die, or twenty if you were a child.

Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023, as far as I could find, doesn't mention people who have died at all.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> What If My User Dies?

### Subtitle:
> We learned to ask "what if my user is blind?". This is what happens when apps never ask about death, and what you can do about your own accounts today.

### SEO Title:
> What Happens to Your Data When You Die? Designing for Death in UX

### SEO Description:
> A plain look at thanatosensitive design, what Facebook, Instagram, Google and Apple offer when a user dies, what GDPR, Estonian, Nigerian and US law say, and how to set a legacy contact.

---

I saw a TikTok where someone found a setting in their Instagram for what happens to the account after they die, and I couldn't stop thinking about it. I study human-computer interaction, and one of the things that led to accessibility in design is when people started asking questions like "what if my user is blind?" or "what if my user needs this, or needs that?". Those questions helped build the standards a lot of websites follow today. So I started asking a different one. What happens when your users die, and how do you design for that?

Researchers have been asking it for a while. In 2009, Michael Massimi and Andrea Charise wrote a short paper for CHI, the main conference in my field, and named the idea thanatosensitivity, from the Greek word for death. They described it as an approach that "actively integrates the facts of mortality, dying, and death into HCI research and design." Massimi went on to work with bereaved parents, and in a 2012 interview with the University of Toronto he said, "Seeing a friend complain about a mediocre latte while you are mourning the loss of your child just doesn't work." The scale is growing too. In 2019, Carl Öhman and David Watson at the Oxford Internet Institute estimated that if Facebook got no new users after 2018, the dead could outnumber the living on it by 2070. In their scenario where Facebook keeps growing, Nigeria alone made up more than 6% of the dead profiles by 2100.

When an app doesn't know that one of its users has died, it keeps doing what it was built to do. In July 2024, Amanda Silberling wrote in TechCrunch that Facebook reminded her twice in one week to wish two friends who had died a happy birthday. Facebook has said since 2019 that it uses AI to keep people who have died out of birthday reminders, but her friends' accounts were never memorialized, and the reminders came anyway. In 2018, Gillian Brockell, a video editor at the Washington Post, wrote an open letter to tech companies after her baby was stillborn, because the baby ads kept coming. A Facebook ad executive replied that there was a setting to block painful ad topics and that it "still needs improvement." If you build apps, the same thing is probably happening with your automated emails. A bookstore app's "we miss you, come back" email doesn't know who has died.

Access is the hardest part. Most of us protect our accounts with two-factor authentication, so after someone dies, the code goes to a phone that might be locked, switched off, or attached to a cancelled number, and the family can't get to the photos. If nobody does anything, the account can go away on its own, since Google has been able to delete personal accounts unused for two years since December 2023. And yet giving families everything has its own cost, because a dead person's messages are also other people's messages. When Facebook explained in 2017 why families can't read a dead person's messages, Monika Bickert said, "In a private conversation between two people, we assume that both people intended the messages to remain private." In 2018, Germany's highest civil court disagreed in one case and let parents inherit their 15-year-old daughter's Facebook account, treating it in a similar way to letters and diaries. I don't think there's one right answer for everyone. I believe the fairest answer is to let people decide while they're alive.

That's what the better tools try to do. Facebook launched the legacy contact in February 2015, with research from Jed Brubaker, now at the University of Colorado Boulder, working alongside Vanessa Callison-Burch at Facebook. A legacy contact can pin a post, change the profile and cover photos, accept friend requests and, if you allowed it, download what you shared, but they can't log in or read your messages. Instagram memorializes accounts with "Remembering" next to the name, lets immediate family ask for removal, and in some countries now offers a legacy contact or deletion after death. Google's Inactive Account Manager lets you pick a waiting period of 3 to 18 months and up to ten people, who then get three months to download what you chose for them. Apple's Digital Legacy, since iOS 15.2, gives your legacy contact your photos, messages, notes and files if they have your access key and a death certificate, but not your passwords or purchased media, and only for three years. Brubaker once said, "I just hope that as a result of my work, death is a little bit kinder to people," and I think you can see that in how carefully these limits were drawn, even when two companies drew them in opposite places.

The law doesn't settle much of it. GDPR's Recital 27 says, "This Regulation does not apply to the personal data of deceased persons," and leaves the rest to each country. Estonia, where I live, protects a person's data for ten years after death, or twenty if they died as a child, and using it during that time usually needs close family's consent. Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023, as far as I could find, doesn't mention people who have died. In most US states, the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act says that a choice you made in a company's own online tool generally comes before your will. So in a lot of cases, the setting inside the app decides what happens.

If you build apps, a few questions go a long way. How will you find out a user has died, and who will you believe? Can users choose someone to look after the account, and what can that person do? Can the family get photos and files out without logging in as the person? Which reminders, emails and ads should stop, and are subscriptions still charging a card? What stays private, even from family? And what words are you using, "Remembering" or "deceased user"? When I wrote about Uber leaving Nigeria, I said everybody is always about the onboarding and nobody designs the exit. Death is one more exit, and it will happen to every user you have.

For you as a user, the one thing I'd do today is set a legacy contact. On an iPhone it's in Settings, under your name, Sign-In and Security, then Legacy Contact. On Google, look for the plan for your digital legacy under Data and privacy. On Facebook and Instagram, search your settings for "memorialization" or "legacy contact." Then tell the person you picked, and if it's Apple, tell them where the access key is. And if this brought back someone you've lost, I'm sorry. Take your time with it.
