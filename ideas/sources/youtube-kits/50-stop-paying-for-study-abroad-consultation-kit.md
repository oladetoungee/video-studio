**[SEGMENT 2: YOU DON'T NEED TO PAY FOR STUDY ABROAD CONSULTATION]**

If you want to study abroad, you don't need to pay anybody for consultation.

Don't let anybody scam you.

You have the school.

The school has their website.

You can write an email to them if you are confused about anything.

A lot of these consultants are doing exactly what you can do for yourself.

They go to the same website you can go to.

They read the same requirements page, and they fill the same application form.

They're not doing anything for you that you can't do.

So just find the school, apply to the school.

In fact, you can reach out to the programme manager of the school, write an email, and you will get actual responses.

A programme manager is the person, or the small team, that runs the programme at the university. Their email is usually right there on the programme page.

**[SEGMENT 3: WHERE TO FIND FREE, OFFICIAL STUDY ABROAD INFORMATION]**

If you want to apply to study abroad by yourself, you need to know where the free, official information is.

The first place is always the university's own website.

Find the page for the exact programme you want, and read everything on it. The requirements, the deadlines, the fees, and how to apply.

A lot of universities also have a page for applicants from specific countries. Tallinn University, for example, has a page just for applicants from Nigeria.

Then a lot of countries have an official website for international students.

**[ON SCREEN: Study in Estonia / Study in Germany + DAAD / Study in NL (Nuffic) / Campus France Nigeria / EducationUSA / Erasmus Mundus catalogue]**

There's Study in Estonia.

There's Study in Germany, where you can search programmes through the DAAD database or the Higher Education Compass, which lists more than 20,000 degree programmes.

There's Study in NL for the Netherlands, run by Nuffic.

For France, Campus France has offices in Nigeria, in Abuja and in Lagos.

For the US, EducationUSA has advising centres in Nigeria. The Lagos centre says it runs free counselling sessions every Wednesday.

And for Erasmus Mundus, the European Union has a catalogue of the joint master's programmes it supports.

I want to talk about three of these properly.

Study in Estonia tells you to contact the admission office at the university directly if you have questions about a programme.

It also says one online application can cover several programmes, and that application fees at Estonian universities range from 0 to 120 euros, and they're non-refundable.

So yes, you might pay a fee. You pay it to the university, through the university's own system.

The Erasmus Mundus catalogue doesn't take applications at all. It tells you to go to each programme's official website and contact the consortium, which is the group of universities that runs it.

Most of them take applications between October and January, for the next academic year. If you want scholarships, I made a full video on scholarships for African students in Europe.

And Campus France and EducationUSA are the ones you can actually walk into in Nigeria and ask questions, so you don't need a middleman to talk to them.

All of this is as of October 2026, so check the official page before you apply.

**[SEGMENT 4: HOW TO APPLY FOR A MASTER'S ABROAD BY YOURSELF]**

If you want to apply for a master's abroad by yourself, this is how I would do it.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the step-by-step is the payoff for people who came from search]**

First, find the programme.

For me, I saw the programme lead of my programme posting on LinkedIn, and I decided that was the programme I wanted.

So follow the people who run the programmes you like. You'll learn a lot about the programme before you even apply.

Second, read the whole programme page, then read the page for your country, then the page about the English test.

Third, make a list of every document they ask for, with the deadline next to it.

Before I moved, I had docs and spreadsheets everywhere. That's what a consultant would do for you anyway, and you can do it in one evening.

Fourth, if something isn't clear, email the programme. I'll show you the email in a second.

Fifth, apply early.

My programme's application page says that if you apply as soon as possible, the admission specialists can give you feedback before the deadline, and you get a chance to update your application.

**[ON SCREEN: HCI at Tallinn University, autumn 2027: applications 1 Nov 2026 to 1 Mar 2027]**

That feedback is free. It comes from the people who actually decide.

**[SEGMENT 5: HOW TO EMAIL A PROGRAMME MANAGER BEFORE YOU APPLY]**

If you're confused about anything when you're applying to study abroad, you can email the programme manager. This is the kind of email I would send.

**[ON SCREEN: the email template]**

```
Subject: Question about the MSc [programme name], autumn 2027 intake

Hello,

My name is [your name], and I'm planning to apply for the MSc [programme name] for the autumn 2027 intake.

I have a bachelor's degree in [your degree] from [your university] in Nigeria, and I have worked as a [your role] for [number] years.

I have read the requirements on the programme page, and I have one question. [Your question. For example: Would my work experience in software development count for the "previous experience" requirement?]

Thank you for your time.

Kind regards,
[your name]
```

Keep it short.

Say who you are in one or two lines.

Ask one or two clear questions.

And please, read the page first. If the answer is already on the page, you're asking them to read it to you.

For my programme, the email is on the programme page. It's hci-studies@tlu.ee.

Every programme has its own, so look for the contact on the page of the programme you want.

**[SEGMENT 6: YOU CAN WRITE YOUR MOTIVATION LETTER YOURSELF]**

A lot of people pay someone to write their motivation letter for them, and I don't think you need to.

My main advice for the application is to sell your work and your experience well.

In the motivation letter, sell your experience and your passion.

If you like the programme enough, you won't find it hard to write.

Mine was so long that I had to keep cutting it.

And be original.

A consultant can't write your passion for you, because they don't know what you've done or why you want this.

The UK's Migration Advisory Committee spoke to international graduates for a 2024 review, and one of them said about their personal statement, "I think the agency wrote it for me."

**[SEGMENT 7: YOU ALREADY HAVE AN INTERVIEW, YOU DON'T NEED CONSULTATION]**

Someone recently replied on one of my videos, and they were like, oh yeah, they already have an interview, and they want to pay me for consultation for the interview.

Like, bro, you already got to the point where you have an interview.

The reason why you have an interview with these people in the first place is because they thought you were worth interviewing.

You have impressed them to that point.

You just have to continue doing whatever it is that you did to that point.

You don't need any consultation.

For my programme, on the current admission page, the interview is 60 percent of your score, and it's mostly follow-up questions about you and what you've done.

Nobody can answer those for you.

**[~6:00 BEAT: her mock interview story is the practical answer]**

Of course you can check and ask people.

When I was about to do my interview, I did some mock interviews with some people that were doing the programme as well.

But not in a way where I depended on them, like, oh, I need to do this for me to be selected.

So ask people. Practise out loud. Just don't hand your confidence to somebody you're paying.

**[SEGMENT 8: STUDY ABROAD AGENT SCAMS AND THE RED FLAGS]**

Study abroad agent scams are real, and they've hurt real students.

In 2023, Canada set up a task force after hundreds of international students faced removal because their study permit applications had fake letters of acceptance.

Canada's immigration department said those cases involved unauthorised consultants who gave their clients fraudulent acceptance letters.

And this is the line from the Canadian government I want you to remember.

"The onus remains on applicants to provide truthful information on their applications even if a third party is preparing it."

So if your agent lies, you're the one who carries it.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT LINE]**

And if you think this only happens to other countries, Nigeria's own government has warned about it.

In October 2022, Nigeria's Ministry of Foreign Affairs warned about "unscrupulous elements parading themselves as agents" selling admissions and jobs in Northern Cyprus, and said "many young Nigerians have become stranded."

Then in November 2025, the UK, Canada and Australia started a joint campaign against visa fraud, and they launched it in Nigeria.

They named the red flags.

**[ON SCREEN: red flags: excessive fees / pressure to pay quickly / promises of jobs without proper qualifications / offers to bypass standard requirements / a "guaranteed" visa]**

Demands for excessive fees.

Pressure to pay quickly.

Promises of jobs without proper qualifications.

Offers to bypass the standard requirements.

And promises of guaranteed visas. The missions said nobody can guarantee a visa.

I would add two of my own.

If someone guarantees you admission, that's a red flag, because the university decides that, and they score everybody.

And if an agent creates your application account and won't give you the login, that's a red flag. That's your application. You should be able to open it yourself.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the fair segment is next, which is the reason to stay to the end]**

---

**[SEGMENT 9: WHEN PAYING FOR STUDY ABROAD HELP IS OKAY]**

Now, I'm not saying every study abroad agent is a scammer, because that's not true.

Some universities work with agents officially, and the university pays them.

Loughborough University in the UK, for example, says its agents "receive a commission-based payment from Loughborough University for every student they have supported."

The University of Bedfordshire says "agents are paid by the university on a commission basis once you enrol."

In the UK, there's a sector framework for agents called the Agent Quality Framework, and from April 2026, the Home Office's guidance says universities that use agents have to commit to its main principles.

But even the UK's Migration Advisory Committee, in its 2024 review of the Graduate route, said "agents simply do not have the same incentives as universities," and that relying on agents "may lead to individuals paying for unnecessary add-ons."

So if you decide to use someone, this is what I would do.

Ask the university directly if they work with that agent. A lot of universities list their approved agents on their website.

Ask exactly what you're paying for, and who else is paying them.

And keep your own logins, your own email, and your own documents.

If you have the time to read a page and write an email, I believe you can do this yourself.

Have you ever paid for study abroad consultation? Tell me in the comments what you paid for, and if it was worth it.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Please Stop Paying for Study Abroad Consultation

### Subtitle:
> Where the free, official information is, how to apply for a master's abroad by yourself, and how to tell a legitimate agent from a scam.

### SEO Title:
> How to Apply for a Master's Abroad Without an Agent

### SEO Description:
> A Nigerian master's student in Estonia on applying abroad by yourself, the official free sources, emailing a programme manager, and the agent red flags governments have warned about.

---

I noticed something about Nigerian students. Whenever I make a video about Nigerians studying abroad, a lot of people in the comments ask if they can pay me for consultation. I always want to ask why we are so obsessed with paying people for consultation, because you really don't have to. The school has a website, and if you're confused about anything, you can write them an email. I applied for my own master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Tallinn University by myself, with a chemistry degree and no computer science bachelor's, and I also got into a computer science master's the same way.

A lot of what consultants do is what you can do for yourself. They go to the same university website you can go to, read the same requirements page, and fill the same application form. The first place to look is always the page for the exact programme you want, then the page for applicants from your country if the university has one, then the English test page. Countries also run official sites for international students. Study in Estonia tells applicants to contact the admission office at the university directly with questions about a programme, and it says application fees at Estonian universities range from 0 to 120 euros and are non-refundable. Study in Germany points you to the DAAD database and the Higher Education Compass, which lists more than 20,000 degree programmes. Study in NL is run by Nuffic and has a database of international programmes. Campus France has offices in Abuja and Lagos, and EducationUSA's Lagos centre says it runs free counselling sessions every Wednesday. The European Union's Erasmus Mundus catalogue doesn't take applications at all and sends you to each programme's own website.

The person I'd write to when I'm confused is the programme manager, who runs the programme and whose email is usually on the programme page. A good email is short. Put the programme name and the intake in the subject line, say who you are in a line or two, ask one or two clear questions, and thank them. Read the page first, so you aren't asking them something already written there. My own programme's application page says that if you apply as soon as possible, the admission specialists can give you feedback before the deadline and a chance to update your application, and that feedback comes from the people who actually decide.

I also don't think you need anybody to write your motivation letter. My advice is to sell your work and your experience well, and in the letter, sell your experience and your passion. If you like the programme enough, you won't find it hard to write. Mine was so long that I had to keep cutting it. A consultant doesn't know what you've done or why you want this, and in the UK Migration Advisory Committee's 2024 review of the Graduate route, one graduate said about their own personal statement, "I think the agency wrote it for me."

Someone recently told me they already had an interview and wanted to pay me for interview consultation. If a university has invited you for an interview, they already think you're worth interviewing. You've impressed them to that point, and you just have to keep doing whatever got you there. Of course you can check and ask people. Before my own interview, I did mock interviews with people who were already in the programme, but I didn't depend on them as if I needed them to be selected.

There is also a darker side. In 2023, Canada set up a task force after hundreds of international students faced removal over fake letters of acceptance in their study permit applications, and Canada's immigration department said those cases involved unauthorised consultants who gave clients fraudulent letters. The department also wrote that "the onus remains on applicants to provide truthful information on their applications even if a third party is preparing it." In October 2022, Nigeria's Ministry of Foreign Affairs warned about "unscrupulous elements parading themselves as agents" selling admissions and jobs in Northern Cyprus. In November 2025, the UK, Canada and Australia launched a joint campaign against visa fraud in Nigeria and named the warning signs, which were demands for excessive fees, pressure to pay quickly, promises of jobs without proper qualifications, and offers to bypass standard requirements, and they said nobody can guarantee a visa. I'd add anyone who guarantees admission, and any agent who creates your application account and keeps the login.

To be fair, not every agent is a scammer. Some universities work with agents officially and pay them. Loughborough University says its agents "receive a commission-based payment from Loughborough University for every student they have supported," and the University of Bedfordshire says "agents are paid by the university on a commission basis once you enrol." UK universities have a sector Agent Quality Framework, and Home Office guidance from April 2026 says sponsors that use agents must commit to its main principles. Still, the Migration Advisory Committee wrote in 2024 that "agents simply do not have the same incentives as universities." If you decide to use one, ask the university directly whether it works with that agent, ask what you're paying for and who else is paying them, and keep your own logins, email and documents. If you have the time to read a page and write an email, I believe you can do this yourself. Have you ever paid for study abroad consultation, and was it worth it?
