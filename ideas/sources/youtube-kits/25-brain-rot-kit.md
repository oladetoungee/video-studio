My brain is rotting.

**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT BRAIN ROT MEANS]**

"Brain rot" was Oxford's Word of the Year for 2024.

**[ON SCREEN: brain rot (n.) "the supposed deterioration of a person's mental or intellectual state, especially viewed as the result of overconsumption of material (now particularly online content) considered to be trivial or unchallenging." Oxford, 2024]**

Their definition is the supposed deterioration of a person's mental or intellectual state, from consuming too much content that's trivial or unchallenging.

Oxford says "supposed," so even the dictionary isn't claiming your brain is actually decaying.

**[~1:00 BEAT: brain rot is 170 years old]**

And the phrase is much older than TikTok. Oxford traced its first recorded use to 1854, in Henry David Thoreau's book Walden.

**[ON SCREEN: "While England endeavours to cure the potato rot, will not any endeavour to cure the brain-rot, which prevails so much more widely and fatally?" Henry David Thoreau, Walden, 1854]**

He asked why England was trying to cure the potato rot, when nobody was trying to cure the brain rot. So people have been worried about this for about 170 years.

For me, brain rot is a whole cluster of things happening.

Shortened attention span. Compulsive scrolling, at different levels for different people. Difficulty with deep reading and thinking. Mental fogginess. And a diet of low quality content.

I mean, saying the brain is rotten feels like saying it's damaged. So I wouldn't go that far.

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT BRAIN ROT LOOKS LIKE FOR ME]**

When I talk about brain rot, I'm talking about myself too.

Personally, I don't scroll as much. I don't even think I use social media as much as the average person.

But I still think that from time to time I would indulge myself in compulsive scrolling, for like minutes.

And I don't think I read as much anymore. People meet me and they're like, oh yeah, you read, you always have a book.

But if you knew me, I used to read so much. Not light stuff, I'd read serious stuff.

I remember when I was in school, I would read for hours.

I'd stay up overnight, and I would do this every day, back to back, and I genuinely enjoyed it.

I mean, I still deep read now. I'm doing a master's programme, so I don't have a choice but to read.

But I'm having difficulty deep reading at the level that I want to deep read. And I struggle with mental fog.

Maybe I should even turn these videos to myself now.

It's like my attention is being turned into some habits that I don't like. And I've been waging war against this.

I've been so aggressive against this. And I don't even think I've been aggressive enough.

**[SEGMENT 4: WHAT HAPPENED TO MY ATTENTION SPAN]**

Where I really feel brain rot is my attention span.

I used to live on Medium, reading very long articles.

If you don't know Medium, it's a site where people publish long articles and essays.

And LinkedIn attracted me five or six years ago, because people wrote long posts there, like Substack posts.

And people actually read them and engaged with them.

Now, even me, I lose interest quickly when an article is long.

I know my attention span is getting messed up.

So everything I'm doing in this video is also how I'm trying to fix my attention span.

**[SEGMENT 5: CAN BRAIN ROT BE REVERSED?]**

Before I tell you how I'm reversing brain rot, I want to answer the obvious question. Is it real, and can it be reversed?

When Oxford announced the word, Andrew Przybylski, a psychologist at the University of Oxford, told the BBC that "there's no evidence of brain rot actually being a thing."

But that doesn't mean nothing is happening.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the biggest review so far]**

In 2025, a big review in Psychological Bulletin combined 71 studies, with almost 100,000 people altogether.

**[ON SCREEN: 71 studies, 98,299 people. Nguyen et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2025]**

They found that people who watched more short videos, like TikTok, Reels and YouTube Shorts, tended to have poorer attention and poorer inhibitory control.

Inhibitory control is your ability to stop yourself from doing something on impulse. Like not picking up your phone when you said you wouldn't.

But most of those studies measured people once, at one point in time.

So they can show that two things go together. They can't show which one caused the other.

Think about umbrellas. People carrying umbrellas get wet more often than people who aren't carrying one.

Umbrellas don't make you wet. Rainy days cause both.

It could be that short videos weaken your attention. It could also be that people who already struggle to focus reach for short videos more. Or both. The researchers said this themselves.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: "Stay with me for this next study, because it actually tested cause and effect."]**

So the question I care about is whether things get better when you change what you do. And there's one experiment that tested exactly that.

In 2025, researchers in Canada and the US recruited 467 people, and used an app to block all internet on their phones for two weeks.

Calls and texts still worked. Their laptops still had internet. It was only the phone.

**[ON SCREEN: Two weeks, no mobile internet. Castelo et al., PNAS Nexus, 2025]**

After two weeks without phone internet, people did better on a computer attention test, the kind where you have to stay focused for a long stretch.

The researchers compared the size of that improvement to about ten years of age-related decline in attention. People also reported better mental health and wellbeing.

And the numbers are honest. Only about one in four people kept the block on for at least ten of the fourteen days.

But the improvement still showed up when the researchers counted everyone who signed up, including the people who didn't stick to it.

So that's why I say reversing. In that experiment, attention got better in two weeks of using the phone less.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: USING MY PHONE LESS]**

The first thing I'm doing to reverse brain rot is using my phone less, on purpose.

**[~6:00 BEAT: six years off Instagram]**

When I was nineteen, I used to use Instagram a lot. And I strongly felt that it was very dysfunctional for the life I wanted to live.

So I left Instagram. I didn't use the app for about six years.

I enjoyed Instagram, by the way. At the time, I couldn't even put into words why I was leaving.

These days, my rule for social media is need.

I should only open Twitter, Instagram or TikTok when I need something from there, and my need is usually to post my content. And I break it too sometimes.

I want to be able to open social media, get what I wanted to get out of it, spend five minutes or whatever, and actively own the process of closing it.

And for some months now, I have notifications, even WhatsApp notifications, which is the one I would normally keep on, turned off on my phone.

I do want social interaction. But a lot of the interaction we have isn't even deep.

It's just us responding, commenting on a status. Everything has become so surface level, even with human interaction.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: DOING ONE THING AT A TIME]**

Another way I'm reversing brain rot is by forcing myself not to multitask.

Because I feel like constant switching fragments my working memory. It's such a problem that I have.

I'm not a brain psychologist or whatever, but I understand that working memory is the mental workspace where thinking actually happens.

Think of it like a kitchen counter. It's where the cooking actually happens, and there's only so much space on it.

So every time I switch, I'm having to clear the counter and bring everything back out. Drop it, and reload it. Every time.

**[~9:00 BEAT: her thesis connection]**

By the way, task switching is something I'm so passionate about as a topic. My master's thesis is actually about switching between tools, and I made a whole video on task switching, so I'll link it.

So I feel like, just going back and forth, I'm not reaching this deep continuous state where the level of intellectual work that I want happens.

I'm doing work, of course. But there's a kind of work, actual deep intentional work, that I want to do that I'm not reaching.

I don't know how to put this into words, man.

So now, sit with one thing, finish that thing, no exit. Even at work.

I'm retraining long attention, forcing my brain to relearn that, and to resist the urge to switch.

You see that thing where you're doing something and something else drops into your mind?

Or whatever you're doing is getting to a point where it's getting harder, and you feel like you need a break, and that always makes you switch to something else?

And then you come back, and it's just another thing.

**[SEGMENT 8: LETTING MYSELF BE BORED]**

I'm also reversing brain rot by letting myself be bored again.

I also don't like to get bored anymore. I used to be okay getting bored, and this is something I'm actively trying to bring back.

Because I feel like now we have to fill every gap. You're waiting in a queue. You're commuting on the bus. There's a quiet moment.

It feels like you have to fill it with something. You have to bring out your phone, scroll, do this, do that.

There's this constant ingesting, and we never really get to the point of actually digesting.

And people found it hard to sit with their own thoughts even before short videos.

In 2014, psychologists at the University of Virginia and Harvard asked people to sit alone in a room for fifteen minutes, with no phone and nothing to read. Just their thoughts.

There was a button in the room that gave a mild electric shock. Earlier, these same people had said they would pay money to avoid that shock.

67% of the men and 25% of the women pressed it at least once.

**[ON SCREEN: 15 minutes alone with their thoughts. 67% of men and 25% of women pressed the shock button at least once. Wilson et al., Science, 2014]**

Some of them would rather do something unpleasant than nothing at all. And this was 2014, before TikTok.

**[~11:00 BEAT: the bike]**

So I'm refilling boredom. Deliberately leaving gaps unfilled.

This year I learned how to ride a bike, and I really love it when I take it out to ride.

I try to ride at least every day, or once in two days. No headphones, no music, just riding somewhere.

And in many cases, there's nothing I'm going there to do. We're just doing one freaking boring thing.

I love music, but sometimes, no music, man.

Sometimes I stop and film places I would normally just pass by without noticing.

**[SEGMENT 9: LEARNING TO READ DEEPLY AGAIN]**

And to reverse brain rot, I'm getting my deep reading back.

If you used to read for hours and now you can't get through a chapter, you're not the only one.

Maryanne Wolf is a scientist who studies how the brain learns to read. In 2018, she wrote a book called Reader, Come Home.

In it, she describes trying to reread a novel she had loved when she was young, Magister Ludi by Hermann Hesse.

And she couldn't get into it. She wrote, "I hated the book."

She felt she had lost what she called the "cognitive patience" to read books like that.

So she kept at it, a bit every day, until she could sink into it again.

That sounds a lot like what I'm going through. So this is what I'm doing.

When I'm waiting on something, maybe I'm working and I'm waiting on a response, or I'm waiting on Claude Code, I know there'll be gaps.

Sometimes I tend to just pick up my phone, and I'm forcing myself to pick up a book instead.

Sometimes you do the mental maths of that activity in your head and you're like, oh, I'm too lazy to do this much, so you just pick up your phone, because that is easy.

But I'm picking up more low friction things. Writing. Reading. I started handwriting scriptures these days and I really love it.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: HOW TO FIX YOUR ATTENTION SPAN]**

The reason I keep at all of this is that I understand my attention is trainable.

I believe what you practise, you get better at. And I know that if I keep distracting myself, I cannot do deep work.

I'm training myself. And I hate it every time that I do it.

I know that I am freaking training myself, with this whole context switching, going back and forth.

I know that I'm training myself to become something I don't want to.

And I believe all of this is reversible. That's the part I'm working on currently.

Just altogether working on my attention, my impulse control, my mental consolidation. Just everything.

Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown who writes about focus, has a name for this kind of work.

In a New York Times essay this year, he argued for what he calls "cognitive fitness."

His comparison is physical fitness. If your body was out of shape, you'd start a routine and build up slowly. He says we can do the same for our minds.

**[ON SCREEN: "cognitive fitness." Cal Newport, The New York Times, March 2026]**

And Daniel Pink, in his video on how to fix your attention span, starts with a simple step.

Get a book and time yourself. How long can you read before you get up or check your phone?

Write that time down. That's your baseline. Then you stretch it a little at a time, like a muscle.

**[ON SCREEN: time how long you can read without getting up or checking your phone. That's your baseline. Daniel Pink, "How To Fix Your Attention Span"]**

If you feel like brain rot is happening to you too, don't panic about your brain. There's no evidence it's rotting.

Pick one thing you can actually keep, and keep it. For me, it started with one app at nineteen.

I'm sounding like an influencer. But yeah, what are we all doing about brain rot?

Tell me in the comments one thing you've really tried, even if it only worked a little. I want to hear it.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How I'm Reversing My Brain Rot

### Subtitle:
> I used to read for hours. I don't think my brain is rotting, but my attention span has changed, and I'm working on getting it back.

### SEO Title:
> How to Reverse Brain Rot and Fix Your Attention Span

### SEO Description:
> What "brain rot" means, whether it can be reversed, the experiment that blocked phone internet for two weeks, the science of boredom, and what I'm doing to fix my attention span.

---

My brain is rotting. Okay, I'm being a little bit dramatic. I don't think my brain is rotting per se. But I used to read for hours when I was in school. I would stay up overnight, every day, back to back, and I genuinely enjoyed it. I still read now, because I'm doing a master's and I don't have a choice, but reading deeply at the level I want to takes much more effort than it used to. I used to live on Medium, reading very long articles, and five or six years ago LinkedIn attracted me because people wrote long posts there and other people actually read them and engaged with them. Now even I lose interest quickly when an article is long. I know my attention span is getting messed up, so I'm trying to reverse it.

"Brain rot" was Oxford's Word of the Year for 2024. Oxford University Press defines it as "the supposed deterioration of a person's mental or intellectual state, especially viewed as the result of overconsumption of material (now particularly online content) considered to be trivial or unchallenging." Even the dictionary says "supposed." The phrase is also much older than any app. Oxford traces its first recorded use to Henry David Thoreau's Walden in 1854, where he asked why England was trying to cure the potato rot while nobody tried to cure the brain-rot, "which prevails so much more widely and fatally."

Before trying to reverse anything, I wanted to know whether it can be reversed. When the word was announced, Andrew Przybylski, a psychologist at the University of Oxford, told the BBC that "there's no evidence of brain rot actually being a thing." A 2025 review in Psychological Bulletin, combining 71 studies with almost 100,000 people, did find that heavier short-video use went together with poorer attention and poorer inhibitory control. Most of those studies measured people once, though. People carrying umbrellas get wet more often, and umbrellas don't make anyone wet. In the same way, short videos might weaken attention, or people who already struggle to focus might watch more of them. The study that answered my question is an experiment published in PNAS Nexus in 2025. Noah Castelo and his colleagues blocked all internet on 467 people's phones for two weeks, while calls, texts and laptops still worked. People did better on a sustained attention test afterwards, and the researchers compared the size of that improvement to about ten years of age-related decline. Only about one in four kept the block on for at least ten of the fourteen days, and the improvement still showed up when everyone was counted.

I started by using my phone less, and that began long before this video. When I was nineteen, I felt Instagram was very dysfunctional for the life I wanted to live, so I left it, and I didn't use it for about six years. I enjoyed it, and at the time I couldn't even put into words why I was leaving. These days my rule for social media is need. I open an app when I need something there, which is usually to post my content, and I break the rule sometimes. My notifications have been off for months, even WhatsApp.

I'm also doing one thing at a time. I feel like constant switching fragments my working memory, the mental workspace where thinking happens, and every switch is like clearing a kitchen counter and bringing everything back out again. My master's thesis is about switching between tools, so this one is close to home. Now I sit with one thing, finish it, and try not to leave when it gets hard.

And I'm letting myself be bored. Every gap gets filled now, in the queue, on the bus, in a quiet moment, and there's this constant ingesting where we never really get to digesting. In 2014, Timothy Wilson and his colleagues asked people to sit alone with their thoughts for fifteen minutes, with a button that gave a mild electric shock they had already said they'd pay to avoid. 67% of the men and 25% of the women pressed it at least once. So I ride my bike with no headphones and no music, and sometimes I stop to film places I would normally pass without noticing.

Then there's reading. Maryanne Wolf, a scientist who studies how the brain learns to read, describes in Reader, Come Home trying to reread Hermann Hesse's Magister Ludi, a novel she had loved when she was young. She couldn't get into it. "I hated the book," she wrote. She felt she had lost what she called the "cognitive patience" for it, so she kept at it a bit every day until she could sink in again. When I'm waiting on something now, I pick up a book instead of my phone, and I've started handwriting scriptures.

I believe attention is trainable, which means every time I distract myself, I'm training myself to become something I don't want to be. I also believe it's reversible, and that's the part I'm working on. Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown, argued in a New York Times essay this year for what he calls "cognitive fitness," training the mind the way you'd get your body back in shape, slowly and with a routine. Daniel Pink's first step for fixing your attention span is a simple one. Time how long you can read a book before you get up or check your phone, write it down as your baseline, and stretch it a little at a time. I'm sounding like an influencer. But what are we all doing about brain rot? Tell me one thing you've really tried, even if it only worked a little.

- **Castelo, N., et al. (2025). "Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being."
