**[CHAPTER 1 · 0:00 · Can you do a master's in a different field?]** (Setup 2 for this part, then Setup 1)

[NEW] Can you do a master's in a different field from your bachelor's degree?

I do not have a computer science degree.

I have a bachelor's degree in chemistry, and right now I have a career as a full stack developer, and I'm also currently doing my master's in human-computer interaction.

[NEW] And I also got into a computer science master's without a computer science bachelor's, and I'll tell you how.

If you think that your degree, your bachelor's degree, has locked you out of switching fields when you want to transition, then this video is for you.

[NEW, only if posting before 1 March 2027] Applications for my own programme for 2027 opened on the 1st of November, so if you're applying this year, this is the time to read this properly.

I'll show you how switching careers works, through master's, through jobs, or both, and I'll use my own path as the example all the way through. [NEW] And at the end, the four questions your motivation letter has to answer when you're switching.

---

**[CHAPTER 2 · Read the admission requirements properly]** (TV: the three groups, then the Copenhagen page, then the TLU page)

If you want to do a master's in a different field from your bachelor's, the admission requirements page decides a lot of things, so you have to read it properly.

From what I have seen, programmes usually fall into three groups.

There are some programmes that take any bachelor's degree, like the conversion courses in the UK.

There are some that ask for a degree or experience in the field.

And there are some that ask for specific courses on your transcript.

**[ON SCREEN / BLACK CARDS: 1. any bachelor's degree / 2. a degree OR experience in the field / 3. specific courses on your transcript]**

The third group is where a lot of people get caught.

Let's take the computer science master's degree at the University of Copenhagen as an example.

If your bachelor's is from another university, their page asks for at least 60 ECTS of computer science courses, including programming, computer systems architecture, and theoretical computer science.

It also asks for at least 7.5 ECTS of maths.

If you don't know what ECTS means, they're the study credits used in Europe. A full-time year of study is usually worth 60 of them.

So when they say a related field can apply, it means a related field that has a year's worth of computer science already on your transcript.

**[ON SCREEN: the Copenhagen page on the TV, "60 ECTS" and "7.5 ECTS" in yellow marker]**

That's a strict door for someone like me. (say twice)

But on the same page, they say they can also admit people whose educational qualifications [EDIT: "educational" added, see 7e] are judged equivalent after an individual assessment.

And if your desired field is very far away from what you did your bachelor's degree in, then you probably fall into the second group, which is my case.

The page for my programme at Tallinn University asks for "background and/or previous experience in computer science, design, behavioral or social sciences."

**[ON SCREEN: the TLU HCI page, that line highlighted]**

So basically, when you see a programme you are interested in, try to put it into one of these three groups.

If it falls into group one or group two, that is perfect.

Even if it falls into group three, the one that asks for specific courses, sometimes there's still a door to get in.

---

**[CHAPTER 3 · Build proof and sell your experience]**

If you're switching fields, the thing that really carries your application is proof that you can already do some of the work, especially if you are doing a master's degree.

My bachelor's degree is in chemistry, but I learned how to code, and I started working as a software developer in 2022.

By the time I was applying for my master's in 2025, I'd worked as a software developer and as a technical operations lead, and that work was my proof.

If you don't have work experience in the new field you're trying to move into, your proof can start smaller.

It can be a project that you build, finish and put online.

It can be a course where you did the assignments.

It can be volunteering, or running a community in that field.

But one thing I will always tell you is that you don't start building that proof at the point when you want to start applying.

Two months to the application, and you're just starting to volunteer? No.

You start building it long before the deadline, because you cannot build experience in the months before you apply.

If you have experience in the new field but your degree is in something else, how you describe that experience decides a lot.

If you have work experience like I do, you just have to sell your work and your technical experience well enough.

When I applied for my master's, I applied as someone with a technical background, even though my bachelor's degree certificate says chemistry.

And interestingly, with this same method, I also got into a computer science master's degree without a computer science bachelor's degree. (pays off open loop 1)

I intentionally declined that one, and I chose HCI instead. [HER LINE, optional: one sentence on why, in her words; see Questions]

And this works for job applications too, so stay with me if you are not applying for a master's.

Selling your experience starts with being specific.

Name your role, the tools you used, what you built, and what you fixed.

If you say things like "worked on the back end," that is too vague. Tell them what you did.

Then connect your experience to where you are going.

If you're a software developer and maybe you want to do an HCI master's degree, you build apps and you watch users struggle. That's a real link to a field like HCI.

Whatever experience you're trying to sell, you're selling it to the people making the decision in the new field.

So what I'll tell you is to write it the way the people in that field will read it. Use their words and their tools to communicate your experience.

---

**[CHAPTER 4 · Your old degree comes with you]**

[NEW] And remember those four questions for your motivation letter, they're coming right after this.

When you switch career fields, whatever you did in your bachelor's degree doesn't disappear. It comes with you.

When I was doing my bachelor's degree, I did a reasonable amount of research. I published two papers during my BSc.

So I had already gone through that process once, and that has made me more confident about writing my master's thesis.

My degree is in chemistry, and it trained me to be more analytical. I picked up one or two things from it that are still useful to me now.

Whatever your bachelor's degree is in, even if it's different from the master's you want to do, it has still trained you to do something.

If you have an accounting degree, it has trained you to be more exact with numbers than the average person.

Law trains you to read closely and argue a case.

Biology trains you to run experiments.

Now, I can't use my chemistry degree directly to talk my way into a master's in tech.

But I can use the skills I learned from it, and say clearly how they help me in the new field.

So make a list of what your degree has trained you to do, and use those words in your applications as well.

Your degree still opens doors for you. You just have to know how to communicate the skills it has given you.

---

**[CHAPTER 5 · The motivation letter and the interview]** (printed letter + pen; black cards 1-4)

If you're applying for a master's in a field that your bachelor's degree is not in, your motivation letter is where you explain your switch.

My advice is to sell your experience and your passion.

If you like the programme well enough, you won't find it hard to write.

And please be original. Write it yourself.

I feel like if you are making a career switch like that, your letter needs at least these four things.

Why this field? And why not the field you did your bachelor's degree in?

What have you already done in this field? Have you actually done something in it, or are you just fantasising about it?

Why this programme?

And what will you do with whatever you get from this programme?

**[ON SCREEN / BLACK CARDS: 1. why this field, and why not your old one / 2. what you've already done in it / 3. why this programme / 4. what you'll do with it]** (pays off open loop 3)

Before you write your motivation letter, read what the department is working on. Read some of their papers, or at least the titles and abstracts.

I remember when I was applying for my programme, they even asked you to talk about your research interests, and what your thesis could be about.

And they could ask why someone with a degree in another field wants to do a master's in their programme.

Answer plainly. You don't have to apologise for your first degree. You did that, so own it. (say twice)

Come with proof. Maybe you've started to build a passion for this new field, and you've started to get some experience in it.

But don't throw away your first degree, and don't talk down on it either.

This video isn't really about how to write motivation letters, but I would say, write your first draft and make it as long as possible.

Write everything that comes to your mind, and then you can start cutting it down to the main, important things.

If your master's application has an interview like mine, and your bachelor's degree was in another field, expect them to ask why you're switching.

For example, my bachelor's degree is in chemistry, and I'm moving into human-computer interaction.

### 6f. Essay in full (her kit's essay; one accuracy word added)

#### Title:
> How to Switch Careers When Your Degree Is Unrelated

#### Subtitle:
> I don't have a computer science degree. My bachelor's is in chemistry, I work as a full stack developer, and I'm doing a master's in human-computer interaction. This is what I'd tell anyone switching fields.

#### SEO Title:
> Career Change With an Unrelated Degree: Master's, Jobs and Proof

#### SEO Description:
> A chemistry graduate turned full stack developer and HCI master's student on switching fields: reading admission requirements, building proof, selling experience, your old degree, the motivation letter and the interview.

---

I do not have a computer science degree. I have a bachelor's degree in chemistry, I have a career as a full stack developer, and I'm currently doing my master's in human-computer interaction. If you think your bachelor's degree has locked you out of switching fields, I want to show you how switching works, through a master's, through a job, or both, using my own path as the example.

If you want to do a master's in a different field from your bachelor's, the admission requirements page decides a lot, so you have to read it properly. From what I have seen, programmes usually fall into three groups. Some take any bachelor's degree, like the conversion courses in the UK. Some ask for a degree or experience in the field. And some ask for specific courses on your transcript, which is where a lot of people get caught. The University of Copenhagen's computer science master's, for example, asks applicants from other universities for at least 60 ECTS of computer science courses, including programming, computer systems architecture and theoretical computer science, plus at least 7.5 ECTS of maths. A full-time year of study is usually worth 60 ECTS, so a "related field" there means a year's worth of computer science already on your transcript. That's a strict door for someone like me. But the same page says they can also admit people whose educational qualifications are judged equivalent after an individual assessment.

My own programme at Tallinn University is in the second group. It asks for "background and/or previous experience in computer science, design, behavioral or social sciences." So when you find a programme you're interested in, try to put it into one of these three groups. If it falls into group one or group two, that is perfect. Even in group three, there's sometimes still a door to get in.

What really carries a switch is proof that you can already do some of the work. My bachelor's degree is in chemistry, but I learned how to code and started working as a software developer in 2022. By the time I was applying for my master's in 2025, I had worked as a software developer and as a technical operations lead, and that work was my proof. If you don't have work experience in the new field yet, your proof can start smaller. It can be a project you build, finish and put online, a course where you did the assignments, or volunteering and running a community in that field. Just don't start when you start applying. If you're two months from the application and only now starting to volunteer, it's too late, because you cannot build experience in the months before you apply.

Then you have to sell it. If you have work experience like I do, you just have to sell your work and your technical experience well enough. When I applied for my master's, I applied as someone with a technical background, even though my certificate says chemistry, and with the same approach I also got into a computer science master's without a computer science bachelor's. I declined that one and chose HCI. This works for job applications too. Be specific about your role, the tools you used, what you built and what you fixed, because "worked on the back end" is too vague. Then connect it to where you're going. If you're a developer who wants an HCI master's, you build apps and watch users struggle, and that is a real link. Write it the way people in the new field will read it, with their words and their tools.

Your old degree doesn't disappear when you switch. It comes with you. During my bachelor's I did a reasonable amount of research and published two papers, so I had already been through that process once, and that has made me more confident about writing my master's thesis. Chemistry also trained me to be more analytical. Whatever your degree is in, it has trained you to do something. Accounting trains you to be more exact with numbers than the average person, law trains you to read closely and argue a case, and biology trains you to run experiments. I can't use my chemistry degree directly to talk my way into a master's in tech, but I can use the skills I learned from it and say clearly how they help me in the new field. So make a list of what your degree has trained you to do, and use those words in your applications. Your degree still opens doors for you. You just have to know how to communicate what it has given you.

Your motivation letter is where you explain the switch. My advice is to sell your experience and your passion. If you like the programme well enough, you won't find it hard to write, and please be original and write it yourself. I think a letter for a switch needs at least four things: why this field and not the one you did your bachelor's in, what you've already done in this field (or whether you're just fantasising about it), why this programme, and what you'll do with whatever you get from it. Read what the department is working on first, even if it's only the titles and abstracts of their papers. When I applied, my programme asked about my research interests and what my thesis could be about. Answer the question of why someone with your degree wants this plainly. You don't have to apologise for your first degree. You did that, so own it, and don't talk down on it either. Write your first draft as long as possible, with everything that comes to mind, and then cut it down to the main, important things.

If your application has an interview, like mine did, expect them to ask why you're switching. My bachelor's is in chemistry and I'm moving into human-computer interaction, so I should expect that question. Practise telling your story: what you studied before, what you did after, how you found your way into this field, and why you're committing to it now. Have the answer ready before anybody asks, because they probably will. And if you want a master's in a field that's different from your bachelor's, give that programme a shot. Your degree might not be the disadvantage you think it is. Don't let the title on your certificate decide for you before they've even turned you down. Bachelor's in accounting, bachelor's in law, master's in whatever you want. Go for it.
