## method
The method is pretty much straightforward. The idea is that before you read a book, you read the structure of the book first.
So you get a new book, you go to the table of contents, and you actually study the table of contents. The chapters. The sub-headings. You look at how the whole thing is laid out before you even read a single page of the actual content.
It is after you have done this that you then actually read the book.
That's the whole thing. It sounds so simple.

## lineage
I've seen this idea on Pinterest and YouTube, I actually did walk through it on YouTube I think. But the oldest version of this idea is from a book called How to Read a Book by Mortimer Adler, from 1940.
And I want to actually read this book full blown, not just tips and summaries from it.

## inspect
He calls it inspectional reading. Before chapter one, you study the contents page, the chapter titles, the sub-headings, and you skim a little to see how the author has laid out their argument.

## priming
The newer version of it is kind of popular. If you go on Justin Sung's YouTube page, I think he has a video where he calls it priming. Same idea, you spend a few minutes on the headings, ask yourself how the parts connect, and the real reading comes after.

## memorise
But I like the TikTok version, where people say you should actually memorise it. That is what I'm doing. The memorise one actually sticks more for me.
Memorise the chapter titles and the sub-chapters, and after you're done memorising, then you read.
So for me, you can skim through it, you can read through it, but memorising just makes the mapping stronger in my mind.

## why
So why does this method work?
At least as far as I understand it, it's about having somewhere to put things.

## float
When you start reading a book cold, every new idea that arrives, your brain has nowhere to file it. So it sort of floats. And then the next idea arrives and that one floats too.
And by the time you're in chapter six of the book, you're already holding a pile of floating things that are not attached to anything.

## shelves
But when you have studied the structure first, you already know the shape of the argument. So when an idea shows up in the book, you know which part of the book it belongs to and what it is doing there.
In your mind, that idea has a place to go.
That's the whole mechanism. You are building the shelves before you start putting things on them.
