**[OPEN]**

So this is my current reading stack.

So today I want to talk about a reading method that I think will work for you if you want to remember everything that you have read. And I'm going to try it practically in this video.

---

**[THE PROBLEM]**

So the problem is that you read a whole book, you sit with it for some days, some weeks, and then someone asks you what it was about, and you can tell them the general vibe of what the book is about, maybe one or two things that stuck.

But it's almost as though what you're left with is a feeling about the book rather than the book itself.

So this method is going to fix that.

---

**[THE METHOD]**

The method is pretty much straightforward. The idea is that before you read a book, you read the structure of the book first.

So you get a new book, you go to the table of contents, and you actually study the table of contents. The chapters. The sub-headings under the chapters. You look at how the whole thing is laid out before you even read a single page of the actual content.

It is after you have done this that you then actually read the book.

That's the whole thing. It sounds so simple.

I've seen this idea on Pinterest and YouTube, I actually did walk through it on YouTube I think. But the oldest version of this idea is from a book called How to Read a Book by Mortimer Adler, from 1940. And I want to actually read this book full blown, not just tips and summaries from it.

He calls it inspectional reading. Before chapter one, you study the contents page, the chapter titles, the sub-headings, and you skim a little to see how the author has laid out their argument.

The newer version of it is kind of popular. If you go on Justin Sung's YouTube page, I think he has a video where he calls it priming. Same idea, you spend a few minutes on the headings, ask yourself how the parts connect, and the real reading comes after.

But I like the TikTok version, where people say you should actually memorise it. That is what I'm doing. The memorise one actually sticks more for me. Memorise the chapter titles and the sub-chapters, and after you're done memorising, then you read.

So for me, you can skim through it, you can read through it, but memorising just makes the mapping stronger in my mind.

---

**[WHY IT WORKS]**

So why does this method work?

At least as far as I understand it, it's about having somewhere to put things.

When you start reading a book cold, every new idea that arrives, your brain has nowhere to file it. So it sort of floats. And then the next idea arrives and that one floats too. And by the time you're in chapter six of the book, you're already holding a pile of floating things that are not attached to anything.

But when you have studied the structure first, you already know the shape of the argument. So when an idea shows up in the book, you know which part of the book it belongs to and what it is doing there. In your mind, that idea has a place to go.

That's the whole mechanism. You are building the shelves before you start putting things on them.

So first of all, the title is telling you that two things already belong together. Reading critically and writing critically are being treated together.

Chapter ten, how to use critical language in your assessments.

**[GOING THROUGH IT]**

So in my head I'm already thinking, because I throw around critical thinking, I throw it around a lot, in my head I'm already thinking I am going to be challenged by the definition this person is going to give of what it means to be critical. Chapter two, different ways to show your criticality. In my head I already know this is going to be practical. I'm going to get tips on how to show my criticality.

Chapter three goes on to score bands and assessment. That already tells me who this book is for and what it wants. So this is not just a book about critical thinking as a life skill. Again, I am assuming by the chapter, it is a book about getting marked well, maybe in academia. Which is useful for me to know, because now I would not be expecting anything philosophical. Now I'm expecting practical academic strategy.

And then chapter four, I am also looking forward to that chapter. The connection between critical reading and thinking and writing. How everything comes together.

Is it possible, in my head I'm also already forming questions, is it possible to think critically but not write critically? Is it possible to do one or the other, or are they bundled together? Like once you think critically, you will read critically, you will write critically. I don't know yet, but I'm anticipating. I already have that shelf in my head.

And then you look at chapter five, six and seven. Essay, dissertation, assessments. In my head, that's a block. That whole block is about structure. How do you organise? If I want to write an essay, how do I organise it? My dissertation, and all of that.

And if you look at the additional assessments part, there's also oral presentation, poster presentation and all of that. So chapter five, six and seven feels like a block to me.

And then chapter eight, nine and ten. Chapter eight I'm also looking forward to. Four ways to approach interpretation. I'm already thinking, is this about when someone says something, where should my mind go to first? How should I interpret what people say? Is it contextual, or anything like that?

Under chapter eight I see that there are some sub-chapters. Agreement. Disagreement. Illustration. Explanation.

Then chapter nine, how to use quotes, how to use paraphrase, and how to summarise. Under it we have plagiarism, direct quotation, paraphrasing, summarising.

And the last chapter is how to use critical language in your assessments. So in my head again I'm thinking, there is critical language. There are languages that are critical, and perhaps there are languages that are not critical. I don't know yet.

All of these are still my guess. Now when I read it, I'm reading to find out, am I right or am I wrong?

When I read this book, I'm able to retain more ideas now, because my mind is expecting things. My mind has a position now.

**[BOOK TWO]**

Right, book two. This one is bigger, it's for my thesis.

Convivial Toolbox, Generative Research for the Front End of Design, by Liz Sanders and Pieter Jan Stappers, published by BIS in Amsterdam.

Now the title first. The title has three major words or phrases in it. There's convivial. There's generative. I know what a toolbox is. Convivial, generative, and front end of design.

So let's take a look at the main word. Convivial. What does convivial mean?

The book is dedicated to a philosopher called Ivan Illich, and convivial tools is his idea. Convivial tools mean tools that give power back to the person using them, rather than tools that make the user dependent on an expert.

So before I read one single page out of this book, I already know the argument. This is my guess. The book is going to say that design research tools should also be able to be used by ordinary people, not just by researchers. That is the vibe this title is giving.

Of course, I already know, because this book is connected to my thesis. But even if I didn't, it would give me that vibe.

**[THE STRUCTURE]**

Now let's move to the structure of the book.

Part one is the underlying principles. It runs from page fourteen to page ninety-five.

Then we have part two, cases from practice.

And part three is how to actually use the tools and the techniques.

And also, keep the page numbers in mind. Part one is about eighty pages. Part two is about fifty pages. And part three is over a hundred and forty pages.

So more than half of this book is the how-to section. So even though it opens with theory, I already know that this is a very practical book. It is pretty much a manual that is pretending to be a textbook.

And that would change the way I read it. Because now I have a guess that the theory at the front is just there to make the manual and the practical part at the back make sense, not the other way around.

---

**[CHAPTER ONE]**

So now if I dive deep into chapter one. Chapter one is called Introduction, and there are sub-chapters.

Who is creative?

Good question. Thank you. I don't know.

I usually would tell people, oh yeah, you're so creative, I love it. I sometimes don't think of myself as being creative. I think of people as ridiculously creative. So it's good to know who is creative.

Why are people expressing a need for creativity now? Business and design are changing.

So look at that. The first real question in this entire book is, who is creative?

It's not even what is design research, or what is generative research. Who is creative?

That tells me that the whole argument rests on a claim about ordinary people. He is going to argue, and this is what I guess because I already know about this, he is going to argue that everybody is creative, and therefore everyone can participate in designing the things that affect them.

Which connects right back to the word from the title. Convivial. Makes sense.

So the title and the first sub-heading are already giving me something. A shelf, in my mind.

---

**[SKIPPING TO THE BACK]**

Now if I skip to the back of the book, I go to part three, chapter ten. Chapter ten is Bridging.

The questions he asks in that chapter. Where are the gaps? Where and when does bridging happen? Who is bridging the gaps? What are the ways we can bridge the gaps?

Now, I haven't read the book. I do not know what gaps he means yet. But now I know that there is a gap problem somewhere in this book. It matters enough that it gets its own chapter.

And when I meet the word gap in part one, I should pay attention to it, because I'm coming back to meet it in the later chapters.

If I had read it cold without doing this, I would probably have missed it. I would have seen the word gap in chapter two and probably just thought of it as, oh yeah, it's just there, it just means a normal gap.

---

**[WHAT I NOTICED]**

So I have been using this method to read, and I noticed that it takes less time than you think.

It sounds like something that will take you a lot of time and you don't want to do that extra work, you just want to get into reading. A few minutes per book, honestly. It's not something that will overwhelm you before you start reading the book. In fact it's something that will raise your anticipation for reading the book.

And this contents page is written by the author on purpose. It is a summary of their argument. Okay? Pay some attention to it.

And the third one, yes, it makes you want to read the book more. Because once you see the shape, you start having questions. You start having, is this author right? Is this author wrong? You anticipate reading it.

---

**[SO WHAT I DO NOW]**

So for non-fiction, this method is just something that I now do. Before reading the book, go over the contents, go over the sub-headings, the headings, the chapters. Take a guess about what the argument of the book is about, before you read.

For me, I prefer the TikTok version of memorising. When I force myself to memorise it, it sticks in a way that, of course you can just read it and not necessarily memorise it, I've done that before, but the memorising actually sticks more for me.

And for fiction, absolutely, I don't think you should be doing this. But I don't know, if you do this for fiction, please let me know.

I think that for fiction, the point is, I love the unknown. It's like a world of unknown for me. So half the point of me reading a novel is not knowing what is coming next.

And does fiction even have a table of contents? Oh, interesting. A fiction book actually has a table of contents. I've never noticed.

But really, there's no point. At least for me. I just want to know, I don't want to know what's up. I want the book to surprise me when it comes to fiction.

Basically, that's the method. Read the structure, read the headings, the chapters, before you start reading the book. Take a guess about what the argument of the book is about.

## 5. MAIN SHORT (her dictation, edited, not rewritten)

**[HOOK]**

If you read a whole book, and then weeks later somebody asks you what the book is about, and you cannot exactly give them concrete points, all you can do is give them the general vibe of it.

It's actually a general problem that a lot of people experience.

But I have a method that could solve this problem, that helps you retain a huge amount of what you read.

**[THE METHOD]**

So the idea is that before you read the book, you read the structure of the book.

Go to the contents page. Actually study the contents page. The chapters. The sub-headings. Only after you've done that should you start reading.

Me personally, I take it a step further. I try to memorise the chapter titles and the sub-headings, and then I start reading.

**[WHERE IT'S FROM]**

It's an old idea that keeps getting repackaged. Initially it was from Mortimer Adler, who called it inspectional reading in 1940. If you watch Justin Sung, I think there's a video of him calling it priming. And there's another version of it on TikTok where people say you should memorise the table of contents outright.

It's still the same method.

**[WHY IT WORKS]**

The reason it works is that it gives ideas somewhere to go.

When you read a book cold, every new idea just arrives. You're getting a lot of ideas from the book and your brain has nowhere to file it, so it floats. By the time you get to chapter six of the book, you're already holding a pile of floating things that is just everywhere.

But when you study the structure first, you already know the shape of the argument. And it builds anticipation, in a way.

So when the idea shows up, you know what part of the book it belongs to.

**[THE EXAMPLE]**

For example, in this book. The title says this is a book on how to read and write critically, and the first chapter is, what does it mean to be critical?

In my head, my mind is already anticipating something. I have an idea of what it means to be critical, but I'm anticipating that the author might disagree with me, or agree with me, or give me a broader, wider perspective on my existing idea.

My mind is anticipating something. My mind has built a shelf.

So when I actually start reading, I have somewhere to put that idea.

**[LAND]**

Okay, that's the idea. And it works. I have used it, and I promise you it works.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Read the Structure Before You Read the Book

### Subtitle:
> A method from 1940 that keeps getting rediscovered, tried on two books from my reading stack.

### SEO Title:
> How to Remember What You Read: The Table of Contents Method

### SEO Description:
> On inspectional reading, priming, and why studying a book's structure before reading it gives new ideas somewhere to land.

---

This is my current reading stack.

I took that phrase from a video I was watching, where someone said this is my current reading stack, and something about it sounded so good that I stole it. So now I say it too.

Right now it holds three things. How to Read and Write Critically by Alex Baratta. Convivial Toolbox, Generative Research for the Front End of Design, which I am reading for my thesis and which one of my co-supervisors lent me. And Miss Marple's Final Cases by Agatha Christie, which I am leaving out of this entirely, because fiction is a different thing.

**The problem**

Here is the problem this method is meant to solve, and I have it badly.

You read a whole book. You sit with it for weeks. Then somebody asks what it was about, and you can tell them roughly what it felt like, and maybe two ideas that stayed with you, and that is all. All those hours, and what you are left holding is a feeling about the book rather than the book.

**The method**

Before you read the book, you read the structure of the book.

You go to the contents page and study it properly. The chapters, then the sub-headings underneath them. You look at how the whole thing has been laid out before you read a single page of the actual content. And then you read.

That is the entire method, which is probably why people skip it.

I first saw it somewhere on Pinterest or YouTube and did not finish whatever it was, so I went looking afterwards, and it turns out to be an old idea that keeps getting repackaged. Mortimer Adler described it in How to Read a Book in 1940 and called it inspectional reading. A newer version circulates on YouTube as priming, where you spend a few minutes on the headings and ask how the parts connect. And there is a stricter version going round on TikTok where people memorise the chapters and sub-chapters before starting.

So when I could not remember whether the thing I saw said memorise or study, the answer is that both exist. Same method, different intensity.

**Why it works**

The reason, as far as I understand it, is about having somewhere to put things.

When you start a book cold, every new idea arrives and your brain has nowhere to file it, so it floats. Then the next one arrives and floats too, and by chapter six you are carrying a pile of unattached things.

Study the structure first and you already know the shape of the argument, so when an idea appears you know which part of the book it belongs to and what it is doing there. You are building the shelves before you start putting things on them.

**Trying it**

Doing it on Convivial Toolbox was more useful than I expected, because the book is built in three parts: principles, then cases, then the how-to.

Which means that before reading anything, I already know what the book intends to do to me. Explain the theory, show it working in real situations, then teach me to do it myself.

And that changes something small but real. If part one feels abstract and I catch myself waiting for it to get practical, that is not a failure of the book. Practical is not what part one is for. The contents page told me so in advance, and I stop fighting the book for something it was never going to give me in that section.

**What surprised me**

Three things.

It took far less time than I had imagined, a few minutes per book rather than the ceremony I had pictured.

The contents page is written by the author, deliberately, as a summary of their own argument, and most of us skip straight past it as though it were the credits at the start of a film.

And doing it made me want to read the book more, because once you can see the shape you start having questions, and then you are reading to answer your own questions rather than simply getting through pages.

**What I am actually going to do**

For non-fiction, every time. Contents, sub-headings, a guess at the argument, then read.

Whether I memorise the contents page, as the strictest version suggests, probably not for every book. Perhaps for the thesis one, since I need that to live in my head and I will be returning to it constantly.

And not for fiction at all, because half the point of a novel is not knowing what is coming.

What is in your reading stack?

- Sources: TU Delft research portal (https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/convivial-toolbox-generative-research-for-the-front-end-of-design/) and OSU Department of Design (https://design.osu.edu/news/convivial-toolbox-book-published)

- *How to Read a Book*, first published 1940, revised with Charles Van Doren in 1972. Adler describes four levels of reading, the second being inspectional reading, which includes systematic skimming: the title page, the preface, the table of contents, the index, and dipping into passages to get the shape of the argument before reading properly.
- Safe to state. One of the most widely cited books about reading there is.
