```text
How to remember what you read, especially non-fiction books: before you start reading, study the book's structure (the contents page, the chapter titles, the sub-headings) and guess what the author will argue.
I try it live on two real books from my reading stack: How to Read and Write Critically by Alex Baratta, and Convivial Toolbox by Liz Sanders and Pieter Jan Stappers.

The idea is old. Mortimer Adler called it inspectional reading in How to Read a Book (1940). Justin Sung calls a version of it priming. I like the version I saw on TikTok: memorise the chapter titles, then read. It takes a few minutes per book, and it gives every new idea a place to go in your mind.

Not for novels, though. For fiction, I want to be surprised.

What is in your current reading stack? Tell me in the comments.
```

**The problem**

You read a whole book. You sit with it for weeks. Then somebody asks what it was about, and you can tell them the general vibe, and maybe one or two ideas that stuck, and that is all. What you are left holding is a feeling about the book rather than the book.

**The method**

Before you read the book, you read the structure of the book.

You go to the contents page and study it properly. The chapters, then the sub-headings underneath them. You look at how the whole thing has been laid out before you read a single page of the actual content. And then you read.

That is the entire method, which is probably why people skip it.

I first saw it on Pinterest, then on YouTube, and it turns out to be an old idea that keeps getting repackaged. Mortimer Adler described it in How to Read a Book in 1940 and called it inspectional reading. A newer version circulates on YouTube as priming, where you spend a few minutes on the headings and ask how the parts connect. And there is a stricter version going round on TikTok where people memorise the chapters and sub-chapters before starting. That is the one I use. Reading the headings helps; memorising them makes the map stronger in my mind.

**Why it works**

The reason, as far as I understand it, is about having somewhere to put things.

When you start a book cold, every new idea arrives and your brain has nowhere to file it, so it floats. Then the next one arrives and floats too, and by chapter six you are carrying a pile of ideas with nothing to attach to.

Study the structure first and you already know the shape of the argument, so when an idea appears you know which part of the book it belongs to and what it is doing there. You are building the shelves before you start putting things on them.

**Trying it**

Chapter one of Baratta's book is a question: what does it mean to be critical? Before reading a page, that told me the author thinks nobody has properly defined the word for us. I throw the phrase critical thinking around a lot, so now I am reading to find out whether my idea of it gets challenged or supported. Chapter three is about score bands and assessment, so I am not expecting philosophy. I am expecting practical academic strategy.

Doing it on Convivial Toolbox was even more useful, because of the page numbers. Part one, the underlying principles, is about 80 pages. Part two, cases from practice, is about 50. Part three, the tools and techniques, is over 140. More than half the book is the how-to section. It opens with theory, but it is a manual masquerading as a textbook.

That changes something small but real. If part one feels abstract and I catch myself waiting for it to get practical, that is not a failure of the book. The theory at the front is there to make the practical part at the back make sense. The contents page told me so in advance.

And at the back, chapter ten is about bridging: where are the gaps, who is bridging them. I do not know yet what gaps the authors mean. But now, when I meet the word gap in part one, I will pay attention to it. Read cold, I would have walked past it.

**What surprised me**

It took far less time than I had imagined: a few minutes per book.

And it made me want to read the book more, because once you can see the shape you start having questions, and then you are reading to answer your own questions rather than simply getting through pages.

**What I do now**

For non-fiction, every time. Contents, sub-headings, chapters, a guess at the argument, then read. And I memorise the chapter titles, because for me that is what makes it stick.

Not for fiction at all, because half the point of a novel is not knowing what is coming. (I had never even noticed that novels have a contents page too.)
