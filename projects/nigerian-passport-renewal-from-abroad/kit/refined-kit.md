This year I renewed my Nigerian passport, because it expired some months ago. I live in Estonia, where there's no Nigerian embassy, so I did it through the Nigerian embassy in Stockholm. I used the contactless renewal option, which meant I never left Tallinn. It's a simple process, but if you're not careful, it takes much longer than it should. I paid in August and my passport didn't get back to me until October, and I had to withdraw from a conference I was paid to speak at because it wasn't back in time. If you need to renew, I'd start at least two or three months before your passport expires.

The first decision is which embassy will process it, because you pick a processing embassy on the application form. If your country has a Nigerian embassy, this part is easy. Estonia doesn't. The Stockholm embassy's website says it covers Denmark, Finland and Norway alongside Sweden, and Estonia isn't on that list. My application went through anyway, and I know a few people here who did theirs through Sweden too. Still, if your country isn't on an embassy's list, I'd ask them before paying anything.

The application starts on the Nigeria Immigration Service passport portal. You choose the option for applying from outside Nigeria, then renewal or reissue, create an account with your NIN and date of birth, fill the form, choose your processing embassy, upload your photo and documents, and pay for the booklet. NIS says the fees for Nigerians abroad are $150 for the 32-page, five-year passport and $230 for the 64-page, ten-year one. That's from its August 2025 fee review, which raised the fees inside Nigeria and left the ones abroad as they were. I paid $230. After you pay, you get an application ID and a reference number, and you'll want your application number every time you contact the embassy.

My first real problem was the photo. The portal kept rejecting it. The one that finally went through had a white background, a portrait shape of three by four at 600 by 800 pixels, and my face filling about 70 to 80 percent of the height, in the centre. My square crops got rejected every time. If I were starting again, I'd shoot it against a white wall from the beginning.

The contactless part is what saved me the trip. It's called contactless biometric enrolment, and it means you capture your face and fingerprints in the NIS Mobile app instead of at the embassy. It started in Canada in late 2024, and it was announced for Nigerians in Europe in February 2025, with the UK and Ireland first. News reports at the launch said it's only for renewals, so first-time applicants and children can't use it. After you pay, the contactless option shows up on your application status page. It's separate from the appointment calendar, and going into the calendar first can lock you into the in-person route. In the app, the face capture was easy. The fingerprints were harder, because each one gets a quality score, and I went back and forth for a while before they went through. I paid $100 for the contactless part. When I did it, the app wasn't on the iPhone App Store in Estonia, so I used a borrowed Android phone. There's an iPhone version now, but it still isn't on the Estonian store.

Then there's the embassy's own admin fee, which you pay straight to the embassy and not on the portal. In Stockholm it was 500 Swedish kronor, non-refundable, and I reached out to them to confirm before I paid. Whenever I emailed them, I put my application number in and kept the message short. While all this was happening, my birth certificate got queried on the application, so I replaced it with a sworn affidavit of age, and after that my application was vetted.

Even with contactless biometrics, you still have to send your old passport to the embassy. I sent mine with my printed application form, my payment receipts and a prepaid return label, so the embassy could send the new one back to me. The Stockholm embassy only takes DHL or UPS.
