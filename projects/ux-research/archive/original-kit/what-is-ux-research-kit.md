**[SEGMENT 1: THE ONE QUESTION]**

If you're building an app, there is one very simple question you need to ask yourself before anything else.

Who is this app actually for? And what do they actually need it for?

That question is the heart of UX research, and it's a big part of what I study in my master's in human-computer interaction.

So in this video, I'm going to start from scratch, from the beginning. UI, UX, and then we'll walk it all the way up to UX research, how it is done, who a UX researcher is, and all of that.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: UI VS UX]**

You've probably heard people say UI UX like it's one word. UI/UX designer. UI/UX course. It's everywhere.

But UI and UX are two different things.

UI stands for user interface. It's what you can see and touch. The buttons, the colours, the layouts, the icons, the screen.

UX, on the other hand, stands for user experience. It's how the whole product feels to use.

Can a user find what they're looking for? Does it make sense to the user? Do they get frustrated using it? Does the user come back to use it?

So you can have an app with a beautiful interface that is a nightmare to use. Great UI, terrible UX.

**[ON SCREEN: UI = what you see / UX = how it feels to use]**

So your UI is kind of part of your UX. The experience is just much bigger than what is on the screen.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT IS UX]**

What is UX?

User experience, or UX, covers everything about how a person experiences a product or a service, I think.

The term was popularised by Don Norman back in the nineties, when he was at Apple. And the way he and Jakob Nielsen described it, user experience covers every part of a person's interaction with a company, its services and its products.

And all of this connects back to an idea called human-centred design.

The idea is simple. You can make sense of it in this one phrase.

Good design doesn't start with screens. It starts with people.

**[ON SCREEN: "Good design doesn't start with screens. It starts with people."]**

So you start from the people who will use a product, their needs, their abilities, their real lives, and you design around them.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHAT IS UX RESEARCH]**

And now, what is UX research?

UX research is how you get to know your users.

It is the work of understanding the people who would use a product, what they need, where they struggle, what they prioritise, so that you can build it the right way, and build it in a way that actually works for them.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: WHY DO UX RESEARCH AT ALL]**

Why on earth do we do research at all?

The idea is simple. You don't want to build the wrong thing.

Building is very expensive. Even with AI, you still spend time and tokens, and you probably still need a team to come together.

A lot of products fail because the team built something that nobody actually needs.

Some other products fail because they built the right thing, but they built it in a way that people just couldn't find their way around, or couldn't figure out.

UX research helps to catch both problems early. It is cheaper to change your idea, or change your sketch, than it is to rebuild an app that is already live.

And doing UX research means that decisions about a product come from what the users, the real people, actually do. Otherwise, they just come from a random person on the team.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: WHO IS A UX RESEARCHER]**

Now, who is a UX researcher? Who actually does UX research?

I mean, a UX researcher does UX research.

A UX researcher works closely with designers, product managers, engineers, and the people running the business. Their job is to bring the real users into those conversations.

A typical week for a UX researcher might look like planning a study, recruiting people to take part, or running user interviews or usability testing.

Then sitting down with the notes and recordings to find patterns.

And finally, sharing what they've found from these users and these studies with the team, in a way that actually changes what gets built.

People usually mix up who a UX researcher is and who a UX designer is, a lot.

A UX researcher's job is to understand the users and what they need. A UX designer's job is to take that understanding and design the product around it.

In small teams, usually one person ends up doing both jobs. And there are a lot of product managers too that do research as part of their jobs as well.

And you do not need to be able to code to do UX research.

What you need is to be genuinely curious about users, about people. Be really good at taking time to study people and find patterns, and really good at listening.

You must be interested in people.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: THE WAYS UX RESEARCH IS CATEGORISED]**

When I started reading about UX research, I kept running into different ways that people categorise it.

You go on Coursera, Coursera has quite a number. You go to different places, and it's a different kind of categorisation.

You see generative and evaluative. Qualitative and quantitative. Behavioural and attitudinal. Primary and secondary. Remote and in person. Moderated and unmoderated.

**[ON SCREEN: the six pairs]**

But there's a useful way to think about it. Some of these pairs describe what kind of information you get, like qualitative or quantitative. Others describe how you run the research, like remote or in person.

Anyway, it's a lot of categorisation. So we'll focus on the first three big pairs, the ones you'll hear the most.

Generative and evaluative. Qualitative and quantitative. Behavioural and attitudinal.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: GENERATIVE VS EVALUATIVE]**

The first big way to split UX research is generative versus evaluative.

Generative UX research happens early, before anything is designed.

It asks questions like, what is the real problem? What do people need? How do they get by right now without this thing we're about to build?

It generates ideas for what to build.

For example, for my thesis, I'm doing generative research.

Evaluative research happens later, after there is a design.

It asks questions like, does this product actually work? Can users use it? Where did the users get stuck while using it?

You're evaluating something that already exists.

So generative UX research helps you decide what to build, and evaluative UX research tells you whether what you built works.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: QUALITATIVE VS QUANTITATIVE]**

The second big way to split UX research is qualitative versus quantitative.

Quantitative UX research is about numbers. How many people completed the task? What percentage dropped off at checkout? How long did it take?

It tells you what is happening, and how much of it.

Qualitative UX research is about understanding. Why did the users drop off? What were they thinking? What confused them?

It's usually smaller and more in depth. It tells you the why.

Usually, you want both. The numbers show you where the problem is, and the conversations show you why the problem is happening.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: BEHAVIOURAL VS ATTITUDINAL]**

Now the third big way to split UX research is behavioural versus attitudinal.

Attitudinal UX research is about what users say. What they think, what they feel, what they tell you that they do. Surveys and interviews are mostly attitudinal.

Behavioural UX research, on the other hand, is about what people actually do. You watch them use the thing, or you look at the data on what they clicked.

And the reason this split matters so much is something I've taken from my programme. It was hammered into our ears during the first semester.

What people say and what people do are often very different.

What people say, what they give you in a survey, is usually different from what they do when you watch them.

If you ask someone how they use an app, they will give you an answer. If you watch them use the app, they could end up doing something completely different from the answer they gave you.

And they might genuinely believe what they told you. We are human beings. We're just not very good at noticing our own behaviour.

So good UX research watches as much as it asks.

What people say still matters. Sometimes they do exactly what they tell you. And when what they say and what they do don't match, working out why is also a very useful finding.

---

**[SEGMENT 11: THE OTHER CATEGORIES]**

Now, there are a few more categories of UX research, and I'll go over those quickly.

Primary research is research you do yourself, like running your own interviews. Secondary research uses what already exists, like published studies, industry reports and all of that.

Remote UX research happens over a video call or through an online tool. In-person research happens in the same room, where you can notice things like body language.

And then we have moderated research, where a researcher is there guiding the session and asking follow-up questions.

And unmoderated research, where people complete tasks on their own, usually through a tool that records them.

---

**[SEGMENT 12: COMMON UX RESEARCH METHODS]**

So how is UX research actually done? There are a lot of methods.

There's the user interview. You sit down with people and talk to them about their lives, their work, their frustrations, and maybe how they use a product, and all of that.

Usability testing. You give someone a task to do with your design, maybe with your app, and you watch the way they do the task, usually while they think out loud.

Then surveys. You can reach a lot of people quickly with surveys, but surveys usually have less depth.

There's contextual inquiry, where you go to where people work and live, and you watch them in their real environment.

There are also diary studies, where people record their experiences over days or weeks, so you see patterns that a single session would miss.

There's card sorting. You give people a set of topics and ask them to group them in whatever way makes sense to them.

There's A/B testing. There are all kinds of UX research methods that we can't go over in this video.

---

**[SEGMENT 13: UX RESEARCH FOR STARTUPS AND SMALL TEAMS]**

You might be thinking UX research sounds like something only big companies with big budgets can do.

But even as a small startup, some research is always better than no research.

You don't need a lab. Talk to five people who would actually use your product before you start to build it.

Watch three of your friends try your app on a video call. Read messages, read feedback. That also counts.

Because good design goes so much deeper than just a nice visual. It's about whether the thing actually works for the people using it.

---

**[SEGMENT 14: UX RESEARCH VS USER RESEARCH]**

Now, is UX research the same as user research?

Most of the time, people use the two terms to mean the same thing. You'll even see job titles that use either one.

Now, user research is the broader one, and UX research sits under it.

User research is about understanding the people themselves, the users, their needs, their habits, their lives, and it can feed into lots of decisions beyond design.

For example, research into how customers feel about your prices or your delivery time is user research. It isn't really about the experience of your product.

UX research is more focused on how people experience a specific product or service.

Either way, they share the same goal, which is making sure decisions are based on real users.

And both of them are also different from market research. Market research is more about what people will buy. UX research is about how people use the product, and how it feels to them.

---

**[SEGMENT 15: WILL AI REPLACE UX RESEARCH?]**

This is also a very good question to answer. Will AI replace UX research?

AI tools are actually already doing a lot of the slow work. They can transcribe interviews and summarise hours of notes, which saves UX researchers a lot of time.

I cannot imagine having to do my thesis four years ago. I wouldn't do it.

So AI is changing how UX research gets done, right? In a good way, I think, if you use it well.

---

**[SEGMENT 16: THE UX RESEARCH JOB MARKET]**

Now, if you're thinking about a career in UX research, I am still a student. I'm currently doing my master's in human-computer interaction.

And from what I've noticed, the number of jobs with the title UX researcher is small compared to other design roles.

That's because a lot of companies expect their designers and their product managers to do some research work themselves. So companies have fewer people whose whole job is research.

But it's still a very much needed skill. I think it's just the entry level that might be hard to break into.

I wouldn't know yet. I'm not in the job market for UX research yet. But that much I do know.

---

**[SEGMENT 17: HOW TO GET INTO UX RESEARCH]**

As for how to get into UX research, the way I'm getting into it is by doing a master's degree. But I want to believe you can get into it in different ways.

You can learn the basics. The methods, when to use which one, how to ask questions.

There are free guides from places like the Nielsen Norman Group, Maze and the Interaction Design Foundation. If you want a book, read Think Like a UX Researcher by David Travis and Philip Hodgson.

Do real research that nobody pays you for. Pick an app you use, interview a few people, watch them use it, and write up what you found. That might be your first case study.

You can also volunteer to do research for a small business, and things like that.

A lot of UX researchers, from what I've noticed, came from other fields. Psychology, teaching, customer support, marketing, journalism, science.

So if your job already involves talking to people and figuring out what they need, welcome. It's something I really love.

And of course, degrees. Going to school and doing a master's in human-computer interaction, or human-centred design, like the one I'm doing, is one route, and plenty of researchers take it.

---

So that's UX research.

And it's a long video, so if you got bored in the middle and skipped through a lot of it, just don't forget this.

Good design doesn't start with screens. Good design starts with people.

I found that line in a Medium article by a designer called Wardharaheem.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> Good Design Starts With People

### Subtitle:
> UX research explained from the beginning, by someone studying human-computer interaction.

### SEO Title:
> What Is UX Research? UI vs UX, Types of Research and Common Methods

### SEO Description:
> An HCI student's plain guide to UX research, from the difference between UI and UX to generative, qualitative and behavioural research, methods, and how it differs from user research.

---

If you are building an app, there is one question worth asking before anything else: who is this actually for, and what do they need it for? That question sits at the heart of UX research, which is a large part of what I study in my master's in human-computer interaction.

People often say UI and UX as if they were one word, but they are different. UI, the user interface, is what you see and touch: the buttons, colours, layouts and screens. UX, the user experience, is how the whole thing feels to use, which is why a beautiful interface can still be a nightmare to use. Don Norman popularised the term at Apple in the nineties, and he and Jakob Nielsen describe user experience as covering every part of a person's interaction with a company, its services and its products. It connects to human-centred design, the practice of starting from the people who will use something and designing around them. A line from a Medium article by the designer Wardharaheem has stayed with me ever since. Good design doesn't start with screens, it starts with people.

UX research is how you come to know those people. It exists because building the wrong thing is expensive, and because changing a sketch is far cheaper than rebuilding a live product. It also means decisions rest on what real people do, rather than on whoever in the room holds the strongest opinion.

UX researchers work alongside designers, product managers and engineers, planning studies, recruiting participants, running sessions, finding patterns and sharing what should change. Many designers and product managers do research too, and none of it requires coding.

Research is usually categorised in pairs. Generative research comes early and explores what to build, while evaluative research tests whether a design works. Quantitative research measures what is happening and how much, while qualitative research explains why. Attitudinal research captures what people say and behavioural research captures what they do, and the gap between the two is one of the most valuable things a team can learn. Beyond those, research can be primary or secondary, remote or in person, and moderated or unmoderated.

The methods include interviews, usability testing, surveys, contextual inquiry, diary studies, and analytics with A/B testing. And none of it is reserved for large companies. Talking to five prospective users before building already counts, and some research is always better than none.

For anyone considering it as a career, I can only speak as a student. From what I have noticed, jobs titled UX researcher are fewer than other design roles, because many designers and product managers now do research themselves, though the skill remains very much needed. You can learn the methods from free guides, do real research nobody pays you for and write it up as a first case study, volunteer for a small business, or come in from fields like psychology, teaching and support. A master's in human-computer interaction, like mine, is one route among several.

UX research and user research are mostly used interchangeably, though user research is sometimes treated as the broader term. Both differ from market research, which asks what people will buy rather than how they use something and how it feels.
