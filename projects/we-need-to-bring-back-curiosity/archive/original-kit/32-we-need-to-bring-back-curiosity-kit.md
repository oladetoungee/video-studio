**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

We need to bring back curiosity.

We need to bring back the "I don't know" state of mind.

The "I don't know" state of mind is what fuels curiosity.

And that "I don't know" state of mind is disappearing.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: HOW WE USED TO FIND THINGS OUT]**

There was a time we didn't have this easy access to knowledge or information.

**[ON SCREEN: ChatGPT came out in November 2022]**

Even as at four, five years ago, if you wanted to know something, you would have to search on Google, go through like different links, go through a few articles, a few posts, a few things about it, before you would come across it, like, oh yeah, yeah, it's this thing I'm looking for.

And before that, sometimes you'd have to go find a book about it, go to the library, or ask someone.

Sometimes you ask someone, they know a little bit, and then they point you to someone else, and then you talk to three people about it.

And you might get to know it at the end of all of that, or you might not get to know it.

But all of that is disappearing.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: GENERATIVE AI IS FILLING THE GAP]**

Generative AI is just completely filling that gap.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the turn to Claude and "boom, almost immediately." The open question for the viewer is what that does to curiosity, and the next lines answer it.]**

If I don't know something, and I become conscious of the fact that I don't know it, I can just open Claude and prompt Claude, and boom, almost immediately, I can just get to know it.

Obviously, it is a good thing, because it helps us access information faster.

But I also think that it, in turn, decreases the curious state of our mind.

It decreases curiosity because the "I don't know" state, that state where you don't know something, where you want to know something, you're trying to know something, is one of the states of mind that helps curiosity, that breeds curiosity.

And that state of mind is becoming more and more absent.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHAT IS CURIOSITY?]**

Curiosity is the state of having an open question.

We are no longer having open questions.

Our questions are being consistently, immediately answered.

**[ON SCREEN: "a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding." George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity," Psychological Bulletin, 1994]**

Curiosity, according to the words of the psychologist George Loewenstein, is a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding.

In plain words, you notice something that you don't know, and not knowing it feels like a little bit of hunger, and you want to fill it up.

It's like when someone is telling you gist, and then they stop and say, "I'll tell you later."

Now you cannot rest until they tell you.

Curiosity lives in the not knowing, in the half-knowing, in the semi-knowing.

You know enough to have a guess.

You know enough to know that you don't know, and now you're curious.

And that curiosity, that waiting, that open-endedness, it gets your brain ready in a way.

When our questions are answered in a second, it closes that gap too fast for us.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: GOOGLING VS ASKING AI]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the Googling era against the AI era. Viewers who remember clicking through links stay to hear the difference.]**

With Google, you can say that Googling was a form of getting an immediate answer.

But at least we had to click links and read different kind of stuff on different kind of sites.

But with generative AI, we don't even look at a list of links.

We just get one answer that is already put together for us.

You can't even compare it to the Googling era, because then, when you search, you have to find the information and put it together for yourself.

The "I don't know" state.

I don't have a better word for it, but to call it an "I don't know" state of mind.

It used to last longer, in a way that it breeds curiosity well enough, but now it just lasts about as long as it takes to type a prompt.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: I'M NOT ROMANTICISING NOT KNOWING]**

And when I talk about this, it feels to me, even to me, if I wanna counter my own point, it feels to me like I'm romanticising ignorance, or not knowing something.

I think that access to information is really good.

In this age and time, AI is one of the best things that we have, and I'm grateful for it.

I don't want to go back to a time where I have a question and it just sits unanswered for months, or having to go into a library and search and search and search.

I don't want that either.

My point is the prolonged state of not knowing.

That is the thing that I feel like we have lost.

Not knowing something until I read like five articles that different people compile together, and I try to bring everything together, you know.

But that is no longer there.

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> We Need to Bring Back Curiosity

### Subtitle:
> The "I don't know" state of mind used to last a while. Now it lasts about as long as it takes to type a prompt.

### SEO Title:
> Is AI Killing Our Curiosity? Googling vs Asking AI

### SEO Description:
> Why the "I don't know" state of mind fuels curiosity, how generative AI closes that gap almost immediately, and why I miss the prolonged state of not knowing.

---

We need to bring back curiosity, and to do that, I think we need to bring back the "I don't know" state of mind. By that I mean the state where you don't know something, you want to know it, and you're trying to know it. That state is what fuels curiosity, and I think it is disappearing.

There was a time we didn't have this easy access to knowledge or information. Even four or five years ago, if you wanted to know something, you would search on Google, go through different links, and read a few articles and a few posts before you came across the thing you were looking for. Before that, you might have to find a book about it, go to the library, or ask someone. Sometimes the person you asked knew a little bit and pointed you to someone else, and after talking to three people you might get to know it, or you might not.

Generative AI is filling that gap completely. If I don't know something and I become conscious of the fact that I don't know it, I can open Claude, prompt it, and know it almost immediately. That is a good thing, because it helps us access information faster. I also think it, in turn, decreases the curious state of our mind, because the "I don't know" state is one of the states of mind that breeds curiosity, and it is becoming more and more absent.

Curiosity is the state of having an open question, and we don't have many open questions anymore, because our questions are answered almost as soon as we ask them. The psychologist George Loewenstein, in his 1994 paper "The Psychology of Curiosity," described curiosity as "a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding." In plain words, you notice something you don't know, not knowing it feels like a little bit of hunger, and you want to fill it. It's like when someone is telling you gist, and they stop and say, "I'll tell you later." You cannot rest until they tell you.

Curiosity lives in the not knowing, in the half-knowing. You know enough to have a guess, and you know enough to know that you don't know. I think that waiting, that open-endedness, gets your brain ready in a way. When our questions are answered in a second, that gap closes too fast for us.

You could say that Googling was already a form of getting an immediate answer. But at least we had to click links and read different kinds of things on different sites. With generative AI, we often don't even look at a list of links. We get one answer that is already put together for us. When you searched, you had to find the information and put it together for yourself. The "I don't know" state used to last long enough to breed curiosity, and now it lasts about as long as it takes to type a prompt.

When I talk about this, even I feel like I'm romanticising ignorance. I'm not. I think access to information is really good, AI is one of the best things we have right now, and I'm grateful for it. I don't want to go back to a time where a question sits unanswered for months, or where I have to go into a library and search and search. My point is the prolonged state of not knowing. Not knowing something until I've read five articles from different people and tried to bring everything together myself. That is the thing I feel like we have lost.
