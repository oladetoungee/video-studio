**[CHAPTER: Paperwork and how the country runs]**

**[CARD: 01 / how the country runs]**

**[SEGMENT 2: UNDERSTAND HOW THE COUNTRY RUNS]**

The first thing I'd tell anyone about to study abroad is to try to understand how the country they're going to runs.

So don't just be focused on your school, your programme.

Understand how things get done in the country.

In Estonia, for example, when you get here as an international student, you'll probably come on a visa.

One of the things you have to do is apply for a residence permit card.

Estonians have an ID card that everyone has, and as an international student, the residence permit card kind of works the same way.

So basically, you use it to prove who you are online, and to sign documents digitally.

You can set it up on your phone.

Banks use it, and a lot of websites here in Estonia even ask for it before you set up an account and all of that.

**[ON SCREEN: residence permit card = prove who you are online + sign documents digitally + banks and websites]**

Now, this might be different in the country you're going to, if it's not Estonia.

It might not be something as digital as that.

Things might be more manual.

So you want to know what's up.

If I come on a student visa, do I have to get the residence permit?

Or do I keep studying on my visa?

Just try to find out how it all works.

In Estonia, before your residence permit card comes, I don't even think you can start a proper job.

In Tallinn, public transport is free, but only if you're a registered Tallinn resident.

So if you're not a resident, you pay for transport.

So it influences the decisions you make in that way, and you need to understand how it works.

You also want to register your address and stuff like that.

Even if you're living in the school dormitory, you should still register that address, because you're a tenant regardless.

**[ON SCREEN: Estonia: register your address within 1 month of your permit · Tallinn: free public transport for registered Tallinn residents (as of November 2026)]**

But again, the rules can change, and every country is different.

So try to understand how the country you're going to runs, or how the city runs, and try to set things up for yourself.

Okay.

For example, if you're coming to Estonia to study, you can work.

**[CHAPTER: Health insurance and the winter]**

**[CARD: 03 / health insurance]**

**[SEGMENT 4: HEALTH INSURANCE AS AN INTERNATIONAL STUDENT]**

Another thing you need to get right before you study abroad is health insurance as a student.

Health insurance is very important.

You don't want to be in a country or a city where you don't know so many people, where you're still trying to build a community around yourself, and you don't have health insurance.

You need that insurance.

At least from my experience, when I was coming from Nigeria, I had to buy an insurance.

It wasn't really one of those ones that cover a lot.

So yeah.

You can also try to understand how health insurance works in the country.

Is it free?

**Title:** What I Wish I Knew Before Studying Abroad
**Subtitle:** I'm in my second year of a master's in Estonia. These are the practical things I'd tell anyone about to move, based on my experience.
**SEO title:** What I Wish I Knew Before Studying Abroad: Practical Tips From Estonia
**SEO description:** A Nigerian master's student in Tallinn on the practical side of studying abroad: how the country runs, working while you study, health insurance, the weather, housing, language, driving licences and renewing your passport.

If you're looking to study abroad, I wrote this for you. I moved to Tallinn, the capital of Estonia, in autumn 2025 for my master's degree, and I'm now in my second year. These are practical things based on my experience, and I want to put emphasis on based on my experience. Some of them I knew before I came, and some of them I wish I knew. I use Estonia as my example, but I think most of this applies wherever you're going.

The first thing I'd tell you is to try to understand how the country runs, and to not only focus on your school and your programme. In Estonia, you'll probably arrive on a visa and then apply for a residence permit card, and that card works a lot like the ID card Estonians have. You use it to prove who you are online and to sign documents digitally, you can set it up on your phone, and banks and a lot of websites ask for it. Before your card comes, I don't even think you can start a proper job. In Tallinn, public transport is free, but only if you're a registered Tallinn resident, so if you're not, you pay. The Police and Border Guard Board also says you have to register your place of residence within a month after your permit is granted, and you should do that even if you live in the school dormitory, because you're a tenant regardless. Where you're going might be more manual than this, so find out how it works before you land.

The second is the work situation. In Estonia, the Police and Border Guard Board says students on a residence permit for study don't need a separate work permit, but there are conditions. You have to keep studying full-time, and your work cannot get in the way of your studies. If you interrupt your studies, or you don't complete your programme to the required extent, your permit ends 30 days after that. Start planning for what happens after you graduate from the onset, too. In Estonia, after your study permit ends, you can stay 270 more days, which is about nine months, and you can work during that time. If you find a job, you can apply for a residence permit for work. That extra time doesn't apply if the permit was revoked or ended early.

The third is health insurance. You don't want to be in a city where you don't know many people, where you're still building a community around yourself, without it. When I was coming from Nigeria, I had to buy an insurance before applying for my visa, and it wasn't really one that covered a lot. In Estonia, Tallinn University says students from outside the EU need internationally valid private health insurance. Once you have a job with an employment contract, your employer pays social tax, and Tervisekassa, the Estonian Health Insurance Fund, says your cover starts after fourteen days, as long as the contract is longer than a month or has no end date. If you don't have that, read what your private insurance covers before you buy it.

The fourth is the weather. In Estonia, winter is long, winter is dark and winter is so cold. In Tallinn in December, you get about six hours of daylight. I know someone who landed in the hospital during their first winter here. If you're coming from somewhere like Nigeria to the Baltics, it's like going from one end of the world to the other in terms of the weather, so really prepare for it.

The fifth is where you'll live. If your school has a dormitory, that part is usually easy. I stayed in mine for about a month plus, and it was really nice, but I moved out afterwards. The dorm is cheaper. Mine was about €250 a month, and living outside it is roughly double that budget. Think about what kind of living fits you, and your budget, before you come, because it will make life easier for you.

The sixth is the language. Your course is probably in English, but if the country's language isn't, I think learning it helps a lot. In *And the Mountains Echoed*, Khaled Hosseini wrote that "if culture was a house, then language was the key to the front door, to all the rooms inside." At my school, we have to do an Estonian A1 class, so it was easy for me to start. Even where it isn't required, I think it's always nice to meet the culture of the place you're studying in through its language. If you're going to spend a year or two of your life somewhere, you might as well enjoy it.

The last practical ones are documents from home. In Estonia, Transpordiamet says a Nigerian driving licence is valid for 12 months from when your residency starts, and after that you exchange it. Licences like ours are generally exchanged without exams, and you hand the Nigerian one over. I've exchanged mine. And my Nigerian passport expired some months ago. There's no Nigerian embassy in Estonia, so I renewed mine through the Nigerian Embassy in Stockholm, using the contactless renewal option the Nigerian government launched for eligible Nigerians in Europe. I did it in Sweden, and I didn't have to go to Sweden to do it.

And then, enjoy it. Be open-minded about the place. There will be things you don't like and things you do, so lean into the things you like. Even if you're only there for the length of your programme, try to enjoy it. Go for Erasmus programmes if your school takes part. I've been on two so far. Join programmes at school and meet people. Studying abroad will take your whole life if you give it, especially if you're working, but try to breathe the air of the country a little bit. If you're already studying abroad, I'd love to know what you wish you knew before you came.
