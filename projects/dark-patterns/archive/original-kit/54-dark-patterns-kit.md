**[SEGMENT 1: WHAT A DARK PATTERN IS]**

If you get a pop-up that offers you 10% off, and the button to close it says, "No thanks, I hate saving money," there's a name for that.

**[ON SCREEN: a pop-up with a big "Get 10% off" button and, under it, a small grey link that says "No thanks, I hate saving money"]**

It's called a dark pattern.

A dark pattern is a design in an app or a website that tricks you into doing something you didn't mean to do, like buying something extra.

Now, a dark pattern is different from just bad design.

Most of the time, a dark pattern is intentional.

The dark pattern usually helps the business at the expense of the user.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: TYPES OF DARK PATTERNS, PART 1]**

There are a lot of dark patterns that people use to build stuff, and I'll go over a lot of them.

Most of the names I'm using come from Harry Brignull, the UX designer who coined the term "dark patterns" in 2010.

His website is deceptive.design, because a lot of people now call them deceptive patterns.

**[ON SCREEN: deceptive.design, "Deceptive patterns trick people into doing things they didn't mean to."]**

First, we have confirmshaming.

Confirmshaming is when the button to say no is written to make you feel bad.

Like, look at this. "No, I hate saving money."

**[ON SCREEN: the same pop-up, with the grey "No thanks, I hate saving money" link circled]**

The idea is that you'd rather click yes than say you hate saving money.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the first type has landed, and the next few cost people real money]**

Another dark pattern is hard to cancel, which people used to call the roach motel.

The name comes from a cockroach trap, where it's easy to get in and hard to get out.

It's when joining is easy, but leaving is hard.

Say you join an online shop's delivery membership with one click, but to cancel, you have to go through page after page of offers.

We also have another dark pattern called sneak into basket.

It's when something you didn't choose ends up in your cart.

Say you add a pair of shoes, and at checkout there's also a cleaning spray for 5 euros that you never picked.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT LINE: "Keep count of how many of these you've seen this week."]**

And there's hidden costs, where there are fees that only show up at the last step of checkout.

Say the shoes cost 40 euros on the product page, and on the last page, with a service fee added, it's 52 euros.

By then you've typed in your card details, so it feels easier to just pay.

There's also hidden subscription, which is like a free trial that turns into a paid plan without a clear warning.

Say a shop gives you a free month of premium, but asks for your card first, and when the month ends, it starts charging you without telling you clearly.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: TYPES OF DARK PATTERNS, PART 2]**

There are more dark patterns you'll run into when you use apps and shop online.

Another dark pattern is trick wording.

It's a question that is written so that you click the opposite of what you meant to click.

Something like, "Untick this box if you don't want to stop receiving emails."

**[ON SCREEN: a ticked checkbox next to "Untick this box if you don't want to stop receiving emails."]**

You have to read it two or three times to work out which one stops the emails.

Disguised ads are ads that are made to look like part of the page.

Say you search a shop for running shoes, and the first few results are paid ads that look exactly like the normal results.

**[~3:00 BEAT: halfway through the list, and the ones that rush you are still coming]**

We also have nagging.

Nagging is asking again after the user has said no.

Say a shopping app asks you to turn on notifications, and you say no.

Next time, it asks again, and the only options are "yes" and "not now."

Preselection is when the option the company wants is already ticked for the user.

Say there's a box at checkout that says "Add gift wrapping for 2.99 euros," and it's already ticked.

If you don't read every line, you pay for gift wrapping you never asked for.

**[ON SCREEN: code, the only difference is the word "checked"]**
```html
<!-- preselection: already ticked for you -->
<input type="checkbox" name="gift_wrap" checked>

<!-- honest: you decide -->
<input type="checkbox" name="gift_wrap">
```

Fake urgency is a deadline that is not real.

Say a product page shows a countdown that says "This deal ends in 10 minutes."

You refresh the page, and it starts again from 10 minutes.

It's there to rush you, so you buy before you've had time to think.

**[ON SCREEN: code]**
```js
// fake: a brand new 10-minute deadline every time the page loads
const deadline = Date.now() + 10 * 60 * 1000;

// real: the deadline comes from when the sale actually ends
const deadline = new Date(sale.endsAt).getTime();
```

Fake scarcity is when a website says there's only a few left, or that something is selling out fast, when that isn't true.

Say a pair of shoes says "Only 2 left in stock," and when you come back a week later, it still says "Only 2 left."

It's there to make you buy now.

**[SEGMENT 4: WHY COMPANIES USE DARK PATTERNS]**

So why would a company use dark patterns at all?

A lot of the time, it comes down to numbers.

Most product teams test their designs with something called an A/B test.

Half of the users see version A, the other half see version B, and whichever one gets more sales or sign-ups wins.

But an A/B test only measures the number you picked, so it can't tell whether anyone was tricked.

Harry Brignull says this in his book, Deceptive Patterns.

In his words, "A/B testing doesn't judge whether a particular design is actually better or worse for the user."

I think this is how a lot of dark patterns end up in apps.

A team is chasing a number, and nobody stops to ask what the winning version costs the user.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: HOW COMMON DARK PATTERNS ARE]**

So how common are dark patterns?

In January 2023, the European Commission checked 399 online shops, and 148 of them had at least one of the three dark patterns it was looking for.

Forty-two of those websites had fake countdown timers, and seventy were hiding important information or making it hard to see.

**[ON SCREEN: European Commission sweep, January 2023. 399 online shops checked, 148 with at least one dark pattern, 42 with fake countdown timers.]**

**[~6:00 BEAT: the EU number has landed, and the next study is bigger and global]**

Then in July 2024, consumer and privacy agencies from 26 countries, including the FTC, looked at 642 websites and apps that sell subscriptions.

They found that nearly 76 percent used at least one possible dark pattern, and nearly 67 percent used more than one.

They say "possible" because a review like this flags things for a closer look. It doesn't decide what's illegal.

**[ON SCREEN: ICPEN and GPEN review, July 2024. 642 subscription websites and apps, nearly 76% with at least one possible dark pattern.]**

---

**[SEGMENT 6: WHAT THE LAW SAYS ABOUT DARK PATTERNS]**

So are dark patterns illegal?

More and more, depending on where you live. I'm not a lawyer, but this is where things stand as of October 2026.

In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, from 2005, already bans some of these tricks, like falsely saying a deal is only available for a very limited time.

Then Article 25 of the Digital Services Act says online platforms, like marketplaces and social media apps, must not design their interfaces in a way that deceives or manipulates you.

It even names examples, like asking you again after you've already made a choice, and making it harder to cancel than it was to sign up.

**[ON SCREEN: Digital Services Act, Article 25]**

For your data, the GDPR says a pre-ticked box doesn't count as consent.

And if a shop charged you for an extra through a box that was already ticked, EU consumer law says you're entitled to that money back.

**[OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT LINE: "And if you're in the US, this next part is about you."]**

In the US, the best-known case is Amazon Prime.

The FTC said Amazon signed people up for Prime without their consent and made cancelling hard.

In September 2025, Amazon agreed to pay 2.5 billion dollars to settle, without admitting any wrongdoing.

**[ON SCREEN: FTC v. Amazon, settled September 2025. $2.5 billion ($1B civil penalty, up to $1.5B in refunds). No admission of wrongdoing.]**

The FTC also made a "click to cancel" rule in 2024, but in July 2025, a US court threw it out before it took effect, because the FTC skipped a required step.

The FTC started again in March 2026, and as of October 2026, there's no new rule yet.

And the EU is working on a new law, the Digital Fairness Act, which is expected to deal with dark patterns more directly. As of October 2026, it hasn't been published yet.

**[SEGMENT 7: HOW TO SPOT AND AVOID DARK PATTERNS]**

So how do you spot and avoid dark patterns?

Slow down at checkout and when you're cancelling something, because that's where a lot of them show up.

At checkout, read every ticked box, and compare the final total with the price on the product page.

When you see a countdown timer, refresh the page. If it starts again, it was never a real deadline.

When a free trial asks for your card, set a reminder for two days before it ends.

If you subscribed through the App Store or Google Play, you can cancel from your phone's subscription settings.

And when a button makes you feel guilty for saying no, click no anyway.

**[~9:00 BEAT: the study that explains why habits matter more than knowing the names]**

In 2021, a study from the University of Luxembourg surveyed 406 people, and found that being aware of manipulative design didn't stop people being influenced by it.

The title of the paper is a quote from one of them. "I am definitely manipulated, even when I am aware of it. It's ridiculous!"

So I think habits like these help more than just knowing the names.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: IF YOU'RE ASKED TO BUILD A DARK PATTERN]**

Now, if you're a developer or a designer, at some point you might be asked to build a dark pattern.

It usually won't be called a dark pattern. It'll be a ticket that says "pre-tick the newsletter box," or "add a countdown to checkout."

First, ask what number the change is meant to move, and suggest an honest way to get there, like a pause option for people who want to cancel, with a clear cancel button on the same page.

Second, name the risk. I think "this could get us fined" lands better in a meeting than "this feels wrong," even if you mean both.

Third, put your concern in writing, in the ticket or in an email.

And then build the honest version well. Leave the boxes unticked, and make cancelling as easy as signing up.

### SHORT 5: Why companies use dark patterns (25-35 sec)

Why would a company use dark patterns?

Most teams test designs with A/B tests. Half the users see version A, half see version B, and whichever gets more sales or sign-ups wins.

But an A/B test only measures the number you picked, so it can't tell whether anyone was tricked.

Harry Brignull puts it this way. "A/B testing doesn't judge whether a particular design is actually better or worse for the user."

So the tricky version can win, and it ships.

### SHORT 6: What happened to click to cancel? (25-35 sec)

In 2024, the FTC made a "click to cancel" rule in the US, which would have made cancelling as easy as signing up.

In July 2025, a US court threw it out before it took effect, because the FTC skipped a required step.

The FTC started the process again in March 2026, and as of October 2026, there's no new rule yet.

But the FTC can still bring cases. In September 2025, Amazon agreed to pay 2.5 billion dollars to settle the FTC's case over Prime, without admitting wrongdoing.

### SHORT 7: When you're asked to build a dark pattern (25-35 sec)

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> The Box That Was Already Ticked

### Subtitle:
> Eleven dark patterns you'll meet when you shop online, why companies use them, and what the law says in 2026.

### SEO Title:
> Dark Patterns Explained: 11 Types, Examples and the Law in 2026

### SEO Description:
> What dark patterns are, eleven common types with everyday examples, why companies use them, how common they are, and what EU and US law says as of October 2026.

---

You have probably closed a pop-up whose button said "No thanks, I hate saving money." Somebody wrote that line, and there's a name for it. A dark pattern is a design in an app or a website that tricks you into doing something you didn't mean to do, like buying something extra, and it usually helps the business at your expense. Harry Brignull, a UX designer, coined the term in 2010, and his site, now called Deceptive Patterns, still collects and names them. A lot of people now say "deceptive patterns" for the same idea.

That guilt-trip button is called confirmshaming. Hard to cancel, which people used to call the roach motel after the cockroach trap that's easy to get into and hard to get out of, is when joining takes one click and leaving takes page after page. Sneak into basket is when something you didn't choose, like a cleaning spray next to the shoes you picked, ends up in your cart. Hidden costs are the fees that only appear at the last step of checkout, when you've already typed in your card details and it feels easier to just pay. A hidden subscription is a free trial that turns into a paid plan without a clear warning.

The others work on how you read and how fast you decide. Trick wording is a question written so you click the opposite of what you meant, like "Untick this box if you don't want to stop receiving emails." Disguised ads are paid results that look exactly like the normal ones. Nagging is asking again after you've said no, with a "not now" button and no "never." Preselection is the gift wrapping box that's already ticked, and in HTML that is a single word, "checked." Fake urgency is a countdown that starts again when you refresh the page, and fake scarcity is "only 2 left in stock" when that isn't true.

Companies use these because they move numbers, and the way most teams choose between designs makes that easy. An A/B test shows two versions to two groups and keeps whichever one gets more sales or sign-ups. In his book, Brignull writes that "A/B testing doesn't judge whether a particular design is actually better or worse for the user."

They are common. In January 2023, the European Commission checked 399 online shops and found at least one of the three dark patterns it was looking for on 148 of them, including fake countdown timers on 42. In July 2024, agencies from 26 countries, the FTC among them, reviewed 642 websites and apps that sell subscriptions and found that nearly 76 percent used at least one possible dark pattern. And a 2021 study from the University of Luxembourg found that being aware of manipulative design didn't stop people being influenced by it, which its title puts in a participant's words: "I am definitely manipulated, even when I am aware of it. It's ridiculous!"

The law is catching up unevenly. In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, from 2005, already bans falsely saying a product is only available for a very limited time, and Article 25 of the Digital Services Act says online platforms must not design their interfaces in a way that deceives or manipulates users. The GDPR says a pre-ticked box isn't consent, and EU consumer law says that if a shop charged you for an extra through a box ticked by default, you're entitled to a refund. In the US, Amazon agreed in September 2025 to pay $2.5 billion to settle the FTC's case over Prime, without admitting wrongdoing. The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was thrown out by a federal court in July 2025 before it took effect, the FTC restarted the process in March 2026, and as of October 2026 there is no new rule. The EU's planned Digital Fairness Act is expected to deal with dark patterns more directly, but it had not been published at the time of writing.

As a user, the habits that help are small. Read every ticked box before you pay, compare the final total with the product page, refresh any countdown, set a reminder the day a free trial takes your card, and click no when a button tries to shame you. As a developer or designer, when a ticket asks for a pre-ticked box or a harder cancel flow, ask what number it's meant to move, offer an honest way to get there, name the legal risk, put your concern in writing, and then build the honest version well.
