## What she says (with times) vs the kit
**She says** (approx. times, from word timings):
- 0:00-0:45 Open: "Your phone was intentionally and strategically designed to distract you." Pick it up for one thing, 20 minutes later "doing a million and one other things" and forgetting the one thing. "My name is Gbemisola, I'm currently doing my master's in human-computer interaction." Plan: why attention is worth money, where the design ideas come from, the features, the people who built them, what you can do.
- 0:45-1:57 Attention economy: Herbert Simon, 1971, later a Nobel Prize, "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention"; attention is scarce, "traded like currency"; "You don't pay Instagram to use Instagram… you don't pay TikTok, you don't pay Facebook"; "people call it a free service, but in a way it is monetised"; targeted ads; "the more time you spend on the app, the more ads you see, the more the app learns about you"; "your attention is, in a way, the product".
- 1:57-3:59 Persuasive technology: B.J. Fogg, Stanford, Persuasive Technology Lab, book on using computers to change what people think and do; 2007 famous class where students used these ideas to build apps, "within weeks their apps had millions of users", some went on to build popular apps. Fogg Behavior Model: motivation, ability, prompt. Notification = prompt; one tap = ability; curiosity = motivation. Mike Krieger (Instagram co-founder) was his student, "and so was Tristan Harris, who later became one of the loudest critics of this kind of design". "Not to demonise… but the ideas he found and taught were real, and of course the industry picked up on them and made a shitload of money out of that." (Swear word: bleep or cut in any clip.)
- 3:59-4:52 Unpredictable reward: "the bedrock of gambling"; sometimes nothing, sometimes small, sometimes big; psychologists have long known unpredictable rewards bring you back more strongly; "you don't know if there is a new message, a new like on your video, a new video for you to see, a new gist… that not knowing is exactly why you keep picking up your phone to check."
- 4:52-5:25 Pull-to-refresh: drag your feed down and let go; "developed by a developer called Loren [Brichter] for a Twitter app he built called Tweetie, around 2008"; "he himself later said that this thing is really addictive"; unpredictable reward again; "so you keep pulling down".
- 5:25-6:53 Infinite scroll: websites used to have pages, you clicked "next page"; "that click was a stopping point… your brain has the chance to ask, do I actually want to keep going"; "infinite scroll was designed in 2006" (she does **not** name Aza Raskin); a load-more button "would send a cue to my brain… I want to drop my freaking phone". Stopping cues: books have chapters ("I try to get to the end of one chapter so I can drop the book"), TV shows ended and you waited a week, "when I was a kid I would buy movies, you'd buy season one… go back to buy season two"; "your feed does not have a last page, and it's intentionally designed that way".
- 6:53-7:42 Autoplay: next YouTube video starts on its own; "deciding to stop takes effort, deciding to continue doesn't take any effort"; defaults: autoplay on by default, most people don't change settings.
- 7:42-9:01 Notifications and red badges: "did not happen by accident"; "a notification is an interruption that someone else or something else decides to send you"; Facebook's icon "used to be blue to match the rest of the site… according to reports people barely noticed the blue one, so it was changed to red"; red badge + number = "something is unfinished… a sense of urgency"; CHI study (she explains CHI is the main conference in her field): notifications on one week, off another; on-week = more inattention and hyperactivity symptoms.
- 9:01-9:57 Likes: Like button launched on Facebook in 2009; "a small burst of social approval"; "when I make a post and drop my phone I don't know how many likes I will get"; "have you ever made a post… and you keep going back to check it?"
- 9:57-10:18 The "brain drain" claim: one study says a phone on your desk, even switched off, makes you worse at thinking; "a very interesting topic that just attracts a lot of claims". **She does NOT say it failed to replicate** (the kit's "pinch of salt" line is not in the recording).
- 10:18-11:19 EU: Feb 2026 preliminary finding that TikTok's design breaches the Digital Services Act; "the EU deserves like a global award… some regulations can feel overbearing, but some are very helpful"; named infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications, highly personalised recommendations; July: same kind of preliminary finding against Meta (Instagram, Facebook); "one of the first times a regulator has formally said these features… can be a legal problem"; not final; "I live in the EU and I'm very curious to see how this all goes".
- 11:19-12:44 Her rules: at 19 she used Instagram a lot ("would have turned out to be one influencer or something"), felt it was "very dysfunctional to the life I wanted to live", "it wasn't even as popular as it is now", "so I left Instagram and I didn't use the app for six good years"; rule now = **need** (open Twitter/X when she needs something: what people say about a topic, up-to-date information, posting her content; same for Instagram and TikTok); "and of course I break it sometimes"; **all notifications off, even WhatsApp** (people with her number can call); "this sounds a little bit hypocritical because I post content on these apps myself… but it would do nobody any good if we're all just stuck on our phones."
- 12:44-13:56 What to try ("actually backed up by research"): grayscale in accessibility settings (a study: less time on phone; "your phone becomes a little less rewarding"); turn off autoplay on YouTube and Netflix ("puts the stopping point back"); turn off notifications you don't need ("don't turn off Slack… you would not die if you don't get these notifications immediately"); move distracting apps off your home screen; **she uses an app blocker she has paid a yearly subscription for since January** ("I have to go through several mountains and some valleys to access Instagram"); before opening an app ask "what do I need from Instagram right now? Reply to someone, post something, or do I just want to scroll?"
- 13:56-14:52 Close: "your attention is an asset"; the techniques "are intentionally crafted, you need to know that"; next time you scroll for one or two hours remember people built these features, "working so hard for you to stay on Instagram… on TikTok"; "not something I've completely figured out either"; she thinks she uses social media less than average "at least this year, last year"; "it is literally being fought over by different corporations, and you need to take it back, for yourself, for the people who love you, the people that you love, for the things that are important in your life."
