**[SEGMENT 1: THE OPEN]**

Your phone was intentionally and strategically designed to distract you.

It's the reason why you pick it up to do one thing, and twenty minutes later you're still scrolling, and you've forgotten the one thing you wanted to do.

My name is Gbemisola, and I'm currently doing my master's in human-computer interaction.

In this video, I'm going to show you, first of all, why your attention is worth so much money in the first place, because people make a lot of money just from you scrolling.

Then where the design ideas that keep you stuck on your phone come from, the actual features that make this happen, and the people who built them.

And then finally, what you can actually do about it.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: THE ATTENTION ECONOMY]**

To understand why your phone is designed to distract you, you first have to understand something called the attention economy.

Back in 1971, an economist called Herbert Simon, who later won a Nobel Prize, wrote that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.

**[ON SCREEN: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." Herbert Simon, 1971]**

What he meant was that when information is everywhere, the thing that becomes scarce is your attention. And there is only so much human attention to go around.

So the attention economy is a system where human attention is treated as a scarce, valuable thing, and it's traded like currency.

Now think about how social media apps make their money.

You don't pay Instagram. You don't pay TikTok. You don't pay Facebook, right?

People call it a free service, but in a way, it's monetised. They make money from your engagement and your attention, by selling targeted adverts.

So the more time you spend on the app, the more adverts you see, and the more the app learns about you.

It is your time and your attention that are actually being sold. And that's why so many apps are built to keep you in them for as long as possible.

So your attention, in a way, is the product.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: PERSUASIVE TECHNOLOGY AND B.J. FOGG]**

Technology can be designed in a way that changes people's behaviour. And this idea is called persuasive technology.

A lot of it traces back to a researcher at Stanford University called B.J. Fogg.

He ran a lab there called the Persuasive Technology Lab, and he later published a book on using computers to change what people think and do.

To make it even more interesting, in 2007, B.J. Fogg taught a famous class at Stanford where students used these ideas to build Facebook apps. Within ten weeks, those apps had millions of users.

B.J. Fogg also described a simple model of behaviour.

For someone to do something, one, they need motivation. Two, they need the ability to do it. And three, they need a prompt.

Now think about this in terms of social apps. Think about a notification.

The notification is the prompt.

Opening the app takes just one single tap, so that's the ability to do it.

And the motivation, especially with social apps, is the curiosity about what is waiting for you there.

Some of the people who studied with B.J. Fogg went on to shape the apps we use today.

Mike Krieger, one of the co-founders of Instagram, was a student of his. So was Tristan Harris, who later became one of the loudest critics of this kind of design.

This is not to demonise the man. But the ideas he found and taught were real, and of course, the industry picked them up and made a shitload of money out of them.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: UNPREDICTABLE REWARDS]**

B.J. Fogg, a researcher at Stanford, described a simple model of behaviour. For someone to do something, they need motivation, they need the ability to do it, and they need a prompt.

When it comes to you picking up your phone, or going through social media apps, or being stuck on your phone, the prompt in many cases is the notification.

The ability to do it is just one tap. You just have to tap the app open. It's very easy.

And the motivation is a very important one. It's the curiosity that something on that app is waiting for you.

Now, the most powerful part of all this is the unpredictable reward. And it is the bedrock of so many things.

It's also the bedrock of gambling, for example.

You don't know what you'll get out of it. Sometimes nothing. Sometimes a small win. Sometimes a big win.

And psychologists have known for a long time that rewards you cannot predict tend to make you come back more strongly than rewards you can predict.

If I know that if I do this thing, I will get this, I probably wouldn't come back to it as much as I would if there was an unpredictable reward waiting for me.

And that is exactly how your phone works.

You don't know if there's a new message, a new like on your video, a new video for you to see, or new gist.

That not knowing is exactly why you keep picking up your phone to check.

And there are so many designs put in place to make sure you are constantly on your phone and distracted by it.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: PULL-TO-REFRESH]**

The first of many is pull-to-refresh.

You know that gesture where you drag your feed down and let it go, to see if something new has appeared?

That feature was developed by a developer called Loren Brichter, for a Twitter app he built called Tweetie, around 2008.

And he later said himself that pull-to-refresh is addictive.

It's the unpredictable reward again. When you pull down your feed to refresh, you don't know what's coming, but you know something new might pop up.

So you keep pulling down.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: INFINITE SCROLL AND STOPPING CUES]**

Another design that keeps you stuck on your phone is infinite scroll.

If you don't know what infinite scroll is, it's just the ability to keep scrolling, infinitely.

Before it was designed, websites used to have pages. When you got to the bottom and wanted to see more content, you'd have to click "next page."

Now, that click was a stopping point.

It's a very tiny moment where your brain has the chance to ask, do I actually want to keep going?

Infinite scroll was designed in 2006, by a designer called Aza Raskin. And it removed that moment.

You don't have to click anything. The content just keeps loading, and now there's no natural place or chance for you to stop. There's no cue sent to your brain.

If there was a button to load more, that button would send a cue to my brain. Like, okay, do I want to load more, or do I want to drop my freaking phone?

But that's no longer there.

If you notice, older forms of media had stopping cues.

A book has chapters. A TV show used to end, and you'd have to wait for the next episode, sometimes for a week. A newspaper had a last page.

I remember when I was a kid, and we would buy movies. You'd buy season one, and if you wanted to watch season two, you'd have to go back and buy season two.

Now you don't have to.

Your feed does not have a last page, and it's intentionally designed that way, so you can keep scrolling forever.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: AUTOPLAY AND DEFAULTS]**

Another design that keeps you on your phone is autoplay.

If you notice, on YouTube, when a video ends, you don't have to do anything. The next one starts on its own within a few seconds.

When a video is coming to an end, you have two decisions you can make. You can keep watching, or you can decide to stop watching.

The problem is that with autoplay, you don't have to decide to keep watching. That decision has been made for you.

It's when you want to stop watching that you have to put in effort.

So deciding to stop takes effort, and deciding to continue doesn't take any effort at all.

So you can guess which one happens more. Continuing, of course.

And another thing that powers this is defaults. On YouTube, for example, autoplay is on by default.

And most people never change the settings an app comes with. So it just keeps going like that.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: NOTIFICATIONS AND THE RED BADGE]**

Next up is notifications and red badges.

Most apps show notifications as red badges, and that didn't happen by accident. They were intentionally designed.

A notification is an interruption that someone else decided to send you.

And the colour matters. Facebook's notification icon used to be blue, to match the rest of the site, because Facebook gives that blue vibe.

According to reporting by the Guardian, people barely noticed the blue one. So it was changed to red, because red grabs your attention.

Now, if you look at your phone, almost every app uses a red badge with a number on it.

That red colour and that number create a small feeling in your brain that something is unfinished, and you need to go and clear it. A sense of urgency, if that makes sense.

There's a study from CHI, which is the main conference in my field, human-computer interaction, where researchers had people keep their phone notifications on for one week and off for another week.

In the week the notifications were on, people reported more symptoms of inattention and hyperactivity.

Because that's what notifications are meant to do. Call your attention, and make you feel like there's something urgent waiting.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: LIKES AND SOCIAL APPROVAL]**

Another reason you can't let go of your phone is likes and social approval.

There was a time when likes on your posts didn't exist.

The Like button launched on Facebook in 2009, and it has spread across almost every social media app since then, as a like, or a love, or something of that kind.

And it's also a design that's put in place to keep you coming back.

The like button gives you a small burst of social approval. I make a post, someone sees what I posted, and they like it.

And this goes back to the idea of unpredictable rewards.

When I make a post and drop my phone, I don't know how many likes I'll get, and I don't know how many I've already gotten. So it's an unpredictable reward I'm looking forward to, and it keeps me coming back.

Have you ever made a post on social media and kept going back to check it? You keep going back to see how many likes or reactions it has gotten.

That's intentional design, in a way that keeps you coming back.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: THE CLAIMS ABOUT PHONES AND YOUR BRAIN]**

There's a lot of research, and a lot of claims, about phones, attention and distraction.

I was reading about a study from 2017 that says just having your phone on your desk, even if it's switched off, makes you worse at thinking. They called it brain drain.

But when other researchers repeated that exact experiment, they didn't find the same effect. So I'd take that one with a pinch of salt.

It's a topic that attracts a lot of claims.

---

**[SEGMENT 11: WHAT THE EU FOUND IN 2026]**

I was also reading that in February 2026, the European Commission made a preliminary finding that TikTok's design breaks the EU's Digital Services Act.

When it comes to regulation, I think the EU deserves a global award. Some of their regulations can be overbearing, but some of them are very helpful.

In this finding, the EU named infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications, and TikTok's highly personalised recommendations.

**[ON SCREEN: EU preliminary findings. TikTok, Feb 2026. Instagram and Facebook, July 2026.]**

Then in July, they made the same kind of preliminary finding against Meta, for the addictive design of Instagram and Facebook.

This is one of the first times a regulator has formally said that features like infinite scroll can be a legal problem.

These are not final decisions yet. But because I live in the EU, I'm very curious to see how this all goes.

The fact that regulators are starting to see these designs as a problem is something I really want to see the end of.

---

**[SEGMENT 12: MY OWN RULES]**

So we've gone over the fact that you're addicted to your phone, and that there are intentional features put in place to keep you stuck on it. Infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications.

So what exactly can you do about this?

And this is something I started thinking about at a very early stage in my life.

I remember when I was nineteen, I used to use Instagram a lot. I feel like if I had continued using Instagram at that rate, I probably would have turned out to be one big influencer or something.

But around that time, at nineteen, I just strongly felt that it was very dysfunctional for the life I wanted to live.

And this was at a point when it wasn't even as popular as it is now. I already felt like, nah, this thing is doing things to me that I don't like.

So I left Instagram. I didn't use the app for six years.

These days, my rule for social media is need. This is the rule I keep for myself, and I break it too sometimes.

I should only open Twitter when I need something from there, and my need is usually to post my content.

I should only open Instagram when I need something from there. I should only open TikTok when I need something from there.

And all my notifications are turned off. Even WhatsApp.

If someone is trying to reach me and has my phone number, I'll see it when I open the app.

And this sounds a little bit hypocritical, because I post content on these apps myself, and I want people to watch it.

But it will do nobody any good if we are all stuck on this thing.

---

**[SEGMENT 13: WHAT YOU CAN ACTUALLY TRY]**

So if you want to push back on your phone use, there are a few things you can do that are actually backed up by research.

You can try grayscale. Most phones let you turn your screen black and white in the accessibility settings.

There's a study that shows that when people use grayscale, they spend less time on their phones. You take the colour away, so your phone becomes a little bit less rewarding.

Turn off autoplay on YouTube or Netflix. It puts the stopping point back. After episode one plays, you should get the chance to choose whether to continue.

Turn off the notifications you don't need. Don't turn off the Slack notifications you actually need for work. But turn off the red badges on apps that don't need them.

Move your distracting apps off your home screen.

That one extra swipe it takes to get to the app is also a stopping point. It's a moment where you get to ask yourself if you actually want to open it.

And before you open an app, I know this is a lot of work, but just simply ask yourself.

I'm opening Instagram. What do I need from Instagram right now?

Do I want to reply to someone? Do I want to post something? Or do I just want to scroll?

---

**[SEGMENT 14: YOUR ATTENTION IS AN ASSET]**

Your phone is designed to hold your attention.

Your attention is what pays for a lot of these apps, and the techniques being used to hold it are intentionally crafted. You need to know that.

At least, start from knowing. Knowing will not fix everything.

But the next time you're scrolling for one hour, two hours, I hope you're able to remember that there are people who built these features.

There's a whole team working so hard for you to stay on Instagram, and for you to stay on TikTok.

And I hope you're able to drop it when you want to.

It's not something I've completely figured out either. But I think I use social media way less than the average person, at least from what I've calculated.

But I really, really hope everyone gets into this idea and understands that your attention is an asset.

It is literally being fought over by different corporations.

And you need to take it back, for yourself, for the people who love you, and for the things that are important in your life.

You pick up your phone to do one thing, and twenty minutes later you are still scrolling and the one thing is gone. I am doing my master's in human-computer interaction, and a lot of that was designed.

It starts with money. You do not pay Instagram, TikTok or Facebook. They make money by selling targeted adverts, so the more of your time they hold, the more they earn. In 1971 the economist Herbert Simon wrote that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. When information is everywhere, attention is what runs short, and in a way, your attention becomes the product.

The techniques for holding it were studied. B.J. Fogg ran the Persuasive Technology Lab at Stanford, and in 2007 his students built Facebook apps that reached millions of users in ten weeks. His model of behaviour says you need motivation, the ability and a prompt. A notification is the prompt, opening an app takes one tap, and curiosity supplies the motivation. Mike Krieger, one of Instagram's co-founders, studied with him, and so did Tristan Harris, who later became one of the loudest critics of this kind of design.

The strongest ingredient is the unpredictable reward, the same thing that drives gambling. You do not know whether there is a message, a like or nothing, and that not knowing keeps you checking. Loren Brichter, who invented pull-to-refresh, later said himself that it is addictive. Aza Raskin designed infinite scroll in 2006, and it removed the "next page" click that used to give your brain a moment to stop. Older media were full of those moments. Books had chapters, television made you wait a week, and when I was a child you bought season one of a show and went back to the shop for season two. Autoplay removes another stopping point, so stopping takes effort while continuing takes none. Facebook's notification icon was reportedly switched from blue to red because red grabs attention, and the Like button turned every post into a small lottery you keep checking.

Some claims deserve caution. A famous 2017 study said that a phone merely sitting on a desk makes you worse at thinking, but when other researchers repeated it, they did not find the effect. The design decisions are on firmer ground, and in 2026 the European Commission made preliminary findings that the addictive design of TikTok, Instagram and Facebook breaks the Digital Services Act, naming infinite scroll, autoplay and push notifications.

At nineteen I felt Instagram was dysfunctional for the life I wanted, so I left it, and I did not use it for six years. Today my rule is need. I open these apps when I need something there, usually to post my work, and all my notifications are off. I break the rule sometimes. Grayscale, turning off autoplay and moving apps off the home screen all put a few stopping points back, and before opening an app, it helps to ask what you need from it right now.

Your attention is an asset, and it is being fought over. Take it back, for yourself and for the people who love you.
