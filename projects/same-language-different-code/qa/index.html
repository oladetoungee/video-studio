The big idea

What exactly is the Environment Profile?

It's one page with six rows: connectivity, devices, money rails, identity rails, jurisdiction and forgiveness. You fill it in for where your system was built and for where it's going. Every row that's different is a question: does this change a setting, or a decision?

Isn't this just non-functional requirements?

It sits next to them. Non-functional requirements say what the system must achieve, like 99.9% uptime. The Environment Profile describes the world those targets have to survive in. It tells you whether the targets are realistic, and what it takes to hit them.

What's the difference between a setting and a decision?

A setting lives in a config or translation file: a currency symbol, a date format, the language on the screen. Your architecture doesn't notice. A decision is baked into the shape of the system: your payment states, where your data lives, what happens on a timeout, how people sign in. Those are expensive to change later.

I only build for one country. Does this apply to me?

Yes. Your users' environment is still built into your code. You just can't see it, because you've never left it. Writing it down helps the day you get a new market, a big new customer or a new law, and it helps new people on your team who come from somewhere else.

Who should fill it in, and when?

Ideally someone on the team who has lived there. They know what the network is like, how people pay and what ID they carry. If nobody has, talk to users there or read the local rules. It's one page, so it fits into a normal planning meeting.

Why these six rows? What about accessibility, or language?

These are the rows I had to learn myself, moving between Nigeria and Estonia. The list isn't complete, so add your own. Accessibility is a really good one. I almost added it this morning.

Is this only about Africa versus Europe?

No. Those are my two examples because I've built in both. The same thing happens between any two environments: the US and the EU, a big city and the countryside, even two customers with different contracts.

What does “forgiveness” mean?

What users have learned to tolerate, and what can't be undone. A wrong car wash check can be fixed, so we can sample one in five. A wrong payment can't, so we check every one. Forgiveness tells you where to spend your engineering.

Uber

Why did Uber say no to React Native and Flutter? Are they bad?

They're not bad. At the time, the framework library alone was six to eight megabytes, and Uber's whole budget was five. It was a size decision for that environment, not a judgement on the frameworks.

Are those frameworks smaller today?

Yes. Flutter's own docs now say a few megabytes compressed. But the lesson stays the same: you measure against your own users' budget, not against what's normal elsewhere.

So what did Uber build Uber Lite with?

A native Android app, built on Uber's own architecture called RIBs. Their blog post doesn't say which language. To stay small, they used ProGuard and dropped parts of their usual setup, like their plugin framework.

What is Year Class?

Facebook's way of scoring a phone by the year it would have been a high-end phone. So a “Year Class 2015” phone performs like a top phone from 2015. Uber used it to see what their riders actually held, instead of guessing.

Is under 5 MB still realistic today?

The exact number matters less than having one. Uber's real lesson was: pick a budget from your users' reality, then make the build fail in CI when you go over it, so the app can't quietly grow one dependency at a time.

What is Backends for Frontends?

A thin backend layer shaped for one app. The phone asks one question, and that layer talks to all the other services inside the data centre, where the network is fast. So the phone pays for one round trip instead of five. Sam Newman gave the pattern its name.

Why a 1,200-byte budget and not 1,500?

One packet on Ethernet carries up to 1,500 bytes, but the headers use some of that space. A body budget of about 1,200 bytes leaves room for them, so the whole response still fits in one packet.

Why landmarks instead of addresses?

In many cities, people give directions by landmark, and typing a full address on a slow phone is painful. So Uber Lite let riders pick points of interest instead.

USSD

What is USSD, in simple words?

You dial a short code like *123#, and a small text menu pops up. It works on any phone with a SIM, even with no data plan. The mobile operator passes each step to your server, usually as a normal web request.

Do people really still use it?

Every day, in Nigeria, Kenya, Rwanda and many other places, for airtime, banking and mobile money. In 2025 only 36% of people in Africa used the internet, so USSD reaches the people an app can't.

How long does a session last?

Each operator sets its own limit, and it's short. So you design as if the session can drop at any moment.

How does the server know it's the same person when they dial again?

The operator sends the phone number with every request. So the first thing the server does is look for a recent transaction from that number, and show its status: “Your transfer of 5,000 naira is being processed.”

Where can I read more?

Werner Vogels wrote a post in 2025 called “What is USSD (and who cares)?”. The interface is from the 1990s, but behind it is a modern cloud system.

Data and the law

Residency, localisation, sovereignty: what's the difference?

Residency is where your data is stored. Localisation is a law that forces data to stay inside a country. Sovereignty is about whose laws apply to the data, and who can force you to hand it over. Your data can sit in Germany and still be reachable by a foreign government, if the company running the servers falls under that government's laws.

From a cut slide (three words)

Doesn't GDPR say data has to stay in the EU?

Not exactly. GDPR protects personal data and allows it to leave the EU, but only with safeguards, like an adequacy decision or standard contract clauses. India's rule for payment data was stricter: store it only in India.

What happened to Mastercard in the end?

The Reserve Bank of India stopped Mastercard from taking new customers in July 2021. It lifted that in June 2022, once it was satisfied Mastercard had complied. So almost a year without growth in one of its biggest markets.

What is the “atom” again?

The unit of data that always lives together, in one region. For a B2B product, it's usually the company. Everything about one company lives in one region, so no normal query needs data from two places, and the law is satisfied by design.

On top, there's a small global layer that knows which region each company lives in. Just IDs and regions, no personal data.

How does a request find the right region?

Every request carries the company's ID, and a gateway sends it to the region that holds that company.

What about data that isn't about one customer, like a product catalogue?

Not everything has to be regional. You decide dataset by dataset what stays regional and what can be global, and you write that choice down. The Volvo Cars talk here at Øredev last year covers exactly this.

Isn't running many regions expensive?

Yes. More regions means more infrastructure, more deployments and more to monitor. That's why you decide per dataset, and go multi-region only where a law or a promise to a customer asks for it.

What about sending data to AI APIs?

That counts too. Data leaks through side doors: logs, error trackers, analytics, backups, support tools, and now AI APIs. If customer data goes to a model, where that model runs belongs in your jurisdiction row. At work, when we looked at using an AI model to check documents like driving licences, the first question was what the law and our terms allow.

Money

What's the difference between a push and a pull payment?

With a card, the merchant pulls money using your card details, and you can dispute it with a chargeback. With a push payment, like a bank transfer or Pix, your own bank sends the money after you approve it. Once it's gone, it's usually gone.

How does that change the code?

Your payment needs more states. With a push, the customer leaves your app to approve it and may never come back, so you need “waiting for the customer”. The result arrives later, through a webhook. A cash voucher like OXXO can stay pending for days, so it needs an expiry. And refunding an instant transfer means sending a brand new payment the other way.

From a cut slide (payment state machine)

How do you stop someone being charged twice when they retry?

Make the payment safe to repeat. Create the transaction with its own ID the moment the user confirms. If the same request comes in again, return the result you already have instead of charging again. That's what the USSD example does.

Identity

So how would you protect against SIM swap?

Put the defences in the architecture. Tie each account to the user's own phone, so a new phone has to prove itself first. Ask for stronger proof before risky actions, like changing where payouts go. And slow things down right after a SIM change.

From a cut slide (defences)

How would one product support Smart-ID and phone numbers?

Make identity pluggable. Your system talks to one interface that answers two questions: who is this, and how sure are we? The EU's eIDAS rules give the levels: low, substantial and high. Then your payment code asks “is the assurance high enough?”, never “did they use Smart-ID?”.

But every method brings its own sign-up, recovery and support work, so add one only when a market really needs it.

From cut slides (identity interface and its cost)

What is Smart-ID?

An app for proving who you are, used in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania. The website shows a short code, your phone shows the same code, and you confirm with a PIN. So a bank or a startup gets a strong answer to “who is this?” without building identity checks themselves.

Why is the Estonian ID code personal data?

It isn't random. It contains your date of birth, and the first digit tells your gender and the century you were born in. So you treat it as personal data, not just an ID.

Doesn't Nigeria have a national ID?

Yes, the NIN, and SIM cards have to be linked to it. Banks use the BVN, the Bank Verification Number. But for most everyday apps, the phone number is still what people sign up with.

Testing

Are there alternatives to Toxiproxy?

Yes. Browser developer tools can slow the network down, the Android emulator can simulate a slow network, and on Linux, a tool called tc netem can add delay and packet loss. Toxiproxy is nice because it runs inside your automated tests.

What do Open Policy Agent and Checkov do?

They check your infrastructure code before it's deployed. You write a rule like “regional data can only be stored in these regions”, and the build fails when someone breaks it. Checkov scans infrastructure files like Terraform. Open Policy Agent is a general engine for rules like these.

How do I get my team or my manager to care?

Start small: one row, one budget, one test in CI. Then show one real failure from your users' environment. A number like Uber's “56% of riders on old phones” usually does the convincing for you.
