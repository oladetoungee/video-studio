**How this script is built:** your dictation, in your order, cleaned up for the teleprompter. Every segment names its own topic in the first line or two, so a viewer who lands there, or a clip cut from it, understands it. About 2,050 spoken words, so roughly 13 to 14 minutes.

**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

There's so much difference between studying in Nigeria and studying in Europe.

I feel like the educational system is just so different.

I have a video on this, "nobody warned me about studying in Europe as a Nigerian", and I thought I would do a follow-up.

Of course, there are loads of differences, and based on your own experience, you'll find different differences.

But I thought I would go over it again based on my experience here, how you're graded, deadlines and stuff like that.

And by Europe, I mean Estonia, where I am, because I'm not schooling in all of Europe.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: THE LECTURER-STUDENT RELATIONSHIP]**

First of all, I think the lecturer-student relationship is reasonably different between Nigeria and Europe.

Respect shows up in different ways.

The way you respect your lecturers or your professors in Nigeria is very different from the way respect is done here.

In Nigeria, you do not call your lecturer by their first name.

Here in Estonia, everyone is on a first-name basis, and that includes your lecturers.

Well, that's just a small one.

**[~1:00 BEAT: the first difference has landed. Optional line to carry them on:]**

The bigger one is how you're graded.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: HOW YOU'RE ASSESSED]**

How you're assessed is also different between Nigeria and Europe, right?

In a lot of Nigerian universities, the final exam carries most of your grade.

Sometimes the final exam is 70 percent of your grade, and your continuous assessment is maybe 30 percent.

**[ON SCREEN: University of Nigeria, Nsukka (example): 30% continuous assessment + 70% exam]**

So the assignments and your tests, they matter, but the exam at the end really decides a lot of things.

And that's why most of us who school in Nigeria tend to prepare for that big exam day.

But here, it is different.

Work is spread across the whole semester, right?

So instead of the grade relying heavily on the exam, the course might be graded on a project, an essay, a presentation or group work, and that can literally be most of your grade.

Or even all of it.

Group work is such a common thing here, at least in my programme.

And in my school, the lecturer lets you know how you'll be assessed before the course even really starts.

In the first class, the lecturer walks you through the course programme, how you're going to be assessed and all of that.

So you really have to start working from week one.

Because by the final weeks, which are the exam weeks, most of your grade is already decided.

**[~3:00 BEAT: optional line]**

And if you're wondering what happens when you fail or miss an exam here, that's next.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHEN YOU FAIL OR MISS AN EXAM]**

What happens when you fail or miss an exam is also different in Nigeria and in Estonia, where I am.

In a lot of Nigerian universities, if you fail a course, it becomes a carryover.

So you register for it again and write it in a later session.

At Tallinn University, where I'm schooling, it works like this.

A main exam is usually given two dates, plus one retake date.

You register for the exam yourself in the study information system, right?

And if you register and you don't show up, and you didn't cancel, you're marked "non-appeared", and you lose one attempt.

If you fail the main exam, you can retake it once, on the retake date.

And if you still don't pass, you just have to take the whole course again.

**[ON SCREEN: Tallinn University: 2 main exam dates + 1 retake date. Register yourself. Cancel at least 24 hours before. No-show without cancelling = "non-appeared", one attempt gone. Fail the retake = take the course again]**

So if you can't make it for an exam, cancel your registration before the deadline, which is 24 hours before the exam, so you don't lose that attempt.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: GRADING: NIGERIAN CGPA VS ESTONIAN A TO F]**

Grading is another big difference between Nigeria and Europe, because the two systems look alike, but they work differently.

In Nigeria, most universities use a five-point scale.

A is 5 points, B is 4 points, and it goes down from there.

All your grades add up to your CGPA, and your CGPA decides your class of degree.

First class is 4.5 and above, second class upper is 3.5 and above, and all of that.

**[ON SCREEN: Nigeria (UNN example): A = 70 to 100 = 5 points. First class = CGPA 4.50 and above. Second class upper = 3.50 and above]**

In Estonia, you also get a letter. A, B, C, D, E or F.

And each letter has a number. A is 5, B is 4, and downward like that.

So far, it sounds the same.

The difference is what it takes to get each letter.

In Nigeria, an A usually starts at 70.

In Estonia, the education agency, Harno, lists an A as 91 to 100 percent.

So with 70, you don't get an A. On that scale, 70 is a D.

And at my school, each course also sets out its own criteria for each grade, so read them.

**[ON SCREEN: Harno's scale: A 91-100%, B 81-90%, C 71-80%, D 61-70%, E 51-60%, F 0-50% (fail)]**

And some courses here don't even get a grade. They're just pass or fail.

I'm not sure pass or fail courses are that popular in Nigeria.

But here, pass or fail courses are very normal.

I don't think there's been a semester that I didn't have a pass or fail course.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: CREDITS AND ECTS]**

Credits are another thing that works differently between Nigeria and Europe.

Your CGPA in Nigeria is weighted by credit units.

Your average here is weighted by credits too.

And the credits here have a name you see everywhere when you're schooling in Europe.

They're called ECTS.

The European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System is how universities across Europe count your work, so your credits can move between countries.

For example, I did two Erasmus programmes, one in Portugal and one in Cyprus.

The credits I got from those short programmes, I was able to transfer back to my university.

And there's also the time system, right?

A full-time year is 60 ECTS.

In Estonia, one credit is 26 hours of work, and that includes classes, reading and assignments.

So a six-credit course is about 156 hours of work, right?

**[ON SCREEN: 1 ECTS in Estonia = 26 hours. 6 ECTS = about 156 hours. 60 ECTS = one full-time year]**

**[~6:00 BEAT: optional line]**

And most of those hours are not in a classroom, which changes what class feels like.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: THE CLASSROOM AND INDEPENDENT STUDY]**

Another difference between studying in Nigeria and Europe is the classroom setup.

In a lot of Nigerian universities, especially in the big general courses, classes can be very large, and the lecturer does most of the talking.

You listen, you take notes, and you read those notes for the exam.

Here, as far as I can see, the classes are smaller, and you're also expected to participate.

And then there's something called independent study.

In Estonia, one credit is 26 hours of your work, and most of those hours are not in a classroom.

They are on you.

So nobody checks if you did the reading.

You just find out yourself.

It shows in the way you understand the course, the way you discuss, and all of that.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: SCHOOL LIFE ONLINE]**

Studying in Europe also means a lot of your school life happens online.

You still go to class, but so many things are online, and there isn't much submitting on paper or anything like that.

For example, at Tallinn University, we have some systems that we use.

We have the study information system, everybody calls it ÕIS, where you register for your courses and your exams, and you see your results.

You also have Moodle, where your course can happen.

I think most of the lecturers in my programme prefer Google Classroom.

And there are standards.

Even when you're writing your thesis, there are standards in terms of deadlines on the side of students, and there are standards on the side of the lecturers too.

For example, lecturers have seven days to put exam results in the system, or ten days if more than 50 students registered.

**[ON SCREEN: Tallinn University: results in the system within 7 days (10 days if more than 50 students registered). Contest a result within 10 working days]**

So you don't have to anticipate or guess. Things are standardised on both sides, the student side and the lecturer side.

When your results come out, you can get feedback.

In fact, you can even contest your grade if you have enough evidence, and you have 10 working days to do that.

In Nigeria, a lot of universities also have online portals, so all of this won't be new to you if you move abroad.

The basic thing is, as a student, turn on the notifications for all those systems.

Google Classroom, Moodle, this and that, turn on the notifications.

**[~9:00 BEAT: optional line]**

And in a bit, I'll tell you why I gave up on my degree at LAUTECH.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: CAMPUS LIFE]**

Campus life, I feel like campus life is very different between Nigeria and Europe.

Maybe it's because I'm doing my master's and not a bachelor's.

But I feel like in Nigeria, a lot of your life is just embedded in campus, because your university is big, and usually far from where you live, or far from the city.

A lot of Nigerian universities are kind of like small towns.

There's the hostel, there are markets, there's a church, there are so many things inside.

Even getting from your hostel to your faculty may mean taking a shuttle inside the university.

But here, it is different.

My university is right here in the city centre, so it's a normal city commute.

Because the campus is not as huge, the city is where student life happens.

And it's even more different at master's level, because you probably have work and all of that.

What I would just say is that your social life won't come to you the way it does on a Nigerian campus.

On a Nigerian campus, you get entangled in social life automatically, because you find yourself there.

Here, you have to go out and find your social life yourself, in your class, at student events, in the city and all of that.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: STRIKES AND THE ACADEMIC CALENDAR]**

Another big difference between Nigeria and Europe is how predictable the academic calendar is.

If you've been to a Nigerian public university, you know about ASUU strikes.

ASUU is the union for university lecturers, and in 2022, their national strike lasted eight months, from February to October.

I used to be a student of LAUTECH, Ladoke Akintola University of Technology in Ogbomoso.

And the strikes were one of the reasons why I gave up on pursuing my degree at LAUTECH.

It felt like it was always on strike.

And it wasn't only the national ASUU strikes. LAUTECH was owned by two states, Oyo and Osun, and when the money ran short, the lecturers there went on strike over unpaid salaries.

Between 2016 and 2018, the school was shut or on strike for months at a time.

**[ON SCREEN: LAUTECH: jointly owned by Oyo and Osun states until 2020. Shut for long stretches in 2016 and 2017, another long strike in 2018 (BellaNaija, The Will, ICIR)]**

Of course, lecturers aren't intentionally being wicked.

With strikes, they are fighting for their own pay and for funding.

As much as Nigerian students complain about learning in hard conditions, I think a lot of Nigerian lecturers also teach in really hard conditions.

It goes both ways.

So the academic calendar in Nigeria might not be as reliable.

Here, the academic calendar is published ahead of time.

At my school, this year's calendar was approved in March, five months before the semester started in August.

So you can plan your year around it. You plan your job, you plan your travel, you plan your holiday.

So if you're here, you want to take advantage of this as much as you can.

Plan your thesis, plan your internship, plan your job search.

This is a good advantage.

---

**[SEGMENT 11: WHAT A NIGERIAN EDUCATION GIVES YOU]**

I also think that schooling in Nigeria, and I've said this in a video before, gives you so much advantage if you use it well.

They are two different systems, but there's a lot of advantage you can get if you schooled in Nigeria before.

A lot of us that had our foundational education in Nigeria are good at handling a lot of material under pressure.

It's what our exams trained us to do.

And I think we are resourceful. If something is not working, we find another way, right?

And if you're here, that habit also helps.

I think the respect we're raised with still works here. It just shows in a different way.

For example, being respectful to your lecturer here might look like being prepared, keeping your deadlines and all of that.

Starting your work early, asking for help.

You can ask for help. You can really ask for help.

So if you studied in Nigeria and you're moving to Europe to study, you already have a lot of advantages that you're bringing in from Nigeria.

I can guarantee you that you have a lot, if you don't throw them away.

Don't treat your education from Nigeria as something you just forget about.

I promise you, it's gonna help you if you know how to use it well.

In Nigeria, you do not call your lecturer by their first name, and in Estonia everyone is on a first-name basis, lecturers included.

The bigger difference is how you're assessed. In a lot of Nigerian universities, the final exam carries most of your grade. The University of Nigeria, Nsukka, for example, gives 30 percent to continuous assessment and 70 percent to the exam, and that's why most of us prepare for that one big day. Here, work is spread across the whole semester. A course might be graded on a project, an essay, a presentation or group work, and that can be most of your grade, or all of it. In my school, the lecturer walks you through how you'll be assessed in the first class, so you really have to start working from week one.

Failing or missing an exam works differently too. In a lot of Nigerian universities, a failed course becomes a carryover that you write in a later session. At Tallinn University, a main exam is usually given two dates and one retake date, and you register for it yourself in the study information system. If you register and don't show up without cancelling, you're marked "non-appeared" and you lose an attempt. If you fail the retake, you take the whole course again. So if you can't make it, cancel at least 24 hours before.

Grading looks the same and works differently. Most Nigerian universities use a five-point scale where A is 5, and your CGPA decides your class of degree, with first class at 4.5 and above. Estonia also uses letters, A to F, and A is also 5. The difference is what it takes to get each letter. In Nigeria an A usually starts at 70, and Estonia's education agency, Harno, lists an A as 91 to 100 percent, so 70 is a D on that scale. At my school, each course also sets out its own criteria. Some courses don't get a letter at all and are just pass or fail, and I don't think there's been a semester that I didn't have one.

Your average here is weighted by credits, the same way your CGPA is weighted by credit units at home. In Europe the credits are called ECTS, the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System, and they let your credits move between countries. I did two short Erasmus programmes, one in Portugal and one in Cyprus, and I transferred those credits back to my university. A full-time year is 60 ECTS, and in Estonia one credit is 26 hours of work, so a six-credit course is about 156 hours. Most of those hours are not in a classroom. Classes here are smaller and you're expected to participate, and nobody checks if you did the reading, but it shows in the way you understand the course and the way you discuss.

A lot of school life also happens online. At my university the study information system, ÕIS, is where you register and see your results, and courses run on Moodle, though most of my lecturers prefer Google Classroom. There are deadlines on both sides. Lecturers have seven days to put exam results in the system, or ten if more than 50 students registered, and you can contest a grade within 10 working days if you have enough evidence. Campus life is the opposite story. A lot of Nigerian universities are like small towns, with hostels, markets and churches inside them, so you get pulled into social life just by being there. My university is in the city centre, and your social life won't come to you. You have to go and find it.

The difference that changes your whole plan is the calendar. I used to be a student of LAUTECH, and the strikes were one of the reasons I gave up on pursuing my degree there. It felt like it was always on strike. LAUTECH was jointly owned by Oyo and Osun states, and when the money ran short, lecturers went on strike over unpaid salaries, on top of the national ASUU strikes. Lecturers aren't being wicked. They're fighting for their pay and for funding, and a lot of them teach in really hard conditions. Here, my school's calendar for this year was approved in March, five months before the semester started, so you can plan your thesis, your internship and your job search around it.

And I still think schooling in Nigeria gives you so much advantage if you use it well. A lot of us are good at handling a lot of material under pressure, because it's what our exams trained us to do. We're resourceful, and if something isn't working, we find another way. The respect we're raised with still works here. It just looks like being prepared, keeping your deadlines, starting early and asking for help. Don't treat your education from Nigeria as something you just forget about. I promise you, it's going to help you if you know how to use it well.
