If you're looking for cheap countries in Europe to study in, someone has probably told you that Germany is free. For tuition at most public universities, it is. But you also have the whole proof of funds thing. So when I say a country is cheap, I mean two numbers. The first is the tuition fee. The second is the proof of funds, which is the money you have to show the embassy or the immigration office to prove you can live and sustain yourself in the country while you study. Every country sets its own amount, and I use that official amount as a baseline for the cost of living. Three more things change the real cost: the language the programme is taught in, rent in your city, and whether you're allowed to work. Everything here is as of October 2026, and a lot of it changes every year, so please check the official pages yourself before you decide on anything.

Germany first. Most public universities don't charge international students tuition, and you pay a semester contribution instead. The exceptions are Baden-Württemberg, where Heidelberg University says non-EU students pay 1,500 euros per semester, and the Technical University of Munich, which has charged new non-EU students since winter 2024/25 and lists most master's at 4,000 or 6,000 euros per semester, rising for new students from winter 2027/28. For the visa, you show 992 euros for every month, which is 11,904 euros for a year, and most people put it in a blocked account that only lets them take out a fixed amount each month. Germany is planning to raise its student support rate in 2027, and the blocked account amount usually follows it, so check it before you open yours. Make it in Germany says students from outside the EU can work 140 full days or 280 half days a year.

France charges students from outside the EU differentiated fees, which for 2026 to 2027 are 2,902 euros a year for a bachelor's and 3,950 euros for a master's. Universities used to waive them for a lot of international students, but Decree 2026-385 caps how many they can exempt, at 25% for 2027 to 2028 according to the Ministry of Higher Education. The bigger change is the proof of funds. It was 615 euros a month, and from August 2026 it's 47% of the French minimum wage, which Université Grenoble Alpes gives as about 878 euros a month, or about 10,530 euros a year. Add that to the master's fee and France comes to about 14,480 euros for the first year, about 2,500 euros more than Germany. France's living-cost number is still lower than Germany's, but its tuition is higher, so do the maths for your own case.

Italy works differently. At many state universities, what you pay can depend on your family's income, which international students declare with a document called the ISEE parificato, and each university sets its own rules and bands. The Italian Embassy in Abidjan lists the study visa minimum for 2026 to 2027 as 10,179.85 euros a year, plus money for your ticket home. Applications open early too. The University of Padua opened its 2027 to 2028 applications in September 2026, and its first window closes on 15 November 2026.

Austria has one of the lowest tuition fees I looked at, 726.72 euros per semester for students from outside the EU plus a small student union fee, so about 1,500 euros a year. It keeps a list of countries whose citizens don't pay tuition at all, and the list includes Benin, Ethiopia, Senegal, Togo and Uganda, but not Nigeria. The proof of funds depends on your age. According to the OeAD, for 2026 it's 722.58 euros a month if you're under 24 and 1,308.39 euros a month from 24, shown for twelve months in advance. If you're under 24, Austria is one of the cheapest. If you're 24 or older, which a lot of master's applicants are, its proof of funds is one of the highest I looked at.

In the Czech Republic, Study in Czechia says public universities offer tuition-free programmes in Czech regardless of nationality, while programmes in English charge fees that each university sets. The proof of funds is a formula based on the official minimum cost of living, and the Czech Embassy in Addis Ababa gives 115,810 Czech crowns for a 12-month stay. Poland's proof of funds is low on paper, at 776 zloty a month plus your rent and 2,500 zloty for a ticket home, according to the Wielkopolska Voivodeship Office.

I'm doing my master's in Estonia, at Tallinn University. My first choice was the UK, but the UK was just too expensive. Tallinn University lists most of its master's programmes for the 2027 intake at between 4,000 and 5,200 euros a year. I'm paying 6,000 euros a year, and my programme, Human-Computer Interaction, is one of the most expensive. For new students from outside the EU it now costs 7,000. I have friends in my school paying about two-thirds of what I pay, so check the programme, not only the country. The Police and Border Guard Board's student guide says you need at least 880 euros a month in 2026, and you can work alongside your studies as long as they come first. I work full-time alongside my master's.

Norway has charged students from outside the EU, the EEA and Switzerland since autumn 2023, and at NHH a master's for 2026 starters is 204,000 Norwegian kroner a year, with UDI asking for 170,368 kroner for living costs on top. The University of Eastern Finland charges 10,000 to 12,000 euros a year for its master's in English, and Migri wants 9,600 euros in your account for a year of study. Both have scholarships, and some of them cover tuition, but without one, they're the expensive end of this list. All of these are starting numbers from someone observing, not applying, so use them to decide where to start researching. And don't forget rent. If you're schooling in a city like Paris, rent is going to choke you.
