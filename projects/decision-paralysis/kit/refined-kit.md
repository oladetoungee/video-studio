And you watch three different videos on which language app is best for you to learn French.

**[SEGMENT 3 · Maximizers and satisficers]**

Some people get stuck on decisions more than others, and researchers have a name for these two sides.

Maximizers and satisficers. Try to check which one you are. **[PHRASE CARD: Maximizer / Satisficer]**

A satisficer looks until they find something good enough, and then they stop.

A maximizer wants the best possible option, so they keep looking.

So say you want to buy a phone case. **[HOLD UP A PHONE CASE]**

The satisficer wants a black phone case that fits, that is under 20 euros, and they buy the first one that ticks all of those boxes.

A maximizer, however, will read every single review and check different shops, and then eventually buy one.

And then they see a better one the next week, and then they feel bad about it.

If you're like me, you sometimes just want to do everything.

And wanting to do every single thing the best way is a lot of load to carry.

So I would be a maximizer about a few things that matter to me, but I'm learning to be a satisficer about the rest.

**[SEGMENT 5 · Is decision paralysis the same as decision fatigue?]** *(everything after your questions is from the earlier kit, see 0h question 3)*

Is decision paralysis the same as decision fatigue?

A lot of people blame decision paralysis on decision fatigue.

Decision fatigue is the idea that making a lot of decisions wears you out.

So let's just talk about it. Is it the same thing? Are they connected? Are you stuck because you've used up your decision energy?

I don't think they're the same thing.

Decision fatigue is about being tired after making a lot of decisions.

Decision paralysis is being stuck on a decision, so you don't make it at all.

But I do think they can be connected.

Say you've had a long day at work, and you've been making small decisions since morning.

Then at night, you sit down to decide if you should start learning French, and it feels a lot heavier than it should.

So I wouldn't say you're stuck just because you've used up your decision energy for the day.

### 6f. Essay (in full; her kit's essay, kept)
**Title:** When You Want to Do Everything and End Up Doing Nothing
**Subtitle:** What decision paralysis is, the four questions I ask when I'm stuck, and how I break the loop.
**SEO title:** I Want to Do Everything So I Do Nothing: Decision Paralysis

You literally want to do everything, so you end up doing nothing, and it becomes a loop you have to force yourself to break out of. Decision paralysis is when you have a decision to make and you get so stuck thinking about it that you don't decide at all. Some people call it analysis paralysis. Say you want to start a YouTube channel, learn French, get fit and finally build that side project. You open ten tabs comparing cameras, you watch three videos on which language app is best, and you check gym prices. At the end of the week you haven't started any of it, you feel bad about that, and next week the list is even longer.

There's an old fable, one of Aesop's, usually called The Fox and the Cat. The fox brags that he has hundreds of ways to escape when the hunters' dogs come, and the cat says she only has one. When the dogs come, the cat runs straight up a tree. The fox is still deciding which of his ways to use, and the dogs catch him. I find it interesting that the one with the most options was the one who got caught. If you stay in the loop long enough, doing nothing slowly becomes your habit, and you might start to think you're lazy, even though it didn't start as laziness.

Some people get stuck more than others, and there are names for the two sides. A satisficer looks until they find something good enough and then stops. A maximizer wants the best possible option, so they keep looking. If you're buying a phone case, the satisficer wants a black one that fits and costs under 20 euros, and buys the first one that ticks those boxes. The maximizer reads every review, checks different shops, buys one, and then sees a better one the next week and feels bad about it. If you're like me and you sometimes want to do everything, wanting to do every single thing the best way is a lot to carry. So I would be a maximizer about a few things that matter to me, and I'm learning to be a satisficer about the rest.

When you're stuck on a decision, doing nothing feels like a safe choice. If you start a business and it fails, it feels like your fault. If you never start it, nothing happened, and it doesn't feel like anybody's fault. That safe feeling is part of what keeps the loop going.

A lot of people blame all of this on decision fatigue, the idea that making a lot of decisions wears you out. I don't think they're the same thing. Decision fatigue is about being tired, and decision paralysis is about being stuck. They can be connected, because after a long day of small decisions, a big one at night feels heavier than it should. But you can also wake up rested on a Saturday morning, with nothing else to decide, and still be stuck on that same decision. People argue a lot about how much decision fatigue really explains, so I don't lean on it too much. I just try to make my big decisions when I'm rested.

When I'm stuck, I always try to get to the results, to how helpful the decision would be. I think we often stall on a decision because it sounds like a good idea, but we're not convinced it will really help, and we might not admit that to ourselves. So I use four questions I read in a Ben Carson book when I was a teenager. The book is Take the Risk, and he calls it a best/worst analysis. What is the best thing that can happen if I do this? What is the worst thing that can happen if I do this? What is the best thing that can happen if I don't do it? What is the worst thing that can happen if I don't do it? For me, it forces me to face the size and the impact of the decision, whether it will matter in a good way, a bad way, or not much at all, and it always helps me cut down the options.

Then I have to start. The first thing is to pick one thing and park the rest on a list. I think of myself as a multipotentialite, someone with a lot of interests, and the switches I've made in my life were never me giving up on things. But if I want to start four new things this month, no amount of planning will help me, because I'm already too busy for four new things. So I pick one and look at the rest later.

The second thing is to find the smallest next action, which David Allen in Getting Things Done calls "the next physical, visible activity". When I wanted to learn how to ride a bike, the usual me would have wanted to buy a bike first. The smallest thing I could do was borrow my neighbour's bike. In the same way, "learn French" is too big an action, and "download an app to learn French and do lesson one" is something you can do now. The third thing is to attach it to a time and a place. I told myself I was going to learn to ride that week, and I put it up on Facebook, telling a lot of people I didn't know that I needed to learn to ride a bike that week. That's an implementation intention, like "7pm on Tuesday, I will do lesson one." Your decision paralysis loop will not break on its own. You have to force it to break by doing one small thing. I'd love to know what decision you've been stuck on, and what your smallest next action would be.
