```
Ghana just launched Marrify, a public platform where you can search and verify marriages, divorces and licensed marriage officials. It launched in Accra on 28 August 2026 with the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection, and it pulls records from the Registrar-General's Department, the courts, local assemblies and the Ghana Gazette, with divorce records reportedly going back as far as 1886. You can even check whether a church, a pastor or a marriage venue is licensed to conduct a marriage.

My first reaction as an African? Wow. Centralised data. Finally, finally.

I've been living in Estonia for about a year, and people always call it the digital hub of Europe. I'm not going to lie, it's so real. Everything here is online: your health records, businesses, you can get married online, you can divorce online, and coming from Nigeria, the way it all syncs blew my mind. So when I see Ghana building in this direction, my first instinct is: yes please, more of this. One reliable record instead of pieces of paper everywhere.

But then I read TechCabal's piece on the drawbacks. A divorce record is a personal thing about someone's life, and now it's searchable by anyone who has your name. I like open data, but a business that takes public money is not the same as somebody's divorce.

In this video I think out loud about:
- consent: divorce papers sitting in a courthouse somewhere in 2003, vs anyone checking them on their phone in three seconds
- data minimisation: if the question is "is this person free to marry?", the answer can be yes or no. No spouse's name, no dates, no history
- access levels: maybe individuals see one level and certain institutions see more
- why technology is not neutral: in Estonia a divorce record could just be administrative, nobody cares. In an African context, divorce, especially for women, is still pretty stigmatised
- reciprocity: in Estonia transparency runs both ways. If someone looks up my marriage record, do I get to see that they looked?

I really want African countries building this kind of infrastructure. I just think that as people, we still need to probe it a little and question it.

If you're Ghanaian, I'd really love to hear how this feels from where you are.
```
