teraflop 5 days ago | root | parent | next [–]

> I don’t think you can get the SCT from a log without the log publishing it?

Well you can, but only if the log cooperates.

That is, the SCT is merely a promise to add the certificate to the log by some particular deadline. Nothing technically stops the log from disregarding that promise. But if it does fail to log the certificate, the SCT itself is proof of its misbehavior.

And it's not enough to subvert a single log, because browsers require SCTs from multiple distinct operators in order to consider a certificate valid.

tialaramex 4 days ago | root | parent | next [–]

The algorithms don't care because it doesn't make sense to care, the same reason a "smallest non-interesting number" wouldn't make sense - now that number is interesting precisely because it was cited as the smallest non-interesting number.

However practical applications do care, and so e.g. the "Debian Weak Keys" are a relatively small set of keys which would have been generated if you used a particular patched OpenSSL to generate "random" key pairs after a mistake patched out most of the randomness. Many of them were used by Debian sysadmins particularly in the window between the patch and the discovery - and so all CAs are expected to check that you're not trying to get a certificate for the public half of one of those pairs.

There's nothing "bad" about those keys except that well, we know some people used them so bad guys will try the private halves of those keys just in case. Likewise there are a few apps which shipped with a baked in "default" private key, and all the paperwork to apply for a certificate but obviously a user may not realise that if they just fill it out with their name and details, they're now vulnerable to attack by literally any other user of that software. They were supposed to generate a random key pair and re-generate the paperwork for the resulting random values. If they don't realise we need the CA to say "Nope, that's a bad idea, not issuing" even though these keys aren't special in any other way than they're given to every single user of that software.

One-liner to remove eMudhra from trust chain:

Mac OS

sudo security find-certificate -c "eMudhra" -p /Library/Keychains/System.keychain > /tmp/emudhra.pem && sudo security add-trusted-cert -d -r deny -k /Library/Keychains/System.keychain /tmp/emudhra.pem

Ubuntu Linux

sudo sed -i '/eMudhra/s/^/!/' /etc/ca-certificates.conf && sudo update-ca-certificates --fresh

Windows Powershell

Get-ChildItem -Path Cert:\LocalMachine\Root, Cert:\LocalMachine\CA | Where-Object { $_.Subject -like "eMudhra" } | ForEach-Object { Move-Item -Path $_.PSPath -Destination Cert:\LocalMachine\Disallowed -Force }

Windows cmd

certutil -store CA "eMudhra" | findstr "Serial" > nul && certutil -exportstore CA "eMudhra" %temp%\emudhra.p7b && certutil -addstore -f "Disallowed" %temp%\emudhra.p7b

Browsers will demand for modern certificates that they're accompanied by SCTs which indicate the certificates have been logged publicly. Certificates signed locally by an attacker aren't and the revoked CA certs should be immediately rejected by the CT providers.

basilikum 5 days ago | root | parent | next [–]

Slight specification:

When you're using a modern browser that browser will require any certificate to have a certificate transparency log stamp. Certificate Transparency logs are public append only logs of issued certificates.

So you access example.org. Your ISP uses the compromised CA certificate to create and sign a rogue certificate for example.org. It now has to submit this certificate to a public certificate transparency log and gets back a proof for that. Now it can use that rogue certificate and the proof that it's in a CT log to impersonate example.org and MITM your traffic.

CT does not prevent compromise, but it makes it far easier to detect. If the people behind example.org have a look at the CT logs they will see the rogue certificate there that they did not request. This makes rogue certificates and by extension CA compromise detectable. So your ISP cannot just do this forever in secret.

toast0 5 days ago | root | parent | prev | next [–]

You can pin in software you distribute. If you're big enough (top 100 websites mostly), you can ask browsers to pin certs and they may included it in their preloads.

> And what happens when you want to switch?

You have to update your pins and then wait long enough that you don't mind losing users who never update their software. When it's just in software you distribute, you can change hostnames in new versions and use the new CA for new versions and the old CA for old versions...

For web browsers, there's not a reasonable way to make a nicer transition. You have to serve old certs for old pins, and TLS handshakes from browsers don't include enough information to know if the client has an old pin or a new pin; or at least, mostly they don't --- if you get your pins changed and the browser TLS stack changes so the handshake changes after that, maybe you can use that... when sha1 certs were phased out, it was somewhat popular on high volume sites to look at the TLS Client Hello to decide to serve a sha256 cert or a sha1 cert.

DNS CAA only pins issuance, not acceptance; clients can not check if the CA that issued a cert was listed in DNS CAA at the time of issuance, and they do not check if it's listed at time of presentation. I'm pretty sure issuing a cert contrary to a CAA record is a mississuance and would result in sanctions from the CA/Browser forum, but clients would still accept the cert until it's revoked.

There was briefly browser support for site initiated pin on first user (HTTP Public Key Pinning), but it got dropped as mostly no one was using it and it was easy to get wrong and break your site.

hoherd 4 days ago | parent | next [–]

Because the S in IOT stands for Security.

IOT devices famously never get firmware updates, which means most of them will remain vulnerable to this leaked trust chain until the cert expires in 2039. That means you can forge an HTTPS server for any domain that an IOT device connects to and act as if you are the authoritative server. This means intercepting encrypted traffic and being able to decrypt it, or serving payloads to the device from a fake trusted server.

If your TV connects to whatever.lg.com, you can create a certificate for that domain using this intermediate signing cert, and pretend to be that server. That's how https://dv-g3b-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/ was created.

> Web of trust has been working between humans for over a million years.

I'm not sure that's really true. Human social organization has, until around 10,000 years ago, been centered on small groups of people where everyone literally knew each other. The ethnographic studies on non-state complex societies generally tends to show a lot of kinship-based networks that developed, and even in state societies that are facilitating long-distance trade, trade patterns and introduction into new areas is heavily reliant on membership of these kinship groups. State societies generally deemphasize these kinship groups, eventually doing away with them like in modern society, but the mechanisms for developing social trust are based on a very centralized development of the legal system--the government essentially enforces that people have to be trustworthy, or they face the wrath of government otherwise.

Essentially, in short, the idea that humans have regularly been trusting people based on asking their close compatriots who they can vouch for doesn't seem to accurately model how trust develops in societies for most of human history--you either have vouching based on direct observation (in small societies), vouching based on identity (larger non-state societies), or centralized authority vouching for people (state societies, including today).
