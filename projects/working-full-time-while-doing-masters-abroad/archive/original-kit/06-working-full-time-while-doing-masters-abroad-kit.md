## 1. YOUTUBE VIDEO SCRIPT

**How this script is built:** twelve segments. Segments 1 to 3 (about 1:50) are the open, the rules and your goal. Segments 4 to 12 are the nine tips. Every segment names its topic in its first line, so a clip cut from it makes sense on its own.

**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

I have a full-time job as a full-stack software developer here in Estonia, and I'm also doing a full-time master's degree here as well.

I've been doing both since October 2025, and this month makes it one year of working full-time and studying full-time.

In this video, I'll show you how I do both, how I plan my week, and some tips that work, some from my life and some from research.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: FIRST, CHECK IF YOU'RE ALLOWED TO WORK WHERE YOU STUDY]**

First things first, if you're an international student, confirm if international students can work where you're studying.

Where I study, in Estonia, there is no limit on the number of hours.

What they look at is whether you're keeping up with your studies.

**[ON SCREEN: Estonia, residence permit for study: you can work, hours "not limited", but work must not interfere with your studies and you must stay full-time (Police and Border Guard Board, Study in Estonia, October 2026)]**

So what does being a full-time student mean in numbers?

In Estonia, full-time study means around 30 credits, called ECTS, every semester.

At my university, after your first year, you need at least 45.

If you fall below that, you're moved to part-time study, and falling into part-time study can result in your permit being revoked.

**[ON SCREEN: Tallinn University: at least 22.5 ECTS per semester studied, checked every 2 semesters. Below that, part-time, and your study permit is at risk. As of October 2026, check your own school's rules]**

**[~1:00 BEAT: the "can I even do this" question is answered, and her line carries them on]**

So, that being said, if you're in a place where you can work full-time and also study, this video is for you.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WORKING WHILE STUDYING IS THE REAL GHETTO]**

Working while studying, I swear, is the real ghetto.

Once Monday starts for me, it's almost as though it keeps running until Friday, even until Saturday.

So it's not like this video is going to make it completely soft life, no.

When I got this job, I remember setting a very ambitious goal for myself.

I wanted to do work so well that work does not know that school exists, and I wanted to do school so well that school does not know that work exists.

And so far so good, I think I've done that in a reasonable manner.

Yeah, there have been days of almost running mad, but these are some tips that I think can help you.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: TIP 1, UNDERSTAND YOUR CREDITS AND PLAN YOUR SEMESTER FROM DAY ONE]**

Tip number one, if you're working and you're studying, is to understand your credits at the start of every semester, before the semester gets busy.

Know how many credits each course is worth, how many you need this semester, and how many you need by the end of the year.

Most courses, at least in my school, tell you how they're going to assess you at the start, in the first class, in the course outline.

So on that same day, you already have all the information on how the course is going to go.

How many hours of classes it has, and how it's going to be graded.

Take every class, every deadline and every exam date, and get it into your calendar as soon as possible.

And based on that understanding, you get to choose your courses.

Of course, you want to choose courses that you like.

But if you're working full-time, you also want to choose courses whose class commitment won't overwhelm you.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: TIP 2, PLAN YOUR WHOLE DEGREE AND BUDGET MORE TIME]**

Tip number two for working while doing a master's is to plan your whole degree as well, across all your semesters.

One of my goals was to clear all my non-thesis credits this autumn.

In fact, I had most of my non-thesis credits cleared already last semester.

So next semester, I really just have my thesis to hammer on.

And when you're planning big things, like your thesis, a professional placement or an internship, give them more time than you think they need.

I feel like a lot of us students underestimate the time we need to complete stuff.

**[~3:00 BEAT: the first number in the video, and it's about their thesis]**

There's a well-known study on this from 1994, by the psychologists Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross.

They asked psychology students how long their senior thesis would take.

On average, the students said about 34 days, and on average, it took them about 55.

Only about 30% of them finished in the time they predicted.

**[ON SCREEN: predicted about 34 days, took about 55 days, about 30% finished on time (Buehler, Griffin and Ross, 1994)]**

Psychologists call this the planning fallacy, and it's very normal.

So always budget more time.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: TIP 3, TIME-BLOCK YOUR WEEK]**

Tip number three, if you have a full-time job and a full-time master's, is to time-block your week.

A good way to plan your week is to start with the things that cannot move.

Your classes cannot move, assignment deadlines cannot move, and work meetings cannot move.

So those go into the calendar first.

And then you give your study time its own block.

Instead of just keeping a long to-do list, you give every part of your day a job.

Cal Newport, a computer science professor who writes about focus, calls this time blocking.

**[ON SCREEN: a mock weekly calendar. Classes, deadlines and work meetings first, then study blocks filled in]**

For example, in my programme, we do classes in the evening a lot.

In fact, in my first and second semesters, I was in class pretty much every evening.

So in the morning, I'm doing a lot of work, and in the afternoon, I'm doing a lot of work.

And for studying, instead of just saying "oh, study this week", you create a block for it.

"Tuesday, 7 to 9 in the evening, read two papers for Thursday's class."

If you only study when you have free time, and you're working and studying full-time, you won't study, because you rarely have free time.

So create study blocks.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: TIP 4, GET AS MUCH AS YOU CAN IN CLASS]**

Another tip for working while studying is to get as much as you can out of class.

This was a very difficult one for me to keep up with.

Sometimes my class ends by 7:45 or 8pm, after a day of work.

So first of all, listen in class.

You're already stuck in class anyway, so don't procrastinate on whatever is being taught.

Get as much information and as much understanding as you can in class, so that whatever work you need to do after class is not as much.

Because if you don't pay attention in class, and I also do that sometimes, you always end up having to commit more time outside of class.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: TIP 5, ONE CALENDAR FOR WORK AND SCHOOL, AND A WEEKLY REVIEW]**

Tip number five is to use the same calendar for school and work.

Don't have your school calendar separate from your work calendar.

Your work deadlines, your school deadlines, your work commitments, your school commitments, any meeting you have at work, let it all live on the same calendar.

If they live in two different places, you only find out that they clash when it's already too late.

But once they're side by side, you get the full picture, and you can batch your work.

If you know that work has a big deadline in the second week of November, that's not the week to start the projects you were supposed to have done already.

It's like check and balance, right?

You shuffle responsibilities here and there.

**[~6:00 BEAT: her real December, a few weeks away]**

For example, right now in school, I'm doing a very big course, and December will be such a big deal for us, because it's a practical course where we have to work with real-life clients, real companies.

So I'm already thinking about December.

School will really take a lot of my blocks in December, so if I have a big thing at work as well, I should already start planning ahead, like right now.

Then, once a week, do a weekly review.

I do this every weekend, on Sunday evening.

Look at last week, and look at next week.

What is due at school next week?

What is happening at work?

For example, at work I might have a release, or I'm working on a feature, and at school it could be a big deadline.

Then you move your study blocks around those.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: TIP 6, YOUR VACATION DAYS, STUDY LEAVE, AND CARRYING PEOPLE ALONG]**

Tip number six for working while studying is about your vacation days, and I was telling this to my classmate the other day.

As a student, I know it sounds very unfair, but you need to take your mind off your vacation days.

You're going to use your vacation days for school responsibilities, and there's nothing you can do about it.

Do not hold on to your vacation days for vacation alone.

In a country like Estonia, which is a very nice place to be if you're a student, you also get study leave.

You can ask your employer for up to 30 calendar days of study leave a year, and for university studies, 20 of those days are paid at your average salary.

**[ON SCREEN: Estonia, study leave (Adult Education Act): up to 30 calendar days a year, with a letter from your school. For higher education, average salary for 20 of those days. Plus 15 extra days once, to graduate. As of October 2026]**

Another thing, if you intend to work full-time, is to carry people along.

If for some reason work is going to clash with something that will affect school, don't let it clash before you let school know.

You have to be proactive and stay ahead of the situation.

Tell your manager about things in advance.

"Oh yeah, I have this big thing at school, and I want to take study leave."

If there's a clash coming up with a deadline, write to your lecturer as early as possible.

And in many cases, if you're planning well and thinking ahead, you already know when there's going to be a clash.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: TIP 7, BE HONEST ABOUT WHAT KIND OF JOB YOU HAVE]**

**[~9:00 BEAT: her own privilege, said plainly]**

Tip number seven, the honest tip, is to be honest with yourself about what kind of job you have.

I have some privilege here.

With my work, I don't have to be at the office, which is a very big thing.

The commute, going back and forth, is also time lost in transit, at least for my kind of job.

So I can work around the clock.

I do not have a work-life balance, but I'm trying.

And especially when you're working and studying, you don't want to hold on to a picture of a perfect work-life balance.

You have to be willing to give things up.

But if your job has fixed hours, like a customer-facing job where you have to be there and you can't do it after those hours, that's a different situation.

Your study blocks and your school responsibilities have to work around that.

---

**[SEGMENT 11: TIP 8, MEAL PREP]**

Tip number eight, if you work and study full-time, is meal prepping.

Oh my goodness, meal prepping will save your life.

I also used to complain about the fact that I have to use a lot of hours to meal prep.

But I've come to realise that using four hours to meal prep is always going to be a good deal for me, if that guarantees me food for the rest of the week.

Sometimes four hours, sometimes five hours.

I've tried to tell myself, oh, four hours, five hours is too long.

But now, I don't negotiate with it.

Especially if you're like me and you like to eat African food, you need to meal prep, okay?

---

**[SEGMENT 12: TIP 9, PROTECT YOUR SLEEP]**

And the last tip for working while studying is to protect your sleep.

I'm telling you this, and I do not sleep well.

One of the issues I've been dealing with for the past month is migraines.

I've literally had to go to the hospital.

And I know that, whether I like it or not, it's somewhat related to work, sleep, and not getting off my laptop.

Sometimes I'm staring at this laptop for hours, into the night.

In 2019, researchers at MIT tracked the sleep of 88 students in a chemistry class, and the students who slept longer, slept better and slept more regularly tended to get better grades.

**[ON SCREEN: 88 students, sleep measures explained nearly 25% of the differences in grades (Okano and colleagues, MIT, 2019)]**

Mike and Matty have a video on studying with a full-time job when you're tired, and one of their rules is "bedtime, not alarm".

The way I take it, you decide when you go to bed and you protect that, instead of only setting an alarm for the morning.

But yeah, I hope these tips help you.

You can't really have a perfect work-life balance per se, because you're trying to do two big things together.

But it is what it is.

I think the major goal is to at least stay on top of things.

If you're working while studying, tell me in the comments which of these tips you already do.

### SHORT 5: Your vacation days will go to school (25-35 sec)

## 10. SUBSTACK / MEDIUM ESSAY

### Title:
> How I Work Full Time While in Grad School

### Subtitle:
> One year of a full-time developer job and a full-time master's in Estonia, and nine things that help.

### SEO Title:
> How to Work Full Time While in Grad School: 9 Tips From One Year In

### SEO Description:
> A master's student in Estonia on working full time while in grad school: what to check first, how full-time study is counted, and nine tips for planning your semester and your week.

---

I have a full-time job as a full-stack software developer here in Estonia, and I'm also doing a full-time master's degree here. I started my master's in September 2025, a month later I got my job, and this month makes it one year of doing both. That's not all I do either, because I make videos, I do church stuff, and I try to hang out with my friends and enjoy life a little bit. Working while studying, I swear, is the real ghetto. Once Monday starts for me, it keeps running until Friday, sometimes until Saturday.

Before any tips, you have to confirm if international students can work where you are studying. In Estonia, Study in Estonia lists working hours for international students as not limited, and the Police and Border Guard Board says you can work alongside your studies as long as it doesn't interfere with them and you keep studying full-time. Full-time study in Estonia means around 30 ECTS credits a semester. At my university, Tallinn University, they check it every two semesters, so after your first year you need at least 45 credits. If you fall below that, you're moved to part-time study, and that can result in your study permit being revoked. That's as of October 2026, and every school sets its own rules.

When I got the job, I set myself a very ambitious goal. I wanted to do work so well that work does not know that school exists, and do school so well that school does not know that work exists. So far so good, I think I've done that in a reasonable manner, even though there have been days of almost running mad. These are the things that help.

The first is to understand your credits at the start of every semester, before it gets busy. Most of my courses explain in the first class how they'll assess you, so on that same day you know the hours and the grading, and you can put every class, deadline and exam date in your calendar. If you're working full-time, use that to choose courses that won't overwhelm you. Then plan your whole degree. I had most of my non-thesis credits cleared early, so next semester is mostly my thesis. Give big things more time than you think, too. In a 1994 study, Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross asked psychology students how long their senior thesis would take. They said about 34 days on average, it took about 55, and only about 30% finished in the time they predicted.

For the week itself, start with what can't move, like classes, deadlines and work meetings, and then give study its own block. Cal Newport calls this time blocking. Most of my classes are in the evening, so my mornings and afternoons go to work, and study gets a block like "Tuesday, 7 to 9 in the evening, read two papers for Thursday's class." If you only study when you have free time, and you work and study full-time, you won't study, because you rarely have free time. It also helps to get as much as you can in class, since you're already there. When I don't pay attention in class, and I do that sometimes, I end up spending more time on it later.

Keep work and school on the same calendar. If they live in two places, you find the clash when it's already too late. Then every Sunday evening, look at last week and next week, at work and at school, and move your study blocks around releases and deadlines. Right now I'm already planning around December, because I have a big practical course with real clients that month.

Your vacation days will go to school too, and I know that sounds unfair. In Estonia you can also ask your employer for study leave, up to 30 calendar days a year, and for university studies 20 of those days are paid at your average salary. Carry people along. Tell your manager early, write to your lecturer as soon as you see a clash coming, and if you're planning ahead, you'll usually see the clash before it arrives. Be honest about what kind of job you have, too. I don't have to be at the office, which is a privilege, so I can work around the clock. I don't have the work-life balance I want, but I'm trying. A fixed-hours, customer-facing job is a different situation, and your study blocks have to go around it.

The last two are about your body. Meal prepping will save your life. I used to complain about spending four or five hours on it, but it guarantees me food for the rest of the week, and now I don't negotiate with it, especially because I like to eat African food. And protect your sleep. I'm saying that as someone who doesn't sleep well and spends too many nights on a laptop. In 2019, researchers at MIT tracked the sleep of 88 students in a chemistry class, and the ones who slept longer, better and more regularly tended to get better grades. You can't have a perfect work-life balance when you're doing two big things together. It is what it is, and the major goal is to stay on top of things.
