How to read a research paper, and tell if it's any good
The method I use comes from S. Keshav's two-page paper (2007)

I'm an HCI master's student, so I read a lot of papers for my thesis. This is the method I use.

01 / 13
Three parts of a paper do most of the work
01
The abstract
The whole paper squeezed into one paragraph: what they asked, did and found.
02
The method
Who took part, how many people and how they measured it.
03
The discussion and limitations
What the results mean, and what the study cannot tell you.

When you're writing your thesis, the limitations section is often where you find your research gap.

02 / 13
Read it in up to three passes
1
5-10 min
Do I even need this paper?
2
up to 1 hour
What did they find, and how?
3
4-5 hours
Only for the papers your thesis stands on

Keshav's times are for your first papers. Pass three drops to about an hour once you're experienced.

S. Keshav, “How to Read a Paper” (2007), section 2.1

Most of the time, that's enough to know if you need the paper at all.

04 / 13
Ten minutes can save you a whole evening

A lot of the time, after those five or ten minutes, you find the paper isn't relevant to what you're doing. That's where you stop.

After pass one, answer the five Cs
01
Category
What kind of paper is it? A study or a review?
02
Context
Which other papers is it related to?
03
Correctness
Do the assumptions seem valid?
04
Contributions
What does it add to the subject?
05

Is the paper well written?

In pass two, write in the margin
S. Keshav, “How to Read a Paper” (2007), section 2.2
key point
read ref later
check graph

Look properly at the figures and graphs, and mark the references that look relevant. I've started printing the important papers so I can mark them up.

07 / 13
In pass three, re-create the paper in your head
Who would you recruit?
What would you ask them?
Would you measure the same thing?
Where is your version different?

Every place where your version is different is either a weak spot in the paper or an idea for your own research.

08 / 13
Check 1 of 3
Does the number fit the size of the claim?
Kelly Caine, CHI 2016

Kelly Caine looked at every paper published at CHI 2014, a big HCI conference. 12 people in long interviews can tell you a lot about how people experience something, but not what percentage of all people do it.

Check 3 of 3
Who funded it?
Lundh et al., Cochrane review, 2017 (PubMed 28207928)

Funding doesn't exactly make a study wrong. But when the funder benefits from one particular answer, you need to pay a little bit more attention.
