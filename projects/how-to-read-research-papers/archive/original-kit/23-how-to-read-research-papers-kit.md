**How this script is built:** nine segments, in your order. Each one names its own topic in the first line or two, so a viewer who lands there (or a clip cut from it) understands it. About 1,750 spoken words, so roughly 11 to 12 minutes at your pace.

**[SEGMENT 1: OPEN]**

Research papers are not written to be read from start to finish like a novel.

So in this video, I will show you the parts of a paper and the order to read them in.

There's this method that I like.

It's called the three-pass method.

So we're going to go over how to tell if a paper is any good, and how to keep notes so you don't lose what you read.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: THE PARTS OF A RESEARCH PAPER]**

Most research papers are built from the same parts, and you need to know what each part is for, so you stop just reading it like one block of text.

In a lot of fields, the main body follows a pattern called IMRaD **[say: IM-rad]**.

It stands for Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion.

And around those, you usually see a title, an abstract, a related work or background section, a limitations section, a conclusion and the references.

**[ON SCREEN: title, abstract, introduction, related work, methods, results, discussion, limitations, conclusion, references]**

The names change a little from field to field.

In HCI, the field where I'm doing my master's, the results section is often called "findings".

**[~1:00 BEAT: the full list is on screen, and the promise lands that only three parts do most of the work]**

You don't need to read every part closely.

Three parts do most of the work for you.

The first one is the abstract.

The abstract is a short paragraph right under the title.

It's the whole paper squeezed into one paragraph. What they asked, what they did and what they found.

The second one is the method.

The method is where the authors tell you exactly what they did.

Who took part, how many people, what they measured and how they measured it.

This is the part that tells you whether you can trust everything else in the paper.

And the third one is the discussion, together with the limitations.

The discussion is where the authors explain what their results mean.

And the limitations section is where they say what their study can't tell you.

And I think the limitations section is one of the most useful parts of a paper when you're writing a thesis, for example, because it shows you what is still open, and you can often find your research gap there.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: KESHAV'S THREE-PASS METHOD]**

One of the best-known methods for reading research papers comes from a two-page paper called "How to Read a Paper".

It was written by S. Keshav **[say: KAY-shuv]**.

**[ON SCREEN: S. Keshav, "How to Read a Paper", ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 2007]**

In his words, "you should read the paper in up to three passes."

Each pass has a different job.

The first pass tells you what the paper is about, and whether you need it at all.

The second pass tells you what the paper found, and what evidence it has.

And the third pass is for the papers you need to understand completely.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: PASS ONE, THE ORDER TO READ A PAPER IN]**

**[~3:00 BEAT: the first thing they can use on the next paper they open]**

Pass one of Keshav's three-pass method takes about five to ten minutes, and it also gives you the order to read a paper in.

Keshav lists four steps.

First, carefully read the title, the abstract and the introduction.

Second, read the section and subsection headings, but ignore everything else.

Third, read the conclusion.

Fourth, glance over the references, mentally ticking off the ones you've already read.

**[ON SCREEN: Pass 1 (5 to 10 minutes): title, abstract, introduction, headings only, conclusion, glance at the references]**

If you watch my video on how to read a book, this is the same idea of reading the structure first, kind of applied to research papers.

At the end of the first pass, Keshav says you should be able to answer five questions, and he calls them the five Cs.

**[ON SCREEN: the five Cs: Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity]**

Category is what kind of paper this is.

Is it a study? Is it a new system? Is it a review of other papers?

Context is which other papers this paper is related to.

Correctness is whether the assumptions seem valid.

Contributions is what the paper has that was not there before.

And clarity is whether the paper is well written.

A lot of the time, after those five or ten minutes, you will find the paper isn't really relevant to what you're doing.

At this point, Keshav says you can stop, and you've just saved yourself an evening.

So the first pass is usually enough for papers that are not in your research area, but might matter to you one day.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: PASS TWO, READING A PAPER WITH GREATER CARE]**

The second pass of Keshav's method for reading research papers takes up to an hour.

After you've gone through the first pass in five to ten minutes, and you've decided that this research paper is relevant to you, then you move on to reading the paper with greater care, but you ignore the details.

So you can ignore something like a long table of statistics, and you can come back to that later.

While you read during the second pass, Keshav suggests writing down the key points, or making comments in the margins.

And this is the pass where you look properly at the figures.

In his words, "look carefully at the figures, diagrams and other illustrations in the paper," and "pay special attention to graphs."

Also on the second pass, you mark the references you haven't read that look relevant to you.

That's how one good research paper can lead you to the next one.

**[OPTIONAL: "I made a whole video on how to find research papers, so I'll link it here."]**

By the time you're at the end of the second pass, you should be able to explain the main point of the paper, and the evidence for it, to someone else.

Now, if you still don't understand the research paper at this point, you can put it aside, come back later after reading some background, or you can move to the third pass.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: PASS THREE, UNDERSTANDING A PAPER COMPLETELY]**

The third pass of Keshav's method is for the papers you need to understand completely, like the ones your thesis is built on.

For example, for a paper that my thesis is built on, I would need to go through this third pass.

Keshav writes, "The key to the third pass is to attempt to virtually re-implement the paper."

That means you make the same assumptions as the authors of the paper, you rebuild the work in your head, and then you compare your version with theirs.

**[~6:00 BEAT: the "run the study yourself" questions, the most hands-on idea in the video]**

For a study in HCI, that looks like imagining you're running the study yourself.

Who would you recruit? What would you ask them? Would you measure the same thing?

Then you compare your answers with what the authors did.

Every place where your version is different from the version in the paper is either a weak spot in the paper, or an idea for your own research work.

And this third pass takes time.

According to Keshav, who wrote about this method, it can take "about four or five hours for beginners, and about an hour for an experienced reader."

I would say if you're a student, you should be on the higher side. Just budget like three, four, five hours.

But of course, you don't deep dive into a paper like this if you haven't gone through the first and second pass to know it's actually relevant to your work.

You don't want to commit four or five hours to a paper that is not going to help your thesis.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: HOW TO READ A RESEARCH PAPER CRITICALLY]**

Reading a research paper critically means checking whether you should believe it, because a paper being published doesn't make it the right paper to reference or build your own research on.

So there are three things I would check on every research paper.

**[ON SCREEN: sample size, method, who funded it]**

The first is the sample size, which is how many people or things they studied.

Bigger isn't automatically better. Keep this in mind.

In HCI, for example, small studies are common.

Kelly Caine looked at every paper published at CHI **[say: "kai", like the Greek letter]** in 2014, and found that the most common sample size was 12.

**[ON SCREEN: CHI is a major yearly conference in HCI. Kelly Caine, "Local Standards for Sample Size at CHI", CHI 2016]**

So twelve people in long interviews can tell you a lot about how people experience something.

But twelve people can't tell you what percentage of all students do something.

So the question is whether the number fits the size of the claim.

The second thing to look out for is the method, which is how they got their answer, and whether it matches the question.

Say a paper claims that switching between apps stresses people out.

Did they ask people in a survey? Did they watch people work? Did they measure something like time or heart rate?

Was it in a lab? Was it at these people's offices and jobs?

Who were the participants?

If everyone in the study was a university student, I'd be very careful applying the results of that study to, say, nurses on the night shift.

**[~9:00 BEAT, OPTIONAL RE-ENGAGEMENT: "And the third one is easy to miss, because it's usually near the end of the paper."]**

And the third is who funded it.

Look for a funding section, maybe the acknowledgements, or a conflict of interest statement.

Funding doesn't exactly make the study wrong.

But in 2017, a Cochrane review led by Andreas Lundh looked at drug and medical device studies, and found that the studies sponsored by the company that made the product were more likely to report results and conclusions that favoured that product.

**[ON SCREEN: Lundh et al., "Industry sponsorship and research outcome", Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2017]**

So when the funder benefits from one particular answer, you need to pay a little bit more attention.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: PRINT OR DIGITAL, AND HOW TO KEEP NOTES ON RESEARCH PAPERS]**

How do you keep notes on your research papers?

Keeping notes on your research papers matters, but first of all, before that, I would say, how do you even read?

Do you read your research papers digitally?

One method that I have started to adopt myself is printing them out.

For example, I'm writing my thesis, and there are a few papers that are so major that I'm building my own thesis on them.

You might want to print papers like that out and have them at hand, or you can read them digitally, whichever works for you.

But keeping notes on research papers matters, because if you don't write anything down, you will not remember a lot of it.

One tool that was recommended in my school is Zotero.

**[ON SCREEN: a PDF open in Zotero, with a highlight and a note]**

Yeah, and it's really good.

It's free and open source, and you can open your PDF inside Zotero, highlight it and write notes in it.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: WHEN A RESEARCH PAPER STILL DOESN'T MAKE SENSE]**

If you think a research paper is relevant to your research topic, but it still doesn't make sense after following all these steps, then there are a few things you can do.

You can look up the terms that you don't know first of all, because if there's a major word in a section, it can make the whole section feel impossible to understand.

Or you can find a survey paper on the same topic, and read it before you go back to the research paper.

A survey paper is a paper that reviews the research on a topic.

Also, you can use AI to explain one dense paragraph at a time. I do this a lot.

Just check what it tells you against the paper itself, because AI can get things wrong.

### SHORT 5: Is this paper any good? (30-40 sec)

Three things I'd check before I believe a research paper.

First, the sample size. Kelly Caine looked at every paper at CHI 2014, the HCI conference, and the most common sample size was 12. Twelve people in long interviews can tell you a lot about an experience, but they can't tell you what percentage of all students do something.

Second, the method. Did they ask people, watch people, or measure something? And who were the participants?

Third, who funded it. Funding doesn't make a study wrong, but when the funder benefits from one answer, pay a little more attention.

### SHORT 6: Run the study in your head (25-35 sec)

For the papers your thesis is built on, S. Keshav says the key is to "virtually re-implement the paper."

In HCI, that means imagining you're running the study yourself.

Who would you recruit? What would you ask them? Would you measure the same thing?

Then compare your answers with what the authors did.

Every difference is either a weak spot in the paper, or an idea for your own research.

It can take a beginner four or five hours, so only do it for the papers that really matter to your work.

### SHORT 7: When a paper still doesn't make sense (20-30 sec)

If a research paper still doesn't make sense, there are a few things you can do.

Look up the words you don't know first, because one major word can make a whole section feel impossible.

Or find a survey paper on the same topic, which is a paper that reviews the research, and read that first.

You can also ask AI to explain one dense paragraph at a time. I do this a lot.

Just check what it tells you against the paper itself, because AI can get things wrong.

Research papers are not written to be read from start to finish like a novel. If you start at the first word and keep going, you usually get lost somewhere in the middle, because a paper is built from parts that each do a different job, and the order you read them in matters.

In a lot of fields, the main body follows a pattern called IMRaD, which stands for Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion. Around it you usually see a title, an abstract, a related work or background section, a limitations section, a conclusion and the references. The names change a little from field to field, and in HCI, where I'm doing my master's, the results section is often called findings. Three of these parts do most of the work for a reader. The abstract is the whole paper squeezed into one paragraph. The method tells you who took part and what was measured, which is how you decide whether to trust the rest. The limitations section tells you what the study can't tell you, and when you're writing a thesis, I think it's one of the most useful parts, because that's often where you find your research gap.

One of the best-known methods for reading papers comes from S. Keshav's two-page paper "How to Read a Paper", published in 2007 in ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review. In his words, "you should read the paper in up to three passes." The first pass takes about five to ten minutes. You carefully read the title, abstract and introduction, then the section and subsection headings while ignoring everything else, then the conclusions, and then you glance over the references. At the end of it, you should be able to answer what he calls the five Cs, which are the paper's category, its context, whether its assumptions look correct, its contributions and its clarity. A lot of the time, that first pass tells you the paper isn't relevant, and you can stop. If you've watched my video on how to read a book, it's the same idea of reading the structure first, applied to papers.

The second pass takes up to an hour. You read with greater care but ignore the heavy details, like a long table of statistics you can come back to later. You write down the key points, you "look carefully at the figures, diagrams and other illustrations in the paper," and you mark the references you haven't read yet, which is how one good paper leads you to the next. By the end, you should be able to explain the main point and its evidence to someone else. The third pass is for the papers you need to understand completely, like the ones a thesis is built on. Keshav writes that "the key to the third pass is to attempt to virtually re-implement the paper." In HCI, I read that as imagining you're running the study yourself, deciding who you would recruit and what you would measure, and then comparing your choices with the authors'. He says it can take "about four or five hours for beginners," so as a student I'd plan for the higher side, and only after the first two passes have told me the paper is worth it.

Reading critically means checking whether you should believe a paper, because being published doesn't make it the right paper to build your own research on. I check three things. The first is the sample size, and bigger isn't automatically better. Kelly Caine looked at every paper published at CHI in 2014 and found that the most common sample size was twelve, which is plenty for long interviews about how people experience something and not enough to say what percentage of all students do something. The second is the method, including who the participants were. If everyone in a study was a university student, I'd be careful applying it to nurses on a night shift. The third is who funded it. A 2017 Cochrane review led by Andreas Lundh found that drug and medical device studies sponsored by the manufacturer were more likely to report results and conclusions favouring that product. Funding doesn't make a study wrong, but when the funder benefits from one answer, it deserves more attention.

How you read matters too. I've started printing the few papers my thesis is built on and keeping them at hand, and I read everything else digitally. Whichever you choose, keep notes, because you won't remember much of what you don't write down. Zotero was recommended at my school. It's free and open source, and you can open a PDF inside it, highlight it and write notes in it.

And if a paper still doesn't make sense after all of this, look up the terms you don't know first, because one major word can make a whole section feel impossible. You can also read a survey paper on the same topic, which is a paper that reviews the research, before you go back. I also use AI to explain one dense paragraph at a time, and I do that a lot, but I check what it tells me against the paper itself, because it can get things wrong.
