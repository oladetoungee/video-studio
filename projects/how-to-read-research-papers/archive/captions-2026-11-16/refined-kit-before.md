### 6f. Essay (from her kit, in full)
Research papers are not written to be read from start to finish like a novel. If you start at the first word and keep going, you usually get lost somewhere in the middle, because a paper is built from parts that each do a different job, and the order you read them in matters.

In a lot of fields, the main body follows a pattern called IMRaD, which stands for Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion. Around it you usually see a title, an abstract, a related work or background section, a limitations section, a conclusion and the references. The names change a little from field to field, and in HCI, where I'm doing my master's, the results section is often called findings. Three of these parts do most of the work for a reader. The abstract is the whole paper squeezed into one paragraph. The method tells you who took part and what was measured, which is how you decide whether to trust the rest. The limitations section tells you what the study can't tell you, and when you're writing a thesis, I think it's one of the most useful parts, because that's often where you find your research gap.

One of the best-known methods for reading papers comes from S. Keshav's two-page paper "How to Read a Paper", published in 2007 in ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review. In his words, "you should read the paper in up to three passes." The first pass takes about five to ten minutes. You carefully read the title, abstract and introduction, then the section and subsection headings while ignoring everything else, then the conclusions, and then you glance over the references. At the end of it, you should be able to answer what he calls the five Cs, which are the paper's category, its context, whether its assumptions look correct, its contributions and its clarity. A lot of the time, that first pass tells you the paper isn't relevant, and you can stop. If you've watched my video on how to read a book, it's the same idea of reading the structure first, applied to papers.

The second pass takes up to an hour. You read with greater care but ignore the heavy details, like a long table of statistics you can come back to later. You write down the key points, you "look carefully at the figures, diagrams and other illustrations in the paper," and you mark the references you haven't read yet, which is how one good paper leads you to the next. By the end, you should be able to explain the main point and its evidence to someone else. The third pass is for the papers you need to understand completely, like the ones a thesis is built on. Keshav writes that "the key to the third pass is to attempt to virtually re-implement the paper." In HCI, I read that as imagining you're running the study yourself, deciding who you would recruit and what you would measure, and then comparing your choices with the authors'. He says it can take "about four or five hours for beginners," so as a student I'd plan for the higher side, and only after the first two passes have told me the paper is worth it.

Reading critically means checking whether you should believe a paper, because being published doesn't make it the right paper to build your own research on. I check three things. The first is the sample size, and bigger isn't automatically better. Kelly Caine looked at every paper published at CHI in 2014 and found that the most common sample size was twelve, which is plenty for long interviews about how people experience something and not enough to say what percentage of all students do something. The second is the method, including who the participants were. If everyone in a study was a university student, I'd be careful applying it to nurses on a night shift. The third is who funded it. A 2017 Cochrane review led by Andreas Lundh found that drug and medical device studies sponsored by the manufacturer were more likely to report results and conclusions favouring that product. Funding doesn't make a study wrong, but when the funder benefits from one answer, it deserves more attention.

How you read matters too. I've started printing the few papers my thesis is built on and keeping them at hand, and I read everything else digitally. Whichever you choose, keep notes, because you won't remember much of what you don't write down. Zotero was recommended at my school. It's free and open source, and you can open a PDF inside it, highlight it and write notes in it.

And if a paper still doesn't make sense after all of this, look up the terms you don't know first, because one major word can make a whole section feel impossible. You can also read a survey paper on the same topic, which is a paper that reviews the research, before you go back. I also use AI to explain one dense paragraph at a time, and I do that a lot, but I check what it tells me against the paper itself, because it can get things wrong.
