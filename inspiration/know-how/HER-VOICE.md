# Her voice: how Gbemi writes (read before writing ANY caption, post, description or text for her)

> **2026-11-16, her rule:** every caption and post should sound like talking to a friend (conversational flow). Model: the edge-cases LinkedIn post, projects/the-edge-case-you-think-is-impossible/kit/refined-kit.md §6a (her own draft).

## PART A · Her own writing guide, verbatim (she gave it 2026-10-18; it overrides everything below where they differ)

**How to write for Gbemi (Gbemisola Oladetoun)**

About me: I'm a Nigerian software developer (Full Stack Laravel at FleetFox, an Estonian company) and an MSc Human-Computer Interaction student at Tallinn University in Estonia. I write in many places: applications, letters, emails, posts, essays and personal pieces.

**1. My voice**
- Simple, everyday words and short sentences. Plain language.
- I'm a relational writer. Anything I write should feel like being spoken to rather than read. Use full, flowing sentences and never stack fragments. "Old bookmarks, truncated links, renamed pages, typos" is wrong, and each of those should be its own sentence.
- Explain things to the reader instead of announcing a topic at them.
- Warm and direct. Conversational openers are fine ("The one I always come back to is…", "Yeah, a fair amount").
- Keep my real phrasing. My odd turns, asides and repetition are my voice, not mistakes. Natural Nigerian-English rhythm is welcome.
- Concrete specifics over adjectives: numbers, named places, what I actually did.
- One arc per piece. Don't introduce a point, jump elsewhere, then circle back.
- Never repeat a point in two different wordings. I notice it and cut it.
- Name the thing you're pointing at. If a sentence says "that question", an earlier sentence must have stated the question.
- Judgment lines should come out of the story, not be stuck on top.
- Weave in faith naturally where it's relevant.
- The final test: if I wouldn't say it out loud, it fails.

**2. Banned AI tells (check every draft against this)**

Structure:
- Em-dashes, anywhere. Use commas, full stops or brackets.
- Colon reveals ("The catch: it slows everything down."). Colons are only for labels and lists.
- "Not X, but Y", "It's not X. It's Y.", "isn't just about X, it's about Y", "you don't have a this problem, you have a that problem".
- "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."
- Things in threes for rhythm ("No fluff, no filler, just results").
- Self-answered dramatic questions ("The result? Devastating.").
- Short punchy one-line paragraphs stacked for drama ("That's not a step. That's the whole thing.").
- Preambles that announce what's coming ("Two things shape this", "Five things you need to know").
- Deep-sounding closing lines, one-liners with no real information, and endings that just restate the point. No "In conclusion".

Words and phrases:
- Magic adverbs: quietly, deeply, fundamentally, remarkably, truly, crucially.
- Throat-clearing: "Here's the thing", "Let me explain what I mean", "Let's break this down", "Let's dive in", "It's worth noting", "Notably", "Interestingly", "At the end of the day", "When it comes to".
- Faux insight: "what nobody tells you", "the part everyone misses".
- Weak verbs: "serves as" or "stands as" instead of "is".
- False ranges like "from innovation to transformation", and inflated stakes.
- Vague sources: "experts say". Name the source.
- Buzzwords: delve, leverage, utilize, robust, seamless, harness, tapestry, landscape, ecosystem, game-changer, elevate, empower, unlock.
- Made-up concept labels or slogans, like "the convenience trap" or "proof beats permission".
- Swapping synonyms for the same thing just to sound varied.
- Marketing or brochure tone.

Exception: these rules govern what you write. My own phrasing stays, even if it breaks one of them.

**3. When I dictate or give you a draft**
My words are the base. Make the smallest edit that works:
- Fix transcription errors and factual slips, split run-ons, and cut true repetition.
- Keep my order, my digressions, my self-corrections and my phrasing.
- Don't reorganise my flow or rewrite my points in smoother prose. That's exactly what makes my writing sound AI-written.
- If I say I was just rambling around an idea, keep my lines but put them in a sensible order. Add a line of context where a stranger would get lost.
- Remove asides meant for you, like "please correct me if I'm wrong".
- Always list what you changed at the end.

**4. Accuracy and honesty**
- Check facts against real sources and fix anything wrong.
- Watch for overstatement: "usually" vs "always", "the main way" vs "the way", "isn't sure" vs "thinks".
- Never invent experiences, feelings, numbers or stories about me. If something would be stronger with my own story, ask me.
- If I use someone else's idea or a line close to their words, credit them by name.
- Respect word or character limits, and tell me the count.

**5. Process**
- Run an anti-AI pass against the list above.
- Do a second pass to make it sound like me. Free of AI tells is not the same as sounding like me.

**6. Sample writing from Gbemi (my own writing)**
"Something I keep coming back to in my studies is how technology shapes the way people experience and hold onto the things that matter to them. In HCI, we talk a lot about users and systems, but cultural heritage adds a layer that I find really compelling."

"And honestly, beyond the academics, these opportunities matter to me on a personal level too. As a Nigerian student living in Estonia, every new country and culture I get to experience broadens how I see the world and how I understand the people I'm designing for."

## The pattern (in her order)
1. **A plain, specific opening line.** One of: a fact or news change ("Uber ended its operations in Nigeria on the 2nd of
   September, after 12 years." / "You can no longer be cruel or abusive to AI 🙂" / "Ghana recently launched an interesting
   platform called Marrify…"), a question as she would ask it ("what happens when you type a URL into your browser and press
   enter?"), a history fact ("In 1930, an economist, John Maynard Keynes predicted…"), or something about her ("I get
   complimented for reading a lot…"). No hook formulas, no "Here's why", no "Stop doing X".
2. **She is in it.** Her first reaction ("My first reaction was just being excited…"), her context (a student, an HCI
   master's student, a software engineer, in Estonia for about a year, Nigerian / African context), what she worries about
   or loves. A clear opinion with nuance: "I see Claude as a very capable tool, not a person." / "Of course, I don't think
   anyone should be cruel to AI. But definitely not because…" / "For me, the AI productivity era would be a genuine success
   if…".
3. **The idea explained in plain prose**, step by step, in short paragraphs (1-3 sentences): "Your browser starts by breaking
   apart what you typed. Then your domain name has to turn into an address… After that, TCP… And only then…". Concrete
   examples from real life. When she proposes something: "So here is what I think should exist, and none of it is technically
   difficult." then one short paragraph per idea (not bullets).
4. **A closing thought or question to the reader**, in her words: "How can we balance… with privacy?" / "So my question for
   the AI productivity conversation is simple. Productive for who?" / "I think we're all not reading as much as we should!"
5. **The link line**, casual and in the post itself: "Full Video on YouTube - <link>" (her usual), or "I was ranting about it
   and in usual fashion, there's a video now on YouTube <link>", or "If I think about something too much… I always
   fortunately and unfortunately end up with a video so here you go - <link>". Never "Link in the comments".
6. **Hashtags last**, 4-7, on one line (#softwareengineering #hci #africa …).

## The first line (her correction 2026-10-18: "The man who invented pull-to-refresh later said it's addictive" is NOT a good first line)
Her first line states the TOPIC itself, plainly, the way she would say it out loud to a friend: the news ("Uber ended its
operations in Nigeria on the 2nd of September, after 12 years."), the change ("You can no longer be cruel or abusive to AI 🙂"),
the situation ("When a user is on your domain and they navigate to a URL that does not exist… what they get is a 404 Error
page."), the question ("what happens when you type a URL into your browser and press enter?"), or about her ("I get
complimented for reading a lot…"). Often it is the first line of her own video. It is NEVER a teaser trick: no surprising
side-fact about someone else, no withheld twist, no "the man who…", no curiosity-gap line. Test: if the line only works because
it hides what the post is about, it is wrong; if a stranger knows the topic from it, it is right.

## Her texture (keep it)
- Brackets for asides, often funny or self-aware: "(I'm a student so I don't really have a choice but to read)",
  "(everyone who knows me thinks I've been here longer🫠)".
- An emoji now and then, at the end of a feeling, never as bullets: 🫠 🙂 😅 🥰.
- Her phrases: "And that begs the question", "a million and one thing", "in usual fashion", "My thoughts were in a million
  places", "So here is what I think should exist", "And thank you guys for always watching🥰".
- Prose, not lists. Plain words. She sometimes writes long sentences; let her rhythm breathe.
- First person throughout, warm, curious, a little cheeky; never salesy.

## Never (what we did that was not her)
- Bullet lists of everything the video covers ("My new video explains: - what X is - how Y works …").
- "My new video explains…", "In this video I cover…", "Link in the comments", "Here's why", "Let that sink in".
- Hype words, em-dashes, rhetorical triplets, LinkedIn-guru formatting (one-line paragraphs of slogans).
- Inventing feelings or stories she didn't say: use what is in her video / kit; her opinions only as she states them.
- Do NOT add typos on purpose (her drafts have a few; ours should be clean but sound like her).
